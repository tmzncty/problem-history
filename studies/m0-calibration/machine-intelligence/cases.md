# Case MI-01 — Turing, 1950

## Source identity

Alan M. Turing, “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” *Mind* 59, no. 236 (October 1950): 433–460.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433
- Publisher text: https://academic.oup.com/mind/article/LIX/236/433/986238
- Archival offprint: Turing Digital Archive, AMT/B/19.
- Historical date: 1950.
- Version note: use the published *Mind* article for wording and page locators.

## Selected primary evidence

### p. 433, section 1

Turing opens with the short question:

> “Can machines think?”

He then says the ordinary meanings of “machine” and “think” would lead toward an unsuitable definitional exercise, introduces the imitation game, and states that the game questions **replace** the original question.

### later in the article, section 6

Turing returns to the original wording and judges it too imprecise for the work he wants to do, while still discussing objections to machine intelligence and making a prediction about future language and educated opinion.

## Local context supplied to the Agent

- The article is not merely a list of computer capabilities; it contains an explicit methodological move from one question-form to another.
- Turing discusses objections, digital computers, learning machines, and a future performance prediction.
- Do not assume that the later label “Turing Test” settles what Turing himself regarded as the historical problem.

## Calibration questions

- Is “Can machines think?” the final operative problem of the article, an initial formulation, or both in different senses?
- What exactly can be called an actor-explicit reformulation?
- Which modern formulations — “machine consciousness,” “AGI,” “LLM sentience,” etc. — are not licensed by this source packet?
- If another text later uses “intelligence,” what evidence beyond vocabulary would be needed to claim continuity with this episode?

# Case MI-02 — Dartmouth proposal, 1955

## Source identity

John McCarthy, Marvin L. Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude E. Shannon, “A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, August 31, 1955.”

- Open-access reproduction: *AI Magazine* 27, no. 4 (2006).
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1609/aimag.v27i4.1904
- Landing page: https://ojs.aaai.org/aimagazine/index.php/aimagazine/article/view/1904
- Original historical document date: 31 August 1955.
- Mediation note: the linked item is a later reproduction of the proposal, not the physical 1955 typescript. The reproduction notes surviving copies at Dartmouth College and Stanford University.

## Selected primary evidence

On the proposal's opening page, the authors propose a summer study of “artificial intelligence” and state a conjecture that aspects of learning or intelligence could be described precisely enough for machine simulation.

They then identify research tasks including machine language use, abstractions and concepts, problem solving, and self-improvement. Subsequent sections enumerate topics such as neural nets, complexity, abstraction, and creativity/randomness.

## Local context supplied to the Agent

- This is a proposal for a collaborative research project, not a retrospective history of AI.
- The document makes a conjecture and proposes research tasks; it does not report that all of those tasks have already been solved.
- The phrase “artificial intelligence” is part of the actors' own 1955 wording.
- The existence of earlier work on learning machines, cybernetics, automata, computation, or intelligence is not by itself evidence that those actors were already asking the same problem in the same form.

## Calibration questions

- What is the proposal actually trying to make researchable?
- Which parts are explicit actor formulations, and which would require reconstruction?
- Are the relevant stakes philosophical, engineering, programme-building, institutional, or mixed?
- Does the shared machine/intelligence topic establish continuity with MI-01? Give evidence both ways.

# Case MI-03 — Lighthill report, 1972/1973

## Source identity

James Lighthill, “Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey,” report dated July 1972, subsequently published in Science Research Council, *Artificial Intelligence: A Paper Symposium* (London, 1973), pp. 1–21.

- University of Edinburgh AIAI-hosted scan: https://www.aiai.ed.ac.uk/events/lighthill1973/lighthill.pdf
- Historical composition/submission: 1972.
- Publication context: Science Research Council symposium volume, 1973.
- Secondary control: Jon Agar, “What is science for? The Lighthill report on artificial intelligence reinterpreted,” *British Journal for the History of Science* 53, no. 3 (2020): 289–310, DOI https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007087420000230.

## Selected primary evidence

### report introduction, printed p. 1

Lighthill explains that the Science Research Council was receiving increasing requests to support AI research and commissioned a general view to aid policy and decisions.

### discussion of the field, around printed pp. 7–16

He questions the success and coherence of broad, general-purpose AI research, discusses “combinatorial explosion,” distinguishes different motivations for work grouped under AI, and contrasts specialised successes with disappointing general-purpose ambitions.

One short diagnostic phrase is his concern with the existence of a “continuous, coherent field of AI research.”

## Local context supplied to the Agent

- The report is an externally commissioned assessment situated inside research-policy and funding decisions.
- It evaluates a heterogeneous research field and its claims; it is not simply a laboratory paper presenting one algorithm.
- Later historians frequently discuss the report in relation to an “AI winter.” Treat that phrase as a later historiographic description unless actor evidence shows otherwise.
- Agar's 2020 reinterpretation argues that Lighthill's views about the relationship between research and practical problem solving matter for understanding the report; do not reduce it to a timeless claim that “AI is impossible.”

## Calibration questions

- What problem is the report itself commissioned to address?
- What distinct technical/research problems does Lighthill identify inside the field he surveys?
- Does criticising a field's coherence mean denying the possibility of machine intelligence in every sense?
- What relation to MI-02 is supportable: continuation, transformed successor, displacement, or undetermined?

# Case MI-04 — Searle, 1980

## Source identity

John R. Searle, “Minds, Brains, and Programs,” *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 3, no. 3 (1980): 417–457.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00005756
- Publisher record: Cambridge University Press, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*.
- Historical date: 1980.

## Selected primary evidence

The article's abstract states two propositions. The second is especially important for calibration:

> “Instantiating a computer program is never by itself a sufficient condition of intentionality.”

Searle's main argument is directed at that claim and uses the now-famous room thought experiment to separate formal program execution from the intentionality/understanding he thinks is at issue.

## Local context supplied to the Agent

- The target is not merely whether a machine can produce competent observable outputs.
- The paper is a philosophical intervention into claims about what computer programs are sufficient to explain or constitute.
- Later summaries often package the debate under “Can computers think?” or “the Chinese Room.” Such summaries can be useful researcher labels but are not automatically the paper's actor-explicit formulation.

## Calibration questions

- What is Searle explicitly trying to establish or deny?
- Which presuppositions about intentionality, programs, and brain causation are explicit in the abstract?
- Is MI-04 a revival of MI-01, a rejection of MI-01's operative criterion, an analogy-only relation, or something else? Explain why the packet may or may not settle the relation.
- What would be anachronistic to import from present LLM debates?

# Case MI-05 — OpenAI GPT-4, 2023

## Source identity

OpenAI, “GPT-4,” research release, 14 March 2023; associated *GPT-4 Technical Report* submitted to arXiv 15 March 2023.

- Official actor page: https://openai.com/index/gpt-4-research/
- Technical report: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774
- Historical date for the release page: 14 March 2023.
- Source-function note: this is strong evidence for OpenAI's framing of GPT-4, not automatically for every researcher, company, regulator, or user in 2023.

## Selected primary evidence

The release reports benchmark capabilities and alignment work, and says:

> “GPT-4 is not fully reliable.”

It discusses hallucinations/reasoning errors, safety and alignment, expert adversarial testing, monitoring, model behavior, evaluation, predictable scaling, and preparation for future capabilities.

The page also frames GPT-4 as a deployed multimodal model whose capabilities and shortcomings need measurement and mitigation.

## Local context supplied to the Agent

- The document evaluates and deploys a particular model; it is not a philosophical paper on intentionality.
- It contains comparisons with human benchmark performance but also explicit limitations and risk-management concerns.
- Do not infer from human-level performance on selected benchmarks that the actor has asserted human-level understanding, consciousness, sentience, or intentionality.
- Do not assume that the modern prominence of LLMs makes the earlier four cases stages in a prehistory whose telos is GPT-4.

## Calibration questions

- Which problems are actor-explicit on this page: capability, reliability, alignment, safety, scaling, deployment, or something else?
- Is “Can GPT-4 think?” present as an actor question here, or is that a researcher/public analytic overlay?
- Which dimensions show continuity with older machine-intelligence debates, and which show a changed target/stakes/answer space?
- What evidence would be required before relating this episode to MI-04's intentionality problem rather than merely noting topical proximity?
