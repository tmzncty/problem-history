# 1961 OAM: administrative birth-date source note

Status: **M2 source-critical follow-up / institutional-genealogy note, not a frozen Problem Episode**

Pilot: technology–labour

Purpose: tighten the unresolved April/October 1961 establishment chronology for the U.S. Department of Labor's **Office of Automation and Manpower (OAM)** without converting an administrative date into a historical `problem birth` date.

This note supplements:

- `studies/technology-labour/1961-automation-manpower-operationalization.md`;
- `studies/technology-labour/1961-retraining-to-manpower-legislative-transformation.md`.

The main new result is narrow but important: the April instrument can now be identified more precisely as **General Order No. 110, 20 April 1961**. At the same time, the National Archives still gives a second administrative-history establishment date — **Secretary's Order 5-61, 25 October 1961** — and then records a direct successor, the **Office of Manpower, Automation, and Training (OMAT)**, established by **Secretary's Order 17-62, 15 June 1962**.

The April/October relation remains **undetermined** until the underlying orders are compared.

---

## 1. Why this is a Problem-History issue rather than clerical cleanup

The previous packet already found two apparently authoritative dates for the same office. It therefore refused to normalize them into one neat institutional birthday.

That caution was correct.

The new source recovery improves the evidence enough to distinguish at least three different claims:

```text
A. when an office was publicly / operationally created;
B. when a later administrative-history lineage says it was "established";
C. when a historical problem became askable or institutionally important.
```

These are not interchangeable.

Even if A and B are eventually reconciled, neither date by itself proves C.

The OAM case therefore supplies a concrete warning:

```text
administrative establishment date
!=
problem emergence date
```

and, provisionally:

```text
one office name
+
two authoritative establishment records
!=
one recoverable institutional event without further source work
```

## 2. 20 April 1961 can now be tied to General Order No. 110

### Contemporary / official Department of Labor evidence

A GovInfo-preserved U.S. Department of Labor FY1961 report gives a more precise formulation than the previous packet had available. Its searchable record states that the **Office of Automation and Manpower was created by General Order No. 110 on 20 April 1961**.

GovInfo record / PDF entry:

<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-L-4d879b5f25e7274e409c3f421cb68050/pdf/GOVPUB-L-4d879b5f25e7274e409c3f421cb68050.pdf>

The same official record also identifies Deputy Assistant Secretary **Seymour Wolfbein** as the Office's director.

### Evidence function

This materially strengthens the April chronology.

Previously the repository could say:

```text
20 Apr 1961
= strong evidence for operational/public creation
under an identified but not yet numbered General Order
```

It can now say more precisely:

```text
20 Apr 1961
= Department of Labor creation by General Order No. 110
```

### Evidence strength

**High for the Department's April administrative chronology.**

Boundary: the underlying signed/order text of General Order No. 110 has still not been recovered page-for-page. The GovInfo-preserved Department report is official evidence about the order; it is not a substitute for the order if later argument turns on the order's exact wording, delegation of powers, reporting line, or effective date clauses.

## 3. The National Archives still supplies a second establishment act

The National Archives' administrative history for **Record Group 369, Records of the Employment and Training Administration and its Predecessors** states that the Office of Automation and Manpower was established by **Secretary's Order 5-61, 25 October 1961**.

National Archives, RG 369:

<https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/369.html>

This is not weak evidence that can simply be discarded because the April source is earlier. It is an authoritative archival lineage statement using a different administrative instrument.

### What can currently be concluded

```text
20 Apr 1961
General Order No. 110
→ OAM created / operating in Department of Labor chronology

        ? relation not yet recovered

25 Oct 1961
Secretary's Order 5-61
→ OAM "established" in NARA RG 369 administrative history
```

Possible explanations include formalization, reissuance, reorganization, transfer of reporting responsibility, or an archival predecessor-lineage convention.

**None of those explanations is yet proved.**

Do not write one of them as fact until General Order 110 and Secretary's Order 5-61 are compared directly.

---

## 4. NARA adds a direct institutional successor: OMAT, 15 June 1962

The same NARA administrative history gives a further high-value chronology:

- OAM was superseded by the **Office of Manpower, Automation, and Training (OMAT)**;
- OMAT was established by **Secretary's Order 17-62, 15 June 1962**;
- NARA then traces OMAT into the later Manpower Administration lineage.

This is useful because June 1962 falls after enactment of the **Manpower Development and Training Act (15 March 1962)** and because the office name itself now contains `Manpower`, `Automation`, and `Training`.

But the correct inference is limited.

### Strong claim

```text
OAM
→ direct administrative succession
OMAT
```

The archival source explicitly supplies this succession.

### Claim that is not yet licensed

```text
OAM problem formulation
→ transformed into
OMAT problem formulation
```

A renamed/reorganized office is not automatically a transformed historical problem.

To make the second claim we need the text of Secretary's Order 17-62, functional statements, programme records, or actor testimony showing that object, causal model, responsibility, or answer space changed.

This case therefore adds another useful distinction:

```text
institutional succession
!=
problem continuity
!=
problem transformation
```

## 5. Actor layer, institutional layer, and later archival reconstruction

Keep three evidence layers separate.

### Layer 1 — 1961 Department action

The FY1961 Department record is evidence that the Department itself treated OAM as created on 20 April under General Order 110 and placed Wolfbein in charge.

This is **institutional action evidence**.

It is not, by itself, a full actor formulation of what `automation`, `manpower`, or unemployment meant.

For that, use Wolfbein, Goldberg, congressional testimony, programme memoranda, and OAM publications.

### Layer 2 — 1961/62 administrative instruments

General Order 110, Secretary's Order 5-61, and Secretary's Order 17-62 are institutional acts. Their exact text is still needed to reconstruct jurisdiction, reporting line, functions, and any explicit problem statement.

### Layer 3 — later archival administrative history

NARA's RG 369 page is a later archival reconstruction of agency lineage. It is high-quality evidence for how the record group is organized and what predecessor/successor sequence it recognizes.

It must not be silently rewritten as a 1961 historical actor saying:

> “the office really began only on 25 October.”

The archive guide and the original order are not the same source object.

## 8. Hindsight and source-critical risks

1. **Choosing one "real birthday" because a linear chronology looks cleaner.** Two strong sources are using different administrative instruments; preserve the contradiction until explained.
2. **Formal-order date = problem birth.** A bureaucratic act cannot establish when a social/intellectual problem first became askable.
3. **Administrative succession = intellectual continuity.** Agencies can inherit files, staff, or jurisdiction while changing the object they govern.
4. **Renaming = problem transformation.** `OAM` → `OMAT` is a lead, not proof of conceptual change.
5. **Archive guide = original actor text.** NARA's administrative history is a later authoritative finding aid, not Secretary's Order 5-61 itself.
6. **Contemporary annual report = original order text.** It strongly identifies the April order but cannot support wording that only the signed order would establish.
7. **Same office name = same institutional configuration.** The April and October instruments may concern different organizational dimensions; this must be proved, not guessed.

---

## 9. Highest-value next sources

1. **General Order No. 110, 20 Apr 1961** — signed/order text and same-day Department press release.
2. **Secretary's Order 5-61, 25 Oct 1961** — exact text, effective date, purpose, reporting line, and whether it cites/supersedes General Order 110.
3. **Secretary's Order 17-62, 15 Jun 1962** — exact OMAT functions and any explicit relation to MDTA implementation.
4. Department of Labor organization charts / directives between April and November 1961.
5. Office of the Secretary correspondence around Wolfbein, Goldberg, Robert Goodwin, and training/retraining programme design.
6. Seymour Wolfbein's May 1961 material and later OAM/OMAT publications, to compare **problem formulation** with the administrative lineage rather than inferring it from names.
7. RG 369 and RG 174 series descriptions / box lists that can identify surviving copies of the orders and transition records.

---

## 10. Current provisional judgement

There is now enough evidence to strengthen the institutional chronology but **not** to collapse it.

The safest statement is:

> The Department of Labor's FY1961 record identifies OAM as created by **General Order No. 110 on 20 April 1961**. The National Archives' later RG 369 administrative history separately dates OAM's establishment to **Secretary's Order 5-61 on 25 October 1961** and records its supersession by OMAT under **Secretary's Order 17-62 on 15 June 1962**. Until the underlying orders are compared, the April–October relationship remains unresolved.

For Problem History, the methodological payoff is not discovering a single correct institutional birthday. It is learning to keep **administrative acts, institutional lineage, actor problem formulation, and problem emergence** as separate claims that require separate evidence.
