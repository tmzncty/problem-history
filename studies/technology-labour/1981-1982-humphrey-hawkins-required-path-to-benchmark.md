# 1981–82: from Humphrey–Hawkins required path to benchmarked projections

Status: **M2 implementation genealogy / disappearance-boundary test / not a frozen Problem Episode**

Pilot: technology–labour

This packet deepens:

- `1979-1983-humphrey-hawkins-goals-to-forecasts-implementation-drift.md`;
- `1946-1978-full-employment-revival-and-redefinition.md`.

The earlier packet established an important implementation ambiguity:

```text
1979–80 short-term “goals”
= forecasts

while

medium-term projections
= economic performance required to reach statutory unemployment / inflation goals
```

It also showed that in 1980 President Carter used the Full Employment and Balanced Growth Act's timetable-modification mechanism rather than simply declaring the statutory goals dead.

A remaining dating problem was therefore narrow and testable:

> When did the medium-term path itself stop functioning as a path that actually closes on the statutory targets?

Fresh primary evidence now moves that boundary substantially earlier than a simple Reagan-era abandonment story would allow.

The key sequence is:

```text
1979
short-term forecast
+ medium-term required path to statutory target

        ↓

1980
short-term forecast
+ medium-term required path to formally deferred target

        ↓

January 1981, Carter
medium-term table becomes “Economic projections, 1981–86”
+ the report says even the already deferred timetable cannot be met
+ a still more gradual path makes progress but does not close on the target

        ↓

February 1982, Reagan
five-year “Economic Projections, 1982–1987”
+ only significant progress toward unemployment / inflation goals
+ annual goals explicitly described as “benchmarks of economic progress”

        ↓

1983 JEC
explicitly disputes this practice:
goals should guide corrective policy rather than become forecasts
for which the Administration assumes no responsibility
```

The best present-day analytical shorthand is therefore:

**required-path collapse under Carter, followed by explicit benchmarkization under Reagan, inside a still-surviving statutory observatory.**

`required-path collapse` and `benchmarkization` are researcher-side labels. They are not actor vocabulary and are not proposed schema relations.

## 2. Baseline: 1980 still preserved a medium-term closure path

The earlier packet established the relevant baseline from the 1980 Economic Report and monetary-policy oversight.

After the second oil shock, Carter concluded that the original 1983 timetable for 4 percent unemployment and 3 percent inflation was no longer practicable. The Act allowed the President to recommend timetable modifications, and he used that authority.

The revised structure retained:

- short-run 1980–81 figures treated as forecasts;
- a medium-term sequence explicitly presented as the performance required to reach the postponed targets;
- 4 percent unemployment shifted to 1985;
- the 3 percent inflation objective shifted farther out.

In other words:

```text
shock
→ timetable revision
→ new target date
→ new path that still closes on the target
```

That distinction matters because a revised timetable is not yet the same thing as turning the target into a loose aspiration.

Research fixture already supported:

```text
authorized target deferral
!=
target abandonment
```

The 1981 evidence changes the next step.

## 3. January 1981: Carter says even the already deferred timetable cannot be met

The 1981 Economic Report again names the Humphrey–Hawkins interim goals and recalls that the previous year's report had already found the original 1983 timetable unattainable, chiefly in light of the 1979 oil-price shock.

But the report then goes materially farther than the 1980 deferral.

Its economic-policy discussion says that the United States must simultaneously:

- reduce persistent inflation;
- improve productivity growth;
- expand output and employment.

It then states that **even with progress on those fronts, the economy cannot simultaneously reach 4 percent unemployment and 3 percent inflation either on the timetable envisioned in the Act or on the timetable set out in the previous year's report.**

This is the critical dating evidence.

The 1980 path had already postponed the targets but still retained a closure relation:

```text
medium-term projection
→ arrives at revised statutory destination
```

The January 1981 report breaks that relation.

It warns that an attempt to force either unemployment or inflation to the target on the prior timetable would impede progress toward the other and could become self-defeating.

The Administration therefore presents what it calls a **more gradual path**.

This wording still preserves directional commitment:

```text
we still want progress toward the goals
```

But it no longer preserves the older five-year closure structure:

```text
we project a path that reaches the target by the operative target year
```

That is a substantive implementation change.

## 4. Table 26 makes the break measurable

The 1981 report's Table 26 is titled:

`Economic projections, 1981–86`

—not a table of required goal-path performance.

The unemployment projection is approximately:

| year | unemployment rate (%) |
|---|---:|
| 1981 | 7.7 |
| 1982 | 7.4 |
| 1983 | 7.0 |
| 1984 | 6.6 |
| 1985 | 6.2 |
| 1986 | 5.9 |

The inflation path is also still well above the earlier statutory objective by the end of the horizon.

This matters because the previous year's formally modified timetable had pointed toward 4 percent unemployment by 1985.

The new table reaches only about 6.2 percent unemployment in 1985 and 5.9 percent in 1986.

Therefore:

```text
1980
revised target year + path closes on target

1981
same broad normative direction
+ projection horizon continues
- projected path no longer closes on the target
```

This is stronger than merely saying that the target date moved again.

The actor does not simply supply another clear replacement date in the same functional form. Instead, the report openly presents a path of gradual progress that remains short of the target within the planning horizon.

### Method implication

A new research fixture becomes available:

```text
medium-term projection survives
!=
medium-term correction path survives
```

and:

```text
progress-toward-goal language
!=
closure-on-goal architecture
```

## 5. This pushes the chronology before the Reagan Administration

A tempting later narrative is:

```text
Carter implements Humphrey–Hawkins
→ Reagan arrives
→ numerical commitments become loose forecasts
```

The January 1981 evidence blocks that chronology.

The medium-term closure path is already broken in Carter's final report.

This does **not** make Carter and Reagan formulations identical.

The safer chronology is:

```text
1979 Carter
short-term forecasts
+ medium-term required path to statutory 1983 goals

1980 Carter
short-term forecasts
+ medium-term required path to formally deferred goals

January 1981 Carter
prior deferred timetable also declared infeasible
+ “more gradual path”
+ medium-term projections do not reach target

February 1982 Reagan
five-year projections do not reach unemployment/inflation targets
+ annual goals explicitly redescribed as benchmarks
```

Thus:

```text
required-path collapse
precedes
explicit benchmark interpretation
```

That distinction is historically important.

It avoids attributing every weakening of the numerical-goal mechanism to partisan turnover while still allowing the Reagan Administration's interpretation to be substantively different.

## 6. What exactly changed in 1981?

The strongest formulation is not `the goals disappeared`.

They did not.

The report continues to discuss them as desirable destinations and continues to evaluate economic performance relative to them.

The stronger claim is about the relation among four elements:

```text
statutory target
planning horizon
projected path
policy-correction obligation
```

In 1979–80, the medium-term projection was explicitly organized around the performance **required to reach** a target.

By January 1981:

```text
target remains
+
projection remains
+
policy discussion remains

but

projection no longer reaches target inside the operative planning horizon
```

The target therefore begins to function less like the end point of a closure algorithm and more like a remote directional reference.

Researcher shorthand:

**closure-path collapse**.

Again, this is not Carter Administration vocabulary.

---

## 7. Do not overstate the legal conclusion

The 1981 report creates a new historical ambiguity that must be preserved rather than resolved by hindsight.

The Act had allowed timetable modification.

In 1980 Carter used that authority explicitly.

The 1981 report then says even last year's revised timetable is no longer simultaneously achievable and presents a more gradual path.

What remains unresolved in this packet is the exact formal legal status of that 1981 move:

- Was a further statutory timetable modification formally recommended elsewhere in the Presidential / budget package?
- Or did the report effectively allow the numerical planning horizon to cease closing on a specified target date without replacing it with a new operative date in the same form?

Until the relevant statutory report sections, budget message, and congressional response are checked line by line, do **not** code January 1981 as either:

- settled statutory noncompliance; or
- an unquestionably valid formal timetable modification.

Current evidence supports the narrower historical claim:

> Carter's final Economic Report no longer supplies a five-year path that reaches even the previously deferred unemployment target, and explicitly says the prior timetable cannot simultaneously deliver the unemployment and inflation objectives.

That is enough to date the functional change without pretending to settle its legal status.

## 8. 1981 Federal Reserve reporting: observatory continuity without a shared macro closure path

The Board's 1981 annual report shows that Humphrey–Hawkins reporting machinery remains active.

The FOMC continues to:

- review monetary and credit aggregate ranges under the Act;
- provide its own projections;
- compare those projections with Administration forecasts;
- discuss unemployment, productivity, inflation, and output.

The record describes the Administration's mid-year economic numbers as an updated **forecast** and places them alongside FOMC projections.

At the same time, the Fed still recognizes lower unemployment and higher productivity as goals central to Humphrey–Hawkins.

This is not evidence that the goals vanished.

Instead it shows a decomposed observatory:

```text
statutory full-employment / price objectives remain

but

Administration forecast
!= FOMC projection
!= monetary aggregate target range
```

The interinstitutional architecture survives while the idea of one shared quantitative macro path becomes weaker.

Research fixture:

```text
common statutory observatory
!=
common operational forecast
```

and:

```text
reporting under the same Act
!=
acceptance of one shared correction path
```

## 9. February 1982: the Reagan report makes the reinterpretation explicit

The 1982 Economic Report provides a qualitatively stronger actor statement.

The report does not simply ignore Humphrey–Hawkins.

Under `PROSPECTS BEYOND 1983`, it:

- invokes the Act directly;
- restates the general goals of full employment, productivity growth, and price stability;
- notes the Act's five-year numerical-goal requirement;
- recalls that the prior Administration extended the timetable beyond its five-year planning horizon;
- supplies a new 1982–87 projection table.

That makes the later interpretation especially valuable: it is not an accidental omission in a report that forgot the statute.

```text
five-year projection exists
+

+

```

Again:

```text
projection toward a goal
!=

```

## 11. The strongest new actor statement: annual goals as benchmarks

The most important 1982 passage is not the numerical table.

Immediately after discussing the projections, the report argues that government cannot fully anticipate the course of the economy or precisely direct economic outcomes.

It therefore says that the annual goals should be viewed as **“benchmarks of economic progress.”**

That short phrase is highly diagnostic.

The historical transformation can now be stated at actor level:

```text
1979–80 medium-term formulation:
what economic performance is required to reach the statutory objective?

        ↓

1982 Executive formulation:
what projected outcomes indicate progress, with annual goals serving as benchmarks?
```

This is not merely a change in numbers.

It changes the epistemic and responsibility relation between government and outcome.

A `required path` presumes that policy planning should be organized around closing the distance to a destination.

A `benchmark` can instead function as a comparison point for judging progress even when policymakers deny that government can precisely determine the outcome.

Research fixture:

```text
benchmarking
!=
binding correction rule
```

and:

```text
actor-explicit limits on controllability
can weaken outcome obligation
without repealing the stated goal
```

## 12. Why the 1982 language should not be retrojected into the statute

The word `benchmark` is an Executive implementation claim.

It must not be silently converted into:

```text
what Congress in 1978 always meant
```

The 1983 Joint Economic Committee becomes especially important precisely because it disputes this interpretation.

The JEC majority says short-term goals should function as policy anchors / beacons: divergence should warn that policy is not meeting its objectives and should be reconsidered.

It then criticizes **both Administrations since enactment** for supplying forecasts for whose attainment the Administration did not assume responsibility instead of the sort of goals the committee believed the law required.

This is unusually valuable Problem History evidence.

It confirms that:

- the forecast / benchmark practice was visible to contemporaries;
- its meaning was politically and institutionally contested;
- historical actors disagreed about whether the new implementation preserved the problem's original responsibility structure.

Thus:

```text
1982 Executive:
uncertain controllability
→ goals as benchmarks

1983 JEC majority:
policy responsibility
→ goals as anchors that should trigger reconsideration
```

The conflict should remain in the repository.

## 13. Hawkins's stronger claim remains actor-specific

Augustus Hawkins's 1983 additional views go beyond the committee majority.

He argues that Administration paths far from statutory unemployment / inflation levels, without policies adequate to close the gap, violate statutory mandates.

This is direct sponsor evidence about how he understood the law's accountability structure.

But:

```text
sponsor accusation of violation
!=
settled legal conclusion
```

The packet therefore uses Hawkins to identify a strong competing actor formulation, not to adjudicate the law.

## 15. Problem History method contribution

This packet adds several source-grounded fixtures.

### 15.1 Target survival is multidimensional

```text
statutory target survives
!= target date survives
!= closure path survives
!= corrective obligation survives
```

Do not use a single yes/no field for `target continuity` when the source record distinguishes these layers.

### 15.2 Timetable revision and closure-path collapse are different events

```text
target date is formally postponed
+ new path still reaches it
```

is different from:

```text
target remains named
+ projection horizon no longer reaches it
```

The first can be authorized adaptation inside the problem architecture.
The second may indicate a more consequential weakening of that architecture.

### 15.3 Goal / forecast / projection / benchmark are not synonyms

These terms carry different responsibility relations:

```text
goal
→ desired / normatively selected outcome

forecast
→ expected outcome

required-path projection
→ performance needed to arrive at selected outcome

benchmark
→ comparison point for judging progress
```

Historical actors may mix them, but the researcher should not normalize them into one generic `target` field without recording the function each performs.

### 15.4 Observatory persistence can outlive closure architecture

The following can all remain alive:

- statute;
- annual reports;
- CEA;
- congressional review;
- Fed reporting;
- unemployment and inflation statistics;
- five-year projection tables;
- target vocabulary.

Yet the relation:

```text
observed / projected gap
→ policy must change enough to close the gap
```

can weaken.

Therefore:

```text
observatory persistence
!=
problem-governance persistence
```

### 15.5 Epistemic claims can change political obligation

The 1982 report's argument that government cannot precisely anticipate or direct outcomes is not just a technical forecasting statement.

When coupled with the benchmark interpretation, it helps re-specify responsibility:

```text
limited controllability
→ benchmark rather than precise outcome commitment
```

This suggests a broader Problem History question for other cases:

> When actors redefine a phenomenon as only partially controllable, does the governing problem disappear, or does responsibility shift from outcome guarantee to process / effort / monitoring?

Do not generalize this fixture beyond the present case until another domain supplies a comparable example.

## 16. Actor formulation vs later reconstruction vs present analysis

### 16.1 Historical actors

**Carter Administration, January 1981**

- statutory goals remain desirable;
- prior timetable is no longer simultaneously feasible;
- forcing one target too rapidly may worsen the other;
- a more gradual path should make sustainable progress.

**Reagan Administration, February 1982**

- statutory goals remain relevant general objectives;
- five-year projections show progress but not attainment;
- government cannot precisely control macro outcomes;
- annual goals should function as benchmarks.

**Federal Reserve, 1981–82**

- Humphrey–Hawkins reporting remains operative;
- monetary aggregate ranges and Fed projections remain institutionally distinct from Administration economic forecasts;
- shared broad employment / price-stability objectives do not automatically produce one shared quantitative macro path.

**JEC majority, 1983**

- criticizes both Administrations' forecast treatment;
- understands statutory goals as stronger policy anchors that should trigger reconsideration when missed.

**Hawkins, 1983**

- makes a still stronger sponsor-side claim about statutory obligation and noncompliance.

### 16.2 Later institutional reconstruction

Later Federal Reserve histories are useful for mapping how projections and reporting practices evolved.

They are not contemporaneous neutral referees of the dispute.

### 16.3 Present researcher analysis

The following labels are ours:

- `required-path collapse`;
- `benchmarkization`;
- `closure architecture`;
- `soft disappearance of answer obligation`.

They should remain clearly marked as analytical shorthand unless historical actors using equivalent categories are recovered.

## 17. Current relation judgment

Do not freeze a single relation for the whole 1979–83 sequence.

A more useful provisional decomposition is:

### 1979 → 1980

Strong `reformulated` candidate.

The target survives and the medium-term closure path survives, but the Act's contingency mechanism changes the timetable after an exogenous shock.

### 1980 → January 1981

Stronger transformation candidate than previously recognized.

The key change is not merely another numerical update. The medium-term projection stops closing on the previously modified target and becomes a more gradual progress path.

Candidate:

- `reformulated`, if normative goal + observatory + directional policy relation carry most identity weight;
- possibly stronger implementation-decoupling note at the responsibility / target-function dimension.

Do not freeze before resolving the formal status of the 1981 timetable change.

### January 1981 → February 1982

Strong `reformulated` candidate with a new actor-explicit epistemic rationale.

The projection non-closure already exists under Carter; Reagan makes the looser functional interpretation explicit by calling annual goals benchmarks.

### 1982 → 1983 dispute

Not a normal successor edge.

This is better represented as a contemporaneous **identity / compliance controversy** over the meaning of the same surviving statutory apparatus.

## 18. Hindsight risks

### Risk A: partisan compression

Bad:

```text
Reagan killed Humphrey–Hawkins.
```

Why unsafe:

The medium-term closure path is already broken in Carter's January 1981 report.

Safer:

- Carter introduces / preserves forecast treatment and later loses a target-closing medium-term path;
- Reagan explicitly turns the annual-goal function toward benchmarked progress;
- JEC/Hawkins later contest that interpretation.

### Risk B: legal overclaim

Bad:

```text
1981/82 reports violated the statute.
```

Why unsafe:

That is a contested actor claim, especially strong in Hawkins's 1983 additional views, not a settled judicial conclusion recovered here.

### Risk C: lexical disappearance test

Bad:

```text
“full employment” remains, therefore the problem is continuous.
```

The responsibility relation can change while the word survives.

### Risk D: table continuity test

Bad:

```text
five-year projections remain, therefore planning continuity is strong.
```

A table can shift from required attainment path to forecast/progress benchmark.

### Risk E: forecast = goal

Do not normalize actor categories just because they occur in the same statutory reporting framework.

## 22. Working conclusion

The strongest new finding is a revised date and a revised mechanism.

The medium-term Humphrey–Hawkins correction path does **not** survive intact until the Reagan Administration.

By January 1981, Carter's final Economic Report already says that even the previously deferred unemployment / inflation timetable cannot be met simultaneously and replaces the former target-closing path with a more gradual projection that does not reach the target inside the horizon.

The Reagan Administration's 1982 report then makes the looser implementation logic explicit: numerical annual goals should function as **benchmarks of economic progress** because government cannot precisely anticipate or direct macroeconomic outcomes.

The 1983 Joint Economic Committee subsequently contests exactly this move, arguing that goals were supposed to operate as policy anchors rather than forecasts for which the Administration accepted no responsibility.

The emerging disappearance pattern is therefore neither repeal nor lexical extinction.

It is:

```text
statutory goal survives
+ observatory survives
+ projection survives
+ reporting survives

while

required path stops closing on the goal
then
annual goal is reinterpreted as benchmark
then
the weakened responsibility relation becomes an explicit political dispute
```

That is a much stronger candidate for **soft disappearance of an answer obligation** than a simple story of a law being abandoned after a change of Administration.
