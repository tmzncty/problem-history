# 1928–1937: technological unemployment before and during the Depression

> **Status:** M2 Episode B source packet; relation verdicts remain provisional.
>
> **Pilot:** technology/labour, used only as a present researcher retrieval umbrella.
>
> **Purpose:** reconstruct historically specific formulations around `technological unemployment` without letting Keynes 1930 stand in for the whole interwar controversy.
>
> **Boundary:** the stable phrase `technological unemployment` does **not** establish one stable problem. This packet separates congressional, organized-labour, institutional-economics, socialist, British parliamentary, and federal planning formulations.

## Executive finding

The strongest new finding is chronological and structural.

A substantial United States institutional debate over technological unemployment was already underway **before the October 1929 stock-market crash**. Senate Resolution 219 was adopted on 3 May 1928; the Senate Committee on Education and Labor held unemployment hearings from December 1928 into February 1929; William Green of the American Federation of Labor testified on 14 December 1928 about a modern unemployment problem caused in part by machine displacement; and John R. Commons told the committee in February 1929 that actors were `now learning to call` one category technological unemployment.

This makes the following retrospective shorthand unsafe:

```text
Great Depression
    ↓
creates the technological-unemployment problem
    ↓
Keynes names it in 1930
```

The Depression plainly changed the scale, stakes, evidential environment, and politics of unemployment. It did **not** create the first recoverable institutional formulation in this source chain.

The second finding is that the same label already covered materially different problems.

```text
1928–29 Senate committee
    skilled-worker displacement
    + loss of trade/skill
    + transition suffering
    + social distribution of the "price of progress"

William Green / AFL
    machine displacement accepted as a real risk
    + machinery itself not rejected
    + absorption may cease to keep pace
    + responsibility for displaced workers

John R. Commons
    technological unemployment as a newly named category
    + efficiency outrunning market expansion / voluntary attrition
    + unemployment reserve funds
    + stabilization and purchasing power
    + Federal Reserve / macro absorption responsibility

1932 Socialist campaign leaflet
    labor-displacing machine
    + technological displacement as evidence against capitalism
    + systemic political answer

1932 British parliamentary formulation
    rationalisation / improved production
    → fewer workers required
    → national planning problem

1937 National Resources Committee / Weintraub
    technological unemployment as a measurement and planning problem
    + aggregate absorption
    + individual occupational displacement/readjustment
    + indirect causal attribution
    + anticipatory interagency monitoring
```

The safest present hypothesis is therefore that ca. 1928–37 contains a **problem field with competing and changing formulations**, not a single question with one answer.

The 1937 federal report is especially important because it turns the object itself into an epistemic and administrative problem. Displacement may occur indirectly across firms and industries; affected workers and employers may not know that technological change elsewhere is causal. `Technological unemployment` therefore becomes not only a visible machine-versus-worker event but also a problem of measurement, causal attribution, occupational follow-up, forecasting, and policy coordination.

No relation below is frozen. In particular, the resemblance between the 1937 distinction between aggregate absorption and individual dislocation and the 1966 National Commission distinction between aggregate unemployment and particular-worker displacement is currently **researcher analogy only** until transmission is demonstrated.

## 2.2 Why chronology matters

Senate Resolution 219 was adopted on 3 May 1928, and the principal hearings began in December 1928. Thus the institutional problem is already observable before the Wall Street crash of October 1929.

This does not prove that 1928 is the `birth` of technological unemployment. It proves something narrower and stronger:

> a United States federal legislative body had already made unemployment causation, prevention, relief, and technological displacement objects of organized inquiry before the crash.

Any emergence claim must go further backward into the early/mid-1920s and must distinguish first naming from first problematization.

# 3. Senate Committee formulation: individual displacement and the social price of progress

The committee report divides unemployment into several causal/type categories, including cyclical, seasonal, and technological unemployment.

Its technological-unemployment discussion centers on situations in which machines or other devices supplant people and skilled workers find that a trade or skill is no longer needed. The report asks what becomes of such workers, what can be done about individual tragedies, and what those tragedies mean for society.

It explicitly acknowledges the compensation argument that new industries such as automobiles, aircraft, and radio can absorb labour displaced by machinery. But it refuses to treat aggregate absorption as an answer to the individual transition problem: occupational mismatch, delay, idleness, lost income, suffering, and forced changes of environment remain.

The report therefore treats technological progress as capable of producing a socially distributed gain while imposing concentrated transitional costs on particular workers.

A safe actor-constrained reconstruction is:

> If technological progress destroys particular trades or skills even while new industries expand, who bears the costs of the worker's period of displacement and readjustment, and what obligation does society have toward that worker?

This sentence is researcher paraphrase, not a quotation.

### Problem structure

| Field | Committee formulation |
|---|---|
| target/object | workers/trades/skills displaced by machines and devices |
| stakes | idleness, loss, occupational mismatch, transition suffering |
| presupposition | aggregate creation of new work need not solve the individual worker's problem |
| answer space | prevention, relief, stabilization, social burden-sharing, readjustment |
| responsibility | industry and society; explored through hearings and policy mechanisms |
| uncertainty | volume/measurement and relation to other unemployment causes |

### Why this differs from a modern binary

The committee is not simply asking:

```text
Does technology reduce total employment: yes or no?
```

Its formulation can remain live even if total employment later rises, because the key explanandum includes **who is displaced, for how long, at what occupational cost, and who pays**.

# 4. William Green, AFL, 14 December 1928: accept machinery, govern displacement

## 4.1 Actor identity

William Green testified as president of the American Federation of Labor on 14 December 1928.

In the hearing he first distinguishes unemployment associated with national depression and seasonal fluctuations from a `modern industrial` unemployment problem caused to some extent by machine displacement. He also stresses the inadequacy and lateness of unemployment statistics.

Later in the testimony Green says newer industries had so far absorbed many workers displaced by machinery but warns that the `peak of absorption` might eventually be reached while mechanical development and displacement continued.

Most important for hindsight control, Green explicitly rejects an anti-machine characterization of the AFL. He says the Federation had not stood in the way of machinery and regarded machinery as capable of relieving drudgery and increasing income. His problem is the unemployment of people displaced during this process and the need to make provision before it becomes acute.

### Actor-constrained reconstruction

> How can organized labour, employers, and public institutions preserve the gains of mechanization while preventing displaced workers from becoming permanently unemployed or bearing the transition alone?

Again, this is researcher paraphrase.

### Problem structure

| Field | Green / AFL formulation |
|---|---|
| target/object | workers displaced by mechanization |
| stakes | permanent unemployment, skill loss, market/purchasing-power disruption |
| presupposition | mechanization can be socially beneficial; past new-industry absorption may not continue indefinitely |
| answer space | joint study, collective bargaining where possible, public attention, transition provision |
| responsibility | unions + employers + potentially government |
| rejected formulation | `organized labour is simply opposed to machinery` |

### Method consequence

This is a strong real-world failure case for:

```text
labour concern about technological unemployment
!=
labour opposition to technological change
```

The distinction must survive later summaries.

# 5. John R. Commons, February 1929: reserve funds, stabilization, and macro absorption

## 5.1 Source position

John R. Commons testified over multiple sessions; the relevant resumed testimony is on 9 February 1929. He discusses seasonal, cyclical, and what actors were `now learning to call` technological unemployment.

Commons describes the latter as something newly prominent over roughly the preceding five or six years, connecting it to large efficiency gains. His Chicago clothing example describes rising sales and lower prices alongside a reduced workforce; when technical improvement proceeds faster than market expansion or voluntary attrition, additional workers must be laid off.

He then places the problem inside an institutional design:

- unemployment reserve funds can compensate workers laid off because of technical changes while they seek other occupations;
- such funds can help preserve purchasing power;
- industry-wide expansion and creation of new industries cannot be solved solely at the firm level;
- he assigns substantial stabilization responsibility to the Federal Reserve system.

Commons therefore does not use `technological unemployment` merely as a description of a machine taking a job. He makes it part of a theory of business responsibility, employment stabilization, reserve finance, labour cooperation, and macroeconomic absorption.

### Actor-constrained reconstruction

> When productivity improvements reduce labour requirements faster than markets and normal labour turnover can absorb the change, what firm-level reserves and economy-wide stabilization mechanisms can carry displaced workers through the adjustment?

### Difference from Green

Green and Commons overlap but should not be merged.

```text
Green:
mechanization accepted
+ possible exhaustion of new-industry absorption
+ displaced-worker protection / collective responsibility

Commons:
efficiency increase relative to market expansion / attrition
+ unemployment reserve fund design
+ firm stabilization incentives
+ purchasing power
+ Federal Reserve responsibility for aggregate expansion
```

Shared vocabulary and a shared hearing forum create strong **problem-field overlap**, not identity by themselves.

# 6. Depression-era political counter-frame: the machine as evidence against capitalism

## 6.1 1932 Socialist Party leaflet

The University of Tulsa Special Collections preserves James H. Maurer's 1932 leaflet *Unemployment and the Mechanical Man* as item `2006.003.1.2.68` in the Harold Leventhal proletarian archive.

Catalogue:

- https://utulsa.as.atlas-sys.com/repositories/2/archival_objects/95079

The catalogue records a bi-fold campaign leaflet printed on all sides, with a robot labelled `Labor-Displacing Machine` sweeping workers from a payroll sheet. Its scope note reproduces the leaflet's claim that human labour is being replaced by the robot across industry and culminates in an electoral choice between continued capitalism and socialism.

**Evidence strength:**

- high for item identity, date, creator attribution, physical description, and archival provenance;
- medium-high for quoted campaign wording because the archival catalogue reproduces it;
- exact layout/context should still be checked against the digital image before final quotation freeze.

### Why it matters

This formulation changes the answer space dramatically.

The committee/Green/Commons formulations can all accommodate mechanization inside a reformed capitalist institutional order. Maurer's campaign argument uses labour-displacing machinery as evidence for a systemic political alternative.

```text
technological displacement
→ not merely a transition-cost problem
→ evidence about the viability/justice of the economic system
→ electoral-socialist answer space
```

This is a competing Depression-era formulation, not proof that `workers` or `the left` shared one view.

# 8. 1937 National Resources Committee: from displacement controversy to national measurement and planning

## 8.1 Source identity

United States National Resources Committee, Science Committee, Subcommittee on Technology, *Technological Trends and National Policy, Including the Social Implications of New Inventions* (Washington: U.S. Government Printing Office, 1937), House Document 360, 75th Congress, 1st session.

Public scan / metadata:

- Internet Archive item: https://archive.org/details/technologicaltre1937unitrich
- OCR transcription used for retrieval: https://archive.org/stream/technologicaltre1937unitrich/technologicaltre1937unitrich_djvu.txt
- Open Library bibliographic record: https://openlibrary.org/books/OL23346777M/Technological_trends_and_national_policy

The Internet Archive item identifies a 1937 GPO edition, 388 printed pages, prepared by the Subcommittee on Technology of the Science Committee, with William F. Ogburn as subcommittee chair.

**Source-control warning:** the searchable text is OCR. Printed page locators below are useful, but exact quotations must be checked against page images before final Episode freeze.

## 8.2 Institutional problem expansion

The transmittal letter to the President describes the report as a major attempt to identify inventions likely to affect living and working conditions over the following 10–25 years, identify problems their use may bring, and support prompt national adjustment with the least social suffering and loss.

The report's findings state simultaneously that:

- sudden adoption of inventions can produce tragic technological unemployment;
- inventions also create occupations and industries;
- no satisfactory measure of the volume of technological unemployment had yet been developed;
- occupational obsolescence and worker readjustment remain real costs;
- future unemployment depends on technology only in part, alongside population, production composition, demand, markets, migration, hiring practices, and other factors.

This is not a simple government endorsement of either `machines destroy jobs` or `compensation solves everything`.

## 8.3 Administrative answer space

The report recommends a joint committee spanning agencies including Labor, Commerce, Agriculture, Mines, the Interstate Commerce Commission, Social Security Board, and WPA to:

- keep abreast of technological developments;
- identify occupations and industries likely to be affected;
- estimate likely unemployment effects;
- make this information available to affected industry and labour.

The answer space has therefore become partly **anticipatory and administrative**: observe, forecast, classify, coordinate, and disseminate before displacement becomes a completed fact.

# 9. David Weintraub, 1937: the causal object becomes difficult to observe

## 9.1 Section identity

Section V, `Unemployment and Increasing Productivity`, begins on printed p. 67 and is attributed to David Weintraub.

Weintraub says production, employment, and productivity statistics provide an approach toward understanding what had come to be referred to popularly as `technological unemployment`, while warning that statistical analysis cannot by itself settle underlying causes.

This wording matters: even in an official technological-trends report, the label is treated as a popular/problematic category requiring analytic decomposition.

## 9.2 Twofold problem

At printed p. 87, Weintraub gives an unusually explicit formulation. He divides the problem into two parts:

1. whether total production expands sufficiently to offset declining labour requirements and a growing labour supply;
2. how individual employment dislocations accompanying technological progress are adjusted.

This gives Episode B a strong contemporaneous split between **aggregate absorption** and **individual displacement/readjustment**.

The distinction also prevents a common historical error:

```text
new sectors gained jobs
therefore
workers displaced from old sectors were successfully reabsorbed
```

Weintraub explicitly questions that inference because occupational requirements can differ sharply across expanding and contracting sectors.

## 9.3 Displacement as an attribution problem

The report also describes indirect displacement. A technological change in one plant can reduce costs, damage another plant's business, and produce layoffs there. The local workers and employer may describe the event as `slow business` without recognizing that technological change elsewhere contributed causally.

That transforms the research object:

```text
visible machine installed
→ visible worker dismissed
```

is only one case. A broader object is:

```text
technological change somewhere in a production/market system
→ relative cost/output change
→ indirect production decline elsewhere
→ layoffs whose local actors may not identify technology as cause
```

This makes `technological unemployment` partly an **observability and causal-attribution problem**.

That matters methodologically for Problem History: actor recognition can itself be uneven across causal positions. The historian must not infer that every affected worker formulated the problem as technological unemployment merely because a later analyst can trace a technological causal chain.

# 10. What changed between 1928–29 and 1937?

The sources support a provisional transformation hypothesis, not a frozen relation.

## 10.1 Continuity evidence

- the phrase `technological unemployment` remains in use;
- machine/technical change reducing labour requirements remains a recognized mechanism;
- individual displacement and readjustment remain central;
- aggregate creation of new work is repeatedly distinguished from the fate of particular displaced workers;
- public responsibility for mitigating transition remains thinkable.

## 10.2 Discontinuity evidence

- the 1929 committee report foregrounds skilled-worker/trade destruction and social burden-sharing;
- Green foregrounds organized labour's acceptance of mechanization plus protection before absorption fails;
- Commons embeds the issue in firm reserve funds and stabilization institutions;
- Depression political actors can use machine displacement to indict capitalism itself;
- by 1937 federal research foregrounds measurement, statistical decomposability, indirect causation, occupational follow-up, forecasting, and interagency planning;
- `technology` is no longer treated as a sufficient explanation for aggregate unemployment.

## 10.3 Current relation verdict

```text
1928–29 congressional / labour / institutional-economics cluster
        ↓ ?
1937 national planning / measurement cluster

verdict: undetermined
candidate: reformulated or transformed_successor
```

Promotion requires direct transmission/institutional evidence: personnel, citations, reports, committee records, or explicit retrospective recognition linking the earlier investigation to the later federal research programme.

# 11. Keynes 1930 must not stand in for Episode B

The M1 survey already established Keynes's actor formulation of `technological unemployment` as labour-saving innovation outrunning the discovery of new uses for labour, framed as a temporary maladjustment within a longer-run abundance problem.

The sources here show why that text cannot organize the entire episode.

Before Keynes's essay, US congressional, labour, and institutional-economics actors were already debating:

- skill/trade destruction;
- individual transition costs;
- absorption limits;
- statistics and observability;
- unemployment reserves;
- purchasing power;
- business and central-bank responsibility.

Therefore:

```text
Keynes 1930
!=
the Depression-era technological-unemployment problem
```

At most, Keynes is one historically important formulation inside a wider transatlantic field.

No Keynes↔US relation is asserted here without reception/transmission evidence.

# 13. Conditions under which the problem changed

The following are present researcher hypotheses constrained by the evidence, not actor quotations.

## 13.1 Pre-crash productivity and observable displacement

The 1928–29 materials show that rising efficiency and shrinking labour requirements could already be problematized during prosperity. This creates a different causal puzzle from mass unemployment produced during a general collapse.

## 13.2 New statistical categories and administrative observation

Green complains that adequate unemployment statistics arrive too late. Commons distinguishes seasonal, cyclical, and technological unemployment. By 1937 the federal research apparatus is explicitly trying to measure productivity/employment relations and forecast occupational effects.

The problem becomes more governable partly by becoming more measurable—but measurement also exposes causal ambiguity.

## 13.3 Organized labour's conditional acceptance of mechanization

Green's testimony makes a simple `workers versus machines` story untenable. A pro-mechanization stance can coexist with demands that displacement costs be socialized, negotiated, anticipated, or compensated.

## 13.4 Depression-scale unemployment expands the answer space

By 1932, technological unemployment can appear inside arguments for national planning or socialist transformation, not merely firm-level adjustment.

This is not proof that technology was believed to cause all Depression unemployment. It shows that mass unemployment changed the political stakes attached to technological displacement.

## 13.5 New Deal planning institutions alter problem responsibility

The 1937 report places responsibility not only on employer/worker adjustment but on a multi-agency state apparatus capable of technological observation, occupational forecasting, and policy coordination.

This is a possible case of **responsibility migration**:

```text
worker + employer + union
        ↓
firm / reserve / central-bank stabilization
        ↓
interagency technological monitoring and planning
```

The arrows are hypotheses and must not be mistaken for a single institutional succession chain.

# 16. Hindsight / failure risks exposed by this episode

## 16.1 `The Great Depression created the debate`

Unsafe. Federal inquiry and explicit machine-displacement formulations are documented in 1928–29.

## 16.2 `Keynes coined or originated the whole problem`

Unsafe. Whatever Keynes's importance to later economic vocabulary, the US hearings show both the term and organized problematization before his 1930 essay.

## 16.3 `Labour opposed machinery`

Directly contradicted as a blanket claim by Green's testimony.

## 16.4 `Technological unemployment = total unemployment`

Unsafe. Actors repeatedly distinguish technological, cyclical, seasonal, aggregate, and individual-displacement dimensions.

## 16.5 `New jobs elsewhere solve displacement`

Unsafe. Both the 1929 committee and 1937 report distinguish aggregate job creation from the actual occupational trajectory of displaced workers.

## 16.6 `Shorter-hours / socialism / planning proves causal monism`

Unsafe. A proposed remedy can address unemployment without proving that the actor attributes all unemployment to technology.

## 16.7 `Government report = consensus`

Unsafe. The 1937 report explicitly acknowledges measurement limits and competing causal factors.

## 16.8 `Stable label = stable causal object`

Unsafe. Weintraub's discussion expands the causal object from visible machine replacement to indirect market-mediated displacement and measurement problems.

## 16.9 `Later analyst can identify technological causation = historical worker formulated it that way`

Unsafe. The 1937 indirect-displacement example explicitly allows local workers/employers to be unaware of the technological causal chain.

## 16.10 `1937 aggregate/individual distinction = 1966 problem continuity`

Currently only structural analogy.

## 16.11 `Bix/Fano reconstruction = actor evidence`

Not permitted.

## 16.12 `OCR = authoritative quotation`

Not permitted. FRASER and Internet Archive OCR are retrieval aids; scan/page binding is still required for final quotation freeze.

# 17. What remains uncertain

1. **Emergence before 1928.** Commons's `last five or six years` is actor testimony, not a birth date. Early-1920s journals, AFL materials, business literature, and unemployment surveys must be checked.
2. **Term genealogy.** Who first used or popularized `technological unemployment` in US/UK economic and policy discourse remains open.
3. **Direct transmission 1929→1937.** Isador Lubin, BLS, National Research Project, WPA/NRC personnel, citations, and committee files may supply continuity, but it has not yet been proven.
4. **AFL answer space during the Depression.** Green 1928 is strong, but 1932–35 AFL convention records and shorter-hours campaigns are needed before describing a labour trajectory.
5. **Business/engineering counter-formulations.** This packet is currently stronger on labour/government than on business, engineering societies, and corporate public relations.
6. **British Episode B.** Lewis Jones provides one checkpoint; a full British cluster is not yet reconstructed.
7. **Maurer leaflet image verification.** Catalogue evidence is strong but the actual digitized leaflet image should be checked before exact citation freeze.
8. **J. M. Clark.** The 1937 report cites and discusses his 1935 *Economics of Planning Public Works*; direct actor wording should come from Clark's own text, not Weintraub's paraphrase.
9. **Endpoint.** Nothing here proves the problem disappears after 1937. The likely result is persistence plus further transformation into wartime/postwar employment, automation, and productivity debates.

# 20. Research conclusion

The main contribution of this pass is not another history of people fearing machines. It is a more discriminating historical claim:

> Before the Depression, technological unemployment was already an institutionalized problem of displacement, transition cost, stabilization, and responsibility. During the Depression and New Deal, that problem field widened and was partially reconstructed as a political-system problem and as a federal measurement/forecasting problem. The label persisted while the object, responsible actors, causal model, and answer space changed.

That claim is supported strongly enough to justify M2 continuation, but not strongly enough to freeze a 1928→1937 lineage. The next decisive step is direct institutional-transmission evidence and a stronger Depression-era opponent/counter-formulation set.
