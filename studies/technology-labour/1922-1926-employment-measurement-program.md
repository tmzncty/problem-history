# 1922–1926: employment measurement as a problem in its own right

## Status

Research packet. This note reconstructs the problem as posed by the historical actors before the 1928 unemployment-statistics controversy. It deliberately separates actor-level formulations from later institutional reconstruction and present-day analytical vocabulary.

## New finding

The American Statistical Association's Committee on Measurement of Employment was already organized in early 1922, and its 1923 tentative recommendations show that the immediate problem was not simply "how many people are unemployed?" The committee treated the production of timely, comparable, geographically and industrially representative **employment** statistics as a problem in its own right.

The strongest new evidence is the contemporary reprint in the May 1923 *Monthly Labor Review*. It records the committee's authorization, membership, seven tentative recommendations, and five outstanding problems. Crucially, the committee explicitly asked whether manufacturing employment was a fair index once wage earners could transfer from one kind of work to another. It proposed broadening collection to mining, transport/communications, construction, wholesale and retail trade, logging, and agriculture, explicitly ranking industries partly by their significance "from the point of view of control of the business cycle."

This means the later distinction:

    establishment employment decline != persons unemployed

has an earlier precursor:

    manufacturing employment decline != national employment decline

The precursor is actor-level, not a present-day reconstruction.

## Primary evidence

### 1. Committee formation and remit — high

*Monthly Labor Review*, May 1923, pp. 187–188 (FRASER full text):
https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/monthly-labor-review-6130/may-1923-608073/fulltext

The Review states that the ASA board authorized a committee in early 1922 to study methods for collecting, verifying, analyzing, and presenting employment data, and the extent to which standard forms and methods were possible. Mary Van Kleeck chaired it; the remaining members included statisticians from State and Federal bureaus and other specialists.

The Review says the tentative recommendations had appeared in the March 1923 *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, pp. 656–657.

### 2. The 1923 actor problem — high

The same contemporary reprint records seven recommendations:

1. standardize a payroll schedule around total wage earners and wages paid;
2. make BLS the coordinating center for Federal and State employment data;
3. extend participation to additional States;
4. speed reporting and publication so information retains current significance;
5. consider geographic classification;
6. study how transfers of wage earners between kinds of work affect employment statistics, asking explicitly whether manufacturing employment is a fair index;
7. standardize industrial classification so data from different bureaus can be combined.

For recommendation 6, the committee proposed extending periodic collection beyond manufacturing to mining/quarrying, transportation/communications, construction, wholesale trade, retail trade, logging, and agriculture. The text explicitly links feasibility/significance of these series to "control of the business cycle."

The committee's five outstanding problems were agriculture, adequate industrial coverage, geographic representation, uniform industrial classification, and—called the most important—more prompt reporting and publication.

### 3. Relation to the 1921 unemployment/business-cycle program — high for adjacency; medium-high for transmission

Immediately after the committee item, the May 1923 Review summarizes *Business Cycles and Unemployment*, the report of the committee appointed by the President's Conference on Unemployment. It argues that stabilization requires fundamental facts on production, transportation, and employment to be available in current and comparable form and recommends expansion of Commerce and Labor statistical services.

This is strong contemporary evidence that employment measurement was being framed as infrastructure for business-cycle diagnosis/control. It does **not by itself** prove that every ASA recommendation was directly dictated by the President's Conference.

Later BLS institutional history reconstructs the transmission more strongly: Hoover's post-conference committee asked the Russell Sage Foundation to study the adequacy of employment statistics; ASA assumed sponsorship and appointed the Van Kleeck committee; the full results appeared in 1926. This is high-quality later reconstruction, but the original commission/authorization correspondence remains a source debt.

BLS institutional history:
https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/first-hundred-years-bureau-labor-statistics-5181/fulltext

### 4. 1926 consolidation — high bibliographic identity; medium-high for the exact formulation shift

Ralph G. Hurlin and William A. Berridge, eds., *Employment Statistics for the United States* (Russell Sage Foundation, 1926):
https://www.russellsage.org/publications/book/employment-statistics-united-states

Russell Sage describes the volume as a national collection plan and handbook of methods recommended by the ASA Committee on Governmental Labor Statistics, presenting the committee's consensus on problems involved in collecting and publishing adequate employment statistics.

The 1927 BLS *Handbook of Labor Statistics* explicitly says its account is taken from this 1926 report and distinguishes three observation sources:

1. counts/estimates of persons unemployed;
2. employment-office registrations;
3. periodic payroll counts of persons employed.

It identifies payrolls as the most feasible current source of employment statistics, while noting limitations of direct unemployment data and employment-office statistics.

FRASER:
https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/handbook-labor-statistics-4025/handbook-labor-statistics-1924-1926-493213/fulltext

Later BLS institutional history reports that the 1926 volume recommended national BLS coordination, nonmanufacturing expansion, hours and worker-characteristic data, and careful sampling, while explicitly acknowledging that employment statistics did not measure unemployment (for example, they omit people who never obtained employment).

## Actor / reconstruction / present-day separation

### Historical actors

**1922–23 committee problem:** How can fragmented payroll/employment observations be standardized, coordinated, accelerated, geographically broadened, and industrially broadened enough to yield a fair current picture? Worker transfer across sectors is already recognized as a threat to treating manufacturing employment as a national index.

**1923 business-cycle problem:** Stabilization and unemployment prevention require current and comparable facts. Employment statistics are part of an information infrastructure for diagnosis and action.

**1926 mature program:** The committee has developed a national collection plan and method handbook. By this stage the available evidence supports a sharper separation between employed-person/payroll observation and direct unemployment observation.

### Later reconstruction

The 1985 BLS institutional history reconstructs a lineage from the 1921 President's Conference through Hoover's follow-up, Russell Sage, ASA sponsorship, the Van Kleeck committee, and the 1926 report. This is useful institutional genealogy but should not be substituted for the original commissioning correspondence.

### Present-day analytical reconstruction

The following are repository analytical labels, not actor vocabulary:

- `measurement-object ladder`
- `observation regime`
- `solution-program activation`
- `proxy-breakdown transition`

The strongest candidate added by this packet is:

> **proxy-breakdown transition**: an existing proxy becomes problematized when actors identify a movement or population that the observation frame cannot see.

In 1923:

    manufacturing employment
        ↓ proxy for
    broader employment conditions

breaks when:

    workers transfer across sectors
        ↓
    manufacturing-only frame loses them

This is not yet the same as the 1928–29 problem that establishment employment shrinkage cannot directly identify unemployed persons.

## 1923 → 1926 transformation hypothesis

The evidence now supports continuity of the **measurement program**, but problem identity should not yet be frozen.

A provisional transformation is:

    1923
    comparability + timeliness + coordination
    + coverage + sector-transfer bias
            ↓
    1926
    national collection architecture
    + explicit source taxonomy
    + stronger employment/unemployment
      observation-object separation

Evidence for the 1923 side is direct and high. Evidence for the exact 1926 formulation shift is medium-high because the Russell Sage publication identity is direct and the 1927 BLS handbook reproduces the 1926 report, but a page-level audit of the 1926 volume itself is still required before claiming a precise textual diff.

## Why this matters for Problem History

This case blocks two hindsight errors.

### 1. Crisis-origin error

Do not write:

    1928 unemployment controversy
    → invention of employment statistics reform

The reform program was organized by 1922 and already technically articulated in 1923.

A safer relation is:

    1922–26 measurement program
            ↓
    1928 renewed unemployment pressure
            ↓
    acceleration / funding /
    broader institutionalization

### 2. Solution-continuity error

A continuing committee or solution program does not prove that the problem remained identical. A program may survive while its target problem changes from comparability/timeliness, to proxy validity, to person-level unemployment, to policy administration.

Therefore:

    solution continuity
    !=
    problem identity

## Hindsight risks

- Do not call the 1922–23 committee a "technological unemployment" committee. Its stated remit was employment measurement.
- Do not treat "employment" and "unemployment" as interchangeable historical objects.
- Do not infer that the 1923 manufacturing-index warning already contained the later household labor-force concept.
- Do not infer a direct President's Conference → ASA instruction chain solely from later BLS institutional history; recover the commissioning correspondence if possible.
- Do not infer that the 1928 expansion was caused only by machinery-displacement debate. Business-cycle stabilization and a pre-existing statistical reform program are independently documented.
