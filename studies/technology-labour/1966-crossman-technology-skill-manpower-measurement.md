# 1966 Crossman et al.: technological change as a skill-profile and manpower-measurement problem

Status: **M2 source packet / OMPER transition follow-up / not a frozen Problem Episode**

Pilot: technology–labour

This packet closes one of the explicit source debts in `1965-1966-omper-automation-disappearance-test.md`: direct reading of the October 1966 Crossman/Laner/Davis/Caplan study on technological change, job content, and skill profiles.

The main result is stronger than the earlier disappearance test. The report does not merely prove that `automation` / `technological change` remained researchable after the Office of Manpower, Automation, and Training (OMAT) was replaced by the Office of Manpower Policy, Evaluation, and Research (OMPER). It recovers a distinct actor-side research problem inside the 1966 federal manpower field:

```text
technological change
        ↓
changes unit labour requirements and process-level skill profiles
        ↓
may create displacement, mobility/retraining requirements,
and possibly a structural unemployment mechanism
        ↓
therefore the state needs quantitative methods that can distinguish
technology effects from demand, product, and inter-firm differences
        ↓
for manpower forecasting and policy
```

The study also supplies a direct methodological genealogy. Its authors say their method is based broadly on Bureau of Labor Statistics `direct productivity` studies of the late 1940s and early 1950s. The inherited measurement technique is repurposed for a different policy problem: from factory/productivity comparison toward skill-profile analysis, manpower forecasting, and adjustment under the Manpower Development and Training Act.

That yields a useful Problem History fixture:

```text
methodological inheritance
!=
problem identity

same measurement technique
can be repurposed into a different historical problem
```

## 1. Primary source and source control

Edward R. F. W. Crossman, Stephen Laner, Louis E. Davis, and Stanley H. Caplan,
**Evaluation of Changes in Skill-Profile and Job-Content Due to Technological Change: Methodology and Pilot Results from the Banking, Steel and Aerospace Industries**,
U.S. Department of Labor Contract No. 81-04-05,
University of California, Berkeley,
October 1966.

ERIC record:

- <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED015326>
- full scan: <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED015326.pdf>

The title page directly states that the report:

- was prepared under a U.S. Department of Labor contract;
- was issued under the Manpower Development and Training Act of 1962;
- was submitted to the **Director, Office of Manpower Policy, Evaluation and Research**;
- was completed at Berkeley in October 1966.

### Chronology boundary

The report's personnel section shows that the research project itself began before OMPER existed: major project staff dates run from **September 1964** into 1966. OMPER was established in December 1965.

Therefore:

```text
October 1966 submission to OMPER
!=
OMPER originated the project
```

The study is excellent evidence for **post-transition institutional receipt, continuation, and use of technological-change research**, but not proof that OMPER initiated it from scratch.

### Evidence strength

**High / direct / page-image checked primary research report.**

## 2. The actor problem is not simply `does automation cause unemployment?`

The report's Chapter 1 is explicitly titled **“Introduction and Statement of Problem.”**

Its opening formulation is already more differentiated than a generic automation anxiety.

The authors begin from several propositions:

1. technological change is continuous and contributes to long-run productivity and income growth;
2. in aggregate, productivity growth can be offset by growth in demand;
3. this aggregate balance does not imply sectoral balance;
4. individual plants, industries, occupations, and regions can experience displacement while other units experience vacancies;
5. workers may therefore have to move between jobs, industries, occupations, and places;
6. technological change can require relearning and adaptation, and the costs of that adjustment have historically been left largely to spontaneous processes.

The report then places the new federal manpower policy inside this adjustment problem. Under the MDTA, government is attempting to facilitate adjustment to technological change by training workers who are actually or prospectively unemployed for new jobs.

A safe actor-side reconstruction is therefore:

> How does technological change alter the distribution of labour requirements and skills across processes, and how can policy identify resulting displacement, adjustment, and training needs before relying on spontaneous mobility alone?

This is distinct from:

```text
technology independently determines the aggregate number of jobs
```

and also from:

```text
automation merely creates local temporary displacement
```

The report is explicitly interested in the quantitative relation between technology, process labour requirements, skill composition, mobility, and employment response.

### Evidence strength

**High / direct actor problem statement, printed Chapter 1.**

---

## 3. `Structural unemployment` is formulated as a testable mechanism, not a loose label

The report identifies a specific possible structural-unemployment mechanism.

If the new jobs created in a changing economy require substantially higher education or skill than the jobs being destroyed, the economy could simultaneously contain:

```text
vacancies / shortages for qualified workers
+
unemployment among workers who do not meet the new requirements
```

In that case, simply increasing aggregate demand would not by itself solve the mismatch; large-scale re-education or generational replacement could become necessary.

The authors then treat this claim as an empirical question rather than as an axiom. They note prominent arguments that automation and technological development raise skill requirements, but argue that aggregate education and occupational statistics do not adequately isolate the causal effect of technology.

This matters for Problem History because the research object becomes partly a **measurement problem**:

```text
claim:
advanced technology raises required skill
        ↓
problem:
can technology's effect be isolated from
worker supply, management selection, product mix,
firm differences, and broader social change?
```

### Hindsight boundary

`Structural unemployment` here must not be silently equated with every later macroeconomic use of that expression. In this report it is tied to a relatively specific skill-requirement / worker-qualification mismatch hypothesis.

## 4. A temporally layered formulation exists inside the same 1966 report

The Chapter 1 text was written in **March 1966**. In the main text, the authors observe that falling unemployment appeared consistent with the possibility that sufficiently high aggregate demand could absorb less-qualified workers even if technology changed occupational structure.

A later footnote, added with experience through **September 1966**, qualifies that optimism. It says that a structural-unemployment problem now appears to exist because unemployment had not fallen much below roughly 4 percent even while labour shortages were visible.

This is valuable source criticism.

The October publication is not one perfectly synchronized actor statement. It contains at least two temporal layers:

```text
March 1966 formulation
        ↓
September 1966 qualification
        ↓
October 1966 final submission
```

Therefore:

```text
publication date
!=
single formulation date
```

and:

```text
one report
can preserve an unresolved revision in problem diagnosis
```

This should be retained rather than harmonized away.

## 5. The method: technological change becomes a matched-process causal-measurement object

The study's principal methodological contribution is a quantitative technique for measuring changes in skill profile and labour requirements associated with selected technological changes.

The method compares matched old and new processes/products and estimates:

- direct labour hours per unit of output;
- the distribution of those hours across skill levels;
- skill values derived from job evaluation;
- changes in the resulting `skill-profile`;
- differences across comparable firms in an attempt to separate technology from establishment-specific conditions.

The project uses case material from:

- banking;
- steel;
- aerospace / numerical-control machining.

This transforms the problem from a broad public question—`what does automation do to workers?`—into an observational design:

```text
hold product/process comparison as stable as possible
        ↓
measure direct labour per unit
        ↓
locate labour-hour change by skill level
        ↓
replicate across establishments
        ↓
infer which changes plausibly belong to technology
rather than to unrelated firm differences
```

### Evidence strength

**High / direct methodological statement and pilot results.**

---

## 6. Direct methodological genealogy: BLS productivity studies → Crossman skill-profile method

The authors explicitly say their technique is based broadly on the Bureau of Labor Statistics' **direct productivity** studies of the late 1940s and early 1950s.

They further describe a substantial BLS series in roughly 1951–1955 that gathered process-level labour requirements and produced dozens of factory/productivity case studies.

The earlier BLS work was not originally identical to the 1966 manpower problem. The report describes those studies primarily as `direct productivity` work, much of it useful for comparison and technical/productivity analysis. Crossman et al. repurpose the approach to ask about:

- skill-profile change;
- manpower requirements;
- employment forecasting;
- possible structural unemployment;
- training and adjustment policy.

This is one of the cleanest direct genealogies in the technology–labour pilot because the later actors themselves identify the inherited research technique.

But the proper relation is:

```text
BLS direct-productivity method
        ↓ actor-recognized methodological inheritance
Crossman 1966 skill-profile / manpower method
```

not:

```text
same method
therefore same problem
```

### Method fixture

```text
research-infrastructure continuity
can coexist with problem repurposing
```

This should later be compared with the repository's 1930s BLS/NBER/WPA cases, but no 1937→1950s direct transmission edge is asserted here.

## 7. Findings: technological change does not produce one uniform skill trajectory

The pilot results show substantial reductions in direct labour hours per unit in newer processes, but they do **not** support a single universal skill-upgrading story.

Across the cases:

- newer processes commonly use substantially fewer direct labour hours per unit;
- the distribution of reductions across skill levels differs by process;
- in some banking and steel cases, much of the reduction falls in middle skill ranges;
- in the aerospace numerical-control case, substantial reductions can occur among relatively high-skill direct production work;
- mean skill changes are often much smaller than a simple `automation raises skill` thesis would predict.

The report therefore weakens an undifferentiated causal story:

```text
automation
→ higher skill requirements everywhere
```

and replaces it with a process-specific research problem:

```text
which technology
in which process
changes which labour hours
at which skill levels?
```

### Hindsight boundary

This should not be retroactively rewritten as the later `deskilling debate`. Later scholarship can locate Crossman in that historiography, but the report's own problem is framed through skill-profile measurement, manpower prediction, and technological-change adjustment.

---

## 8. Generalized policy conclusion: industrial employment may become weakly responsive to demand

The report's concluding policy section is especially important because it prevents a simple equation between this study and the National Commission's 1966 collective formulation.

The authors call their broad policy implications largely **negative**: the case studies do not indicate a generalized impending shortage of high-level operating skills that would require a special manpower adjustment programme of the kind often predicted from automation rhetoric.

At the same time, they advance another hypothesis. In the mature processes studied, large technological reductions in labour requirements may have pushed industrial production toward what the authors call a **`labor-static`** condition: expanding aggregate demand may increase output without producing proportionate increases in direct industrial employment.

Their policy implication is not `aggregate demand does not matter`. It is narrower:

> If active manpower policy seeks near-full employment for an expanding labour force, attempts to create much additional employment by stimulating these industrial sectors may yield relatively little; policy may need to look toward other sectors of the economy for employment growth.

This formulation combines:

```text
aggregate demand can absorb labour economy-wide
+
particular industries may have weak employment response to demand
because technology has reduced unit labour requirements
```

That is a distinct problem structure.

## 9. Relation to the 1966 National Commission: proximity without identity

The repository's Commission packet establishes a collective formulation in *Technology and the American Economy*:

```text
aggregate unemployment
→ strongly conditioned by aggregate demand / output growth

particular displacement
→ strongly shaped by technological change
```

Crossman et al. occupy the same broad federal problem field but should not be collapsed into Commission doctrine.

The Crossman report adds a process/sector-level question:

```text
technology
→ lower unit labour requirements
→ changes the employment elasticity of particular industries
→ demand growth may raise output with limited direct industrial job growth
```

The relation is therefore best kept provisionally as:

- **contemporary federal proximity:** strong;
- **shared concern with demand vs technology:** strong;
- **same institutional actor:** false;
- **Commission authorship/influence:** unproved;
- **same problem formulation:** too strong;
- **best current researcher description:** complementary / partly competing formulations inside the 1966 federal technology–labour field.

Do not draw a direct Crossman → Commission edge without Commission staff papers, citations, correspondence, or draft evidence.

## 10. OMPER transition: what this source now proves and what it does not

The title page makes the post-transition institutional recipient explicit: the final report is submitted to the Director of OMPER in October 1966.

Therefore this source strongly supports the earlier disappearance test:

```text
Dec 1965:
`Automation` disappears from office title

Oct 1966:
OMPER receives a major federal contract report explicitly devoted to
technological change, skill profiles, job content, and manpower effects
```

The problem has plainly not become unaskable.

But the project personnel chronology prevents the opposite error:

```text
OMPER receives final report in 1966
!=
OMPER originated research after its creation
```

The project began in the OMAT period. The transition is therefore evidence for **institutional inheritance and continuation of a research stream**, not clean post-transition origin.

## 12. Hindsight risks

### 12.1 `Structural unemployment` is not a timeless category

The report's mechanism must be reconstructed from its own skill/qualification argument rather than imported from later textbooks.

### 12.2 `Skill profile` is not automatically `deskilling`

Later labour-process literature can treat these results as prior evidence, but that retrospective debate is not the same historical problem as Crossman et al.'s manpower-measurement task.

### 12.3 Federal contract != federal consensus

The report is a Department of Labor contract study submitted to OMPER. Its authors' `labor-static` and policy conclusions should not be attributed to the Secretary, OMPER leadership, the Manpower Administration, or the National Commission without separate evidence.

### 12.4 OMPER recipient != OMPER originator

The project began in 1964, before OMPER existed.

### 12.5 Method inheritance != problem inheritance

Crossman explicitly inherits a BLS measurement tradition, but uses it for a broadened skill/manpower/forecasting problem.

### 12.6 Sectoral weak job response != post-work thesis

The `labor-static` hypothesis concerns selected industrial sectors and the employment response of those sectors to demand. It is not the Triple Revolution's claim that the social jobs–income order is approaching obsolescence.

### 12.7 Publication date != one synchronized actor formulation

The March text and September footnote preserve a changing diagnosis inside the October report.

### 12.8 Direct production workforce != total labour effect

The authors themselves warn that excluding indirect labour can distort the apparent effect of technology. This is a built-in evidence limitation, not a later criticism that can be omitted.

## 14. What remains uncertain

1. **Contract genealogy.** The personnel chronology proves pre-OMPER project activity, but the original solicitation, award date, OMAT sponsorship chain, and handoff to OMPER still need contract files or correspondence.
2. **OMPER reception.** We do not yet know how OMPER leadership evaluated the report's `labor-static`, skill, or structural-unemployment conclusions.
3. **Commission relation.** No direct Crossman ↔ National Commission transmission edge is proved.
4. **BLS method genealogy before the 1950s.** The authors explicitly identify BLS direct-productivity work as the methodological parent, but this packet does not establish a direct line back to the 1930s BLS/NBER/WPA research programme.
5. **Indirect labour.** The report explicitly identifies this as a major limitation; total employment effects may differ from direct-production results.
6. **External validity.** Banking, steel, and aerospace pilot cases cannot be treated as the entire U.S. economy.

## Bottom line

The direct audit changes the evidential status of the October 1966 Crossman study from a bibliographic persistence marker into a substantive Problem History source.

It shows that, inside the post-OMAT federal manpower apparatus, technological change was being reformulated as a quantitative problem of:

```text
unit labour requirements
+
skill distribution
+
worker mobility / retraining
+
structural mismatch
+
industry employment response to demand
+
manpower forecasting
```

The report inherits measurement techniques from earlier BLS productivity work while repurposing them into a different manpower-policy question. It also sits near—but is not identical to—the 1966 National Commission's aggregate-demand / particular-displacement formulation.

The strongest current conclusion is therefore not simply that the automation problem `persisted` after the word disappeared from the office title. It is that **the same technological difficulty remained institutionally live while its research form, measurement apparatus, policy role, and relation to unemployment were being actively re-specified.**
