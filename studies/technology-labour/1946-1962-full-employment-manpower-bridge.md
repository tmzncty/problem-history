# 1946–1962: from full-employment responsibility to automation and manpower planning

Status: **M2 bridge packet / relation evidence, not yet a standalone frozen Problem Episode**

Pilot: technology–labour

Purpose: fill the repository's largest remaining gap between the 1937 technological-unemployment research programme and the 1964–1966 automation/cybernation split without presuming a continuous problem lineage.

---

## 1. Why this packet exists

The current repository already has strong evidence for:

- a 1928–1937 U.S. `technological unemployment` field that becomes increasingly difficult to isolate from output, demand, labour-force growth, worker trajectories, and indirect displacement;
- a 1932–1937 NBER/WPA/BLS/National Resources Committee research-infrastructure bridge;
- a 1964–1966 split field in which the Triple Revolution, Johnson/statutory adjustment policy, Bell/Solow-style aggregate-demand arguments, and the National Commission do not share one formulation;
- a 1966 Commission distinction between aggregate unemployment and the displacement of particular workers.

What remained unproved was the middle:

```text
1937 research programme
        ?
1940s–1950s institutions
        ?
1962 manpower / automation policy
        ?
1964–1966 National Commission
```

The temptation is to infer continuity because later sources sound structurally similar. This packet instead asks what actually changed in the governable object, responsible actors, causal assumptions, and answer space.

The strongest result is not a direct `1937 -> 1966` edge. It is a documented postwar sequence in which **automation is repeatedly absorbed into broader employment, purchasing-power, growth, mobility, skill, and manpower-planning frameworks**.

A particularly important historical transformation is:

```text
"technological unemployment"
        ->
"automation" inside full-employment / purchasing-power policy
        ->
"automation and technological change" inside manpower requirements,
forecasting, mobility, training, counseling, and placement
```

The last step changes the object that government is expected to observe and act upon. It is not merely a new policy answer to an unchanged unemployment question.

## 3. 1946: a new statutory macro-policy frame, not yet an automation problem

### 3.1 Actor-explicit formulation

Section 2 of the Employment Act declares a continuing federal policy and responsibility, in cooperation with industry, agriculture, labor, and state/local government, to create conditions for useful employment and to promote:

- maximum employment;
- production;
- purchasing power.

It also establishes a national goal of full opportunities for useful paid employment.

### 3.2 What can safely be inferred

This statute creates a durable **policy responsibility frame** within which later technological-change questions can be asked.

It changes the governmental problem environment in at least three ways:

1. `employment` is not only a private or sectoral adjustment matter;
2. `production` and `purchasing power` become explicitly coupled to employment responsibility;
3. federal institutions acquire a continuing mandate to study and report on these macroeconomic objects.

### 3.3 What cannot be claimed

The Act is **not** evidence that Congress in 1946 was asking the later question "what should government do about automation?"

Do not write:

```text
Employment Act 1946 = federal automation policy
```

The later link matters because the 1955 Joint Economic Committee explicitly invokes the Act as the statutory reason it is studying automation.

### 3.4 Relation to 1937

Current relation:

```text
1937 NRC/WPA research programme -> 1946 Employment Act
relation = undetermined
```

There is a structural resemblance: both refuse to reduce unemployment to a single machine-displacement cause and both foreground wider economic conditions. That is not transmission evidence.

No direct institutional, personnel, citation, or drafting chain from the 1937 programme to the 1946 statute has yet been established.

## 4. 1955: Congress inserts `automation` into the Employment Act's employment-production-purchasing-power field

### 4.1 Direct institutional genealogy

The 1955 Joint Economic Committee report opens by saying that, because the Committee is charged under section 5(b) of the Employment Act of 1946 with continuing studies relating to **employment, production, and purchasing power**, it directed its Subcommittee on Economic Stabilization to study the effect of so-called automation on long-run employment and investment.

Source location:

- 1955 report, printed introduction p. 1 / PDF p. 6.

This is a high-confidence direct edge:

```text
Employment Act 1946
        -> statutory study mandate
JEC automation inquiry 1955
```

The edge establishes institutional embedding. It does **not** prove that the 1946 and 1955 historical problems are identical.

### 4.2 The object `automation` is actor-explicitly unstable

The committee says the term is new, not yet in standard dictionaries, and means widely varying things to different people. It therefore uses the term broadly to cover automatic/electronic processes, rapid technological advance, and improved know-how.

It simultaneously entertains two incompatible-looking ways of situating novelty:

- automation can be understood as an old concept or extension of mechanization;
- postwar electronics, atomic science, computing, feedback, and accelerated research may make the coming industrial age more than simply "more of the same."

Source locations:

- printed p. 3 / PDF p. 8;
- printed p. 4 / PDF p. 9.

This is direct actor evidence that `automation` is not a stable technological object from which a stable social problem can simply be read off.

### 4.3 What the committee actually asks about

The report states that the hearings sought information on five broad consequences:

1. possible/probable personnel displacement;
2. shifts in the distribution of mass purchasing power;
3. distribution of productivity gains;
4. effects on business structure;
5. effects on the volume and regularity of private investment.

Source location:

- printed p. 2 / PDF p. 7.

Thus the committee's actor-explicit object is already larger than:

```text
machine -> worker loses job
```

The problem is embedded in a macro-institutional system:

```text
automation / technological change
        x employment
        x purchasing power
        x distribution of productivity gains
        x business structure
        x investment
```

### 4.4 Direct displacement and aggregate full employment are separated

The committee says that maintaining reasonably full employment involves much more than finding new positions for workers directly displaced by automation or other causes. It points to labour-force growth and warns that automation could matter even when no currently employed worker is visibly fired, if it reduces opportunities for new entrants.

Source location:

- printed p. 4 / PDF p. 9.

This is a significant predecessor **structure** to the 1966 Commission's aggregate-versus-particular distinction.

Current relation must remain:

```text
1955 JEC aggregate/direct distinction
        ?
1966 Commission aggregate/particular distinction

relation = undetermined
```

The resemblance is strong. Direct transmission still needs committee records, citations, personnel, staff continuity, or other documentary links.

### 4.5 Worker protection is explicitly distinguished from opposition to technology

The 1955 report says no witness, including organized labor representatives, opposed automation or technological advance. It rejects the inference that labor concern about job loss, skill displacement, retraining, relocation, or severance is evidence of hostility to progress.

Source location:

- printed pp. 4–5 / PDF pp. 9–10.

This yields a reusable failure-case rule:

```text
worker-protection demand != anti-technology formulation
```

### 4.6 Responsibility and answer space

The report's recommendations distribute responsibility across macro policy, government services, business, and labor:

- maintain a healthy, dynamic, prosperous economy so displaced workers can find demand elsewhere;
- treat the Employment Act of 1946 as the existing broad statutory framework rather than immediately enact another general economic law;
- education;
- action on local unemployment pockets regardless of cause;
- better employment services and labour mobility;
- better productivity and occupational-shift statistics;
- management acceptance of human costs of displacement/retraining;
- timing technological change with attention to business conditions and employment needs;
- continuing/recurrent public study.

Source locations:

- printed pp. 12–13 / PDF pp. 17–18.

This is not a simple compensation doctrine. The answer space already combines:

```text
aggregate demand / prosperity
+ information
+ mobility
+ education
+ firm-level adjustment
+ shared displacement costs
+ continuing observation
```

## 5. 1962 President's Advisory Committee: automation is essential, but unemployment cannot be reduced to automation

The President's Advisory Committee on Labor-Management Policy provides an unusually valuable intermediate node because its report contains both a collective formulation and explicit internal disagreement.

### 5.1 Collective propositions

In the report reproduced in the Congressional Record, the committee states three central propositions:

1. automation and technological progress are essential to welfare, economic strength, and defense;
2. progress should not require sacrifice of human values or inequitable individual costs;
3. maximum technological development plus safeguards against individual economic injury requires combined private and governmental action.

Source location:

- Congressional Record, January 23, 1962, printed p. 822 / PDF p. 15.

This is already a different presupposition from an anti-machine or post-work problem:

```text
technological advance is necessary
AND
individual displacement injury is real
```

The policy problem becomes how to hold both commitments simultaneously.

### 5.2 The committee actor-explicitly refuses a one-cause automation model

The report says automation and technological change cannot be dealt with apart from two broader subjects:

- productivity;
- unemployment.

It acknowledges technological displacement but says available data cannot isolate the share of unemployment caused by automation/technology. It lists additional causes or interacting conditions including:

- the 1960–61 recession;
- rapid labour-force growth;
- chronic unemployment in distressed areas;
- foreign competition;
- changing consumption patterns;
- mismatch between job requirements and worker qualifications;
- discrimination by age, sex, race, and creed;
- movement out of agriculture.

Source location:

- Congressional Record, printed p. 823 / PDF p. 16.

This is a direct actor-side change in causal ontology:

```text
NOT:
automation -> unemployment

BUT:
technological displacement
x aggregate growth
x labour-force growth
x geography
x trade
x demand
x skills
x discrimination
x mobility
```

### 5.3 It also refuses to treat continuing unemployment as an inevitable price of automation

The committee argues that displacement problems are not a necessary price of progress but can result from failing to anticipate and provide for them. Its preferred answer space includes:

- stronger economic growth;
- government collection and dissemination of information about future job opportunities;
- education;
- employer lead time and advance reporting;
- cooperation with employee representatives and public employment services;
- timing technological change to use expansion and normal attrition;
- retraining with income support;
- unemployment compensation;
- relocation/mobility support;
- preservation/portability of job equities;
- anti-discrimination measures;
- possible fiscal/monetary measures if an intolerable manpower surplus remains.

Source locations:

- Congressional Record, printed pp. 823–824 / PDF pp. 16–17.

The object `manpower` is already appearing inside the automation field before the MDTA statutory formulation is examined.

## 6. 1962: one committee, competing formulations

The 1962 advisory report is also a useful precursor to the repository's 1966 `same institution != one problem formulation` finding.

### 6.1 Labor members retain shorter-hours as a conditional answer

A footnote records Meany, Dubinsky, Harrison, Reuther, and Keenan wanting a stronger formulation: full employment and maximum output are preferable, but if unemployment is not substantially reduced, a shorter work period through bargaining and/or law should be considered.

McDonald, Reuther, and Keenan further stress that merely preferring full employment at forty hours is not evidence that full employment has been achieved.

Source location:

- Congressional Record, printed p. 824 / PDF p. 17.

This keeps an answer path open that the collective text attempts to subordinate:

```text
preferred:
full employment + growth

conditional labor answer:
if full employment fails -> shorter work time / work sharing remains live
```

### 6.2 Arthur F. Burns disputes the report's causal emphasis

Burns says the report does not adequately distinguish causes of unemployment and risks conveying that technological advance is a major cause of recent unemployment. He argues that remedies should be linked more precisely to cyclical, seasonal, trade-related, local, and other causes.

Source location:

- Congressional Record, printed pp. 824–825 / PDF pp. 17–18.

### 6.3 Henry Ford II disputes an even deeper presupposition

Ford argues that automation/technological advance are not, by themselves, significant causes of aggregate unemployment; he treats technological progress as long-running rather than an accelerating historical rupture, while still conceding that it displaces particular workers.

Source location:

- Congressional Record, printed p. 825 / PDF p. 18.

This gives a very strong mid-bridge split:

```text
collective committee:
necessary technology + real displacement
+ growth / adjustment / public-private safeguards

labor qualification:
full employment first;
shorter hours remains conditional if it fails

Burns:
causal decomposition is inadequate;
do not overattribute unemployment to technology

Ford:
particular displacement is real
but automation is not a major aggregate-unemployment cause
```

That last distinction is strikingly similar to the 1966 Commission's final causal decomposition, but similarity is not authorship or transmission.

### 6.4 Method consequence

The repository should not model 1962 as a single homogeneous `government view`.

This packet supplies an earlier independent case for:

```text
institutional consensus report
+ attached qualifications/dissents
= one institutional episode with competing formulations
```

It strengthens the case that the data model needs `competing_formulations` within an institutional actor container.

## 7. MDTA 1962: the governable object migrates from `automation unemployment` toward `manpower`

Public Law 87-415 is the strongest evidence in this packet for genuine problem transformation.

### 7.1 Congress does not isolate automation

The Act's findings place automation and technological developments beside:

- foreign competition;
- relocation of industry;
- shifts in market demand;
- other structural economic changes;
- rapid growth of the labour force.

It says many workers' skills have become obsolete and that government leadership is needed so the benefits of automation do not become burdens of widespread unemployment.

Source location:

- 76 Stat. 24 / PDF p. 1.

### 7.2 The statutory purpose defines a new information-and-capacity problem

The Act requires the federal government to:

- appraise national manpower requirements and resources;
- develop information and methods for persistent unemployment, including that associated with automation/technological change;
- evaluate technology's impact on human resources;
- develop methods to detect impacts **in advance**;
- study employer/union practices that impede or facilitate worker mobility;
- appraise national manpower-development efforts;
- disseminate information about manpower requirements/development/utilization;
- compile occupational outlook, job-opportunity, skill-supply, and employment-trend information;
- build diversified training programmes, including on-the-job training.

Source locations:

- 76 Stat. 24–25 / PDF pp. 1–2, secs. 102–103 and 201.

The historical object has therefore moved.

Compare:

```text
narrower machine-unemployment object:
Who lost a job because a machine was installed?

manpower object:
What skills will the economy require?
Where are workers and jobs located?
Which qualifications are becoming obsolete?
Can impact be detected before displacement?
What prevents mobility?
How should training / counseling / placement capacity be organized?
```

This is not merely `retraining` added to an unchanged question. A new administrative object—**manpower requirements, resources, utilization, mobility, and forecastability**—has become governable.

### 7.3 Kennedy explicitly places MDTA in an Employment Act lineage

On March 15, 1962, Kennedy called MDTA perhaps the most significant employment legislation since the Employment Act of 1946. He framed it around workers denied employment because they lacked skills required by a changing economy and emphasized anticipation of future employment needs.

Source:

- Kennedy signing statement, March 15, 1962.

This is actor-recognized institutional genealogy:

```text
Employment Act 1946
        -> Kennedy explicitly identifies lineage
MDTA 1962
```

It still does not prove that `automation` was the same problem in both statutes. The 1946 law did not formulate an automation question.

## 8. Documentary afterlife: MDTA creates an observable research programme

The 1966 BLS bulletin provides unusually strong evidence that the 1962 manpower formulation did not remain rhetorical.

Its preface says MDTA requires the Secretary of Labor to evaluate the effects, benefits, and problems of automation/technological progress and to develop methods for detecting impacts in advance. It then says the bulletin helps satisfy that requirement by assessing likely effects on:

- employment patterns;
- occupations;
- labor-management adjustment.

It also explicitly says the 1966 bulletin **extends and updates** _Technological Trends in 36 Major American Industries_, issued in 1964 for the President's Advisory Committee on Labor-Management Policy.

Source location:

- BLS Bulletin 1474 (February 1966), preface, pp. iii–iv in front matter.

This supplies a direct documentary programme chain:

```text
MDTA 1962 statutory research mandate
        ->
BLS forecasting / technological-studies programme
        ->
1964 industry report for President's Advisory Committee
        ->
1966 updated BLS bulletin
```

The bulletin also explains the rationale in problem terms: advance knowledge of impending technological change could let companies, unions, and government formulate retraining/readjustment policy before disruption. It treats technology adoption as dependent on nontechnical conditions including market prospects, competition, investment, management, union attitudes, and regulation.

This is important because it operationalizes the manpower shift:

```text
technological change becomes something to forecast
in relation to occupations, skills, markets, and adjustment institutions
```

### Boundary

The BLS programme proves implementation of MDTA-style research responsibility. It does **not** by itself prove that the independent National Commission of 1964–1966 copied BLS's formulation.

## 10. IDENTITY-CHECK preparation

### Pair A: 1946 Employment Act vs 1955 JEC automation inquiry

**Continuity evidence**

- direct statutory institutional edge: JEC explicitly says Employment Act mandate is why it studies automation;
- shared objects: employment, production, purchasing power;
- 1955 recommendations explicitly say the existing Employment Act is the broad economic framework needed.

**Discontinuity evidence**

- 1946 law contains no actor-explicit automation problem;
- 1955 introduces a contested new technical vocabulary and asks about displacement, distribution of productivity gains, investment, and firm responsibility.

**Provisional relation**

- not enough for problem identity;
- `transformed_successor` or `reformulated` only as a candidate within the policy field;
- institutional continuity = **high**; problem continuity = **undetermined**.

### Pair B: 1955 JEC vs 1962 Advisory Committee

**Continuity evidence**

- both affirm technological progress while recognizing displacement;
- both separate worker-level injury from the whole employment problem;
- both include growth, retraining, mobility, information, and shared public/private responsibility;
- both reject `labor concern = hostility to technology` style simplifications.

**Discontinuity evidence**

- 1962 embeds automation in a much denser unemployment-causation matrix: recession, labor-force growth, distressed regions, foreign competition, skills mismatch, discrimination;
- 1962 expands the answer space into explicit anticipation/lead time, portability/equities, and conditional shorter-hours debate;
- no direct citation/drafting edge has yet been demonstrated.

**Provisional relation**

- `reformulated?` / `undetermined`.

### Pair C: 1962 Advisory Committee vs MDTA

**Continuity evidence**

- same administration and historical moment;
- technology is desirable but displacement/hardship requires anticipatory adjustment;
- shared emphasis on skills, retraining, job information, mobility, public/private responsibility.

**Discontinuity evidence**

- committee report remains a broad labor-management policy consensus with contested macro causation;
- statute creates a formal administrative object called manpower requirements/development/utilization and mandates measurement, forecasting, training, counseling, and placement;
- chronological proximity does not prove the report caused the statute.

**Provisional relation**

- `transformed_successor?` at problem-structure level;
- direct causal relation = **undetermined** pending drafting/administrative records.

### Pair D: MDTA 1962 vs National Commission 1966

**Continuity evidence**

- both presuppose continued technological progress rather than stopping it;
- both distinguish individual adjustment from aggregate economic conditions;
- both make information, education/training, mobility, and policy planning legitimate answer domains.

**Discontinuity evidence**

- MDTA's central administrative object is manpower capacity and skill/mobility mismatch;
- 1966 Commission is a broader national appraisal of technology/economic progress and explicitly separates aggregate unemployment from particular displacement while preserving multiple internal formulations;
- no direct drafting or citation chain is established here.

**Provisional relation**

- `reformulated?` / `transformed_successor?` / `undetermined`.

## 11. What genuinely changed in the problem

The safest current synthesis is not that the United States asked one uninterrupted question—"will machines take jobs?"—from the 1930s through the 1960s.

A better-supported transformation is:

### 1930s

A research problem increasingly asks whether `technological unemployment` can even be isolated from production, market expansion, labour requirements, worker trajectories, and indirect effects.

### 1946

Federal law stabilizes a broader macro responsibility around employment, production, and purchasing power.

### 1955

`Automation` enters this policy architecture. Congress asks simultaneously about direct displacement, mass purchasing power, productivity-gain distribution, business structure, investment, and macro stability.

### 1962

The policy object broadens again. Technology is treated as necessary; unemployment is multicausal; the governable target becomes anticipation and adjustment.

With MDTA, a new administrative vocabulary—`manpower requirements`, `resources`, `utilization`, `mobility`, `occupational outlook`, `training`—organizes what government is expected to know and do.

This is evidence for **problem-object migration**:

```text
machine displacement
        ->
employment / purchasing power / adjustment
        ->
manpower forecasting / skills / mobility / training capacity
```

The old difficulty has not simply been answered. Parts of it have been redistributed into different governable objects.

That is a candidate M2 conclusion, not yet a frozen episode relation.

## 12. Conditions of transformation rather than disappearance

Nothing in this packet supports saying the technology–labour problem disappeared between 1937 and 1964.

Instead, at least four conditions changed what could count as the problem:

1. **Employment Act institutionalization**
   - macro employment/production/purchasing-power responsibility is made durable federal policy.

2. **Postwar technological vocabulary**
   - `automation` emerges as an unstable but politically powerful umbrella, encompassing feedback control, electronics, computing, mechanization, and broader know-how.

3. **High-employment/growth policy**
   - direct displacement is increasingly judged against aggregate growth, labour-force entry, purchasing power, and regional conditions.

4. **Administrative capacity / manpower planning**
   - skills, occupational forecasts, mobility, training, counseling, and placement become objects of federal observation and intervention.

This supports:

```text
problem transformation / responsibility migration
```

not:

```text
problem solved
or
problem became unaskable
```

## 14. Hindsight risks

1. **`Employment Act = automation legislation`.** It establishes a macro policy responsibility before the later automation object is formulated.
2. **`same federal state = same problem`.** Congressional, presidential advisory, Labor Department, BLS, and independent commission actors have different mandates and formulations.
3. **`automation = a stable technology`.** The 1955 committee explicitly says the term varies by speaker and uses it very broadly.
4. **`worker displacement = aggregate unemployment`.** Both 1955 and 1962 materials resist this collapse.
5. **`labor concern = anti-machine`.** The 1955 committee explicitly rejects it.
6. **`training is merely an answer to technological unemployment`.** MDTA creates a broader manpower problem involving skills, future requirements, mobility, counseling, placement, and other structural causes.
7. **`manpower = neutral synonym for labor`.** It is an administrative category with forecasting and utilization implications and must be historicized in its own right.
8. **`same policy instrument = same rationale`.** Retraining can be justified by technological displacement, skill shortage, labor-force growth, defense/Cold War needs, regional distress, or occupational mismatch.
9. **`chronology = transmission`.** January 1962 advisory report and March 1962 MDTA are close in time, but direct causal drafting influence has not yet been demonstrated.
10. **`structural resemblance to 1966 = direct lineage`.** The aggregate/particular distinction is an important lead, not proof of transmission.
11. **`later DOL history = actor testimony`.** Kremen is valuable institutional reconstruction, not contemporary formulation evidence.
12. **`automation caused Black unemployment because later historians connect the two`.** Race appears in the 1962 committee's multicausal unemployment list, but the specific racial problem must be reconstructed from Black labor/civil-rights actors before stronger claims are made.

## 15. Prior-art / duplication check

This bridge is heavily covered by adjacent literatures, so the repository's contribution cannot be "people worried about automation in the 1950s."

- **Prout** reconstructs the politics of automation and jobs across the 1920s–Great Society and is particularly important for congressional/labor political chronology.
- **Resnikoff** argues that `automation` worked as an unstable ideological/discursive category rather than a transparent technical description.
- **Elzway & Resnikoff** sharpen that caution by treating automation as a sociotechnical imaginary and warning against giving the term explanatory authority.
- **Woirol** maps twentieth-century technological- and structural-unemployment debates among economists.

The Problem History increment is narrower:

> recover when institutional actors themselves change what counts as the object of inquiry—from unemployment causation, to macro employment/purchasing-power responsibility, to anticipatory adjustment and manpower capacity—and refuse to call institutional continuity problem identity.

If later work cannot sustain that distinction, this bridge collapses into existing automation-policy history and should not be promoted as a separate contribution.

## Provisional conclusion

The postwar bridge is now much stronger than a retrospective claim that "automation anxiety returned in the 1950s."

What can currently be demonstrated is:

```text
1946:
maximum employment + production + purchasing power
become continuing federal policy responsibility

1955:
automation is inserted into that framework
while the term itself is unstable;
direct displacement is separated from the full-employment problem

1962 Advisory Committee:
technological progress is treated as necessary,
individual displacement as real,
and aggregate unemployment as multicausal;
internal actors still dispute causation and shorter-hours policy

1962 MDTA:
the state object migrates toward manpower requirements,
forecasting, skills, mobility, training, counseling, and placement

1964–66:
these adjustment institutions form part of the background
against which the National Commission later stabilizes
its aggregate-unemployment / particular-displacement distinction
```

The strongest historical claim is therefore not continuity of one eternal technology–jobs question.

It is **continuity of institutions and responsibilities alongside transformation of the problem object**.

That is precisely the kind of bridge the repository needs before any 1937 -> 1966 episode relation can be frozen.
