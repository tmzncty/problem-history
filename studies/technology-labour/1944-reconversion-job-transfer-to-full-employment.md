# 1944 reconversion: from wartime manpower requirements to job-transfer adjustment and full-employment planning

Status: **M2 source packet / transition-and-overlap test / not a frozen Problem Episode**

Pilot: technology–labour

This packet follows two merged source chains:

- `1941-1945-wartime-labor-requirements-repurposing.md`, which established that BLS wartime manpower work estimated labor requirements against production schedules and labor supply;
- `1946-1962-full-employment-manpower-bridge.md`, which begins from the Employment Act of 1946 and later automation/manpower institutions.

The remaining gap is not merely chronological. The key Problem History question is:

> How did a wartime knowledge apparatus built to ask whether enough labor was available for required production become usable, before the war was over, for the nearly inverse question of how many workers would need new jobs when military demand and war contracts disappeared?

The strongest new evidence is concentrated in 1944:

1. BLS prepared `Factors Determining Postwar Job Transfers and Unemployment` for the Senate War Contracts Subcommittee while wartime labor scarcity still existed.
2. In May, the War Contracts Subcommittee explicitly said its initial contract-termination problem had proved too narrow and widened the object to industrial demobilization, reemployment, retraining, unemployment compensation, civilian reconversion, and interagency coordination.
3. The enacted War Mobilization and Reconversion Act of 3 October created a transition administration, required a study of Federal manpower functions, established a Retraining and Reemployment Administration, and synchronized war-production cutbacks with nonwar production—but explicitly prohibited keeping soldiers in uniform or continuing unnecessary war contracts merely to prevent unemployment.
4. By December, the same War Contracts Subcommittee was publishing `Legislation for Reconversion and Full Employment`, moving beyond transition administration toward the aggregate problem of finding an economic substitute for wartime demand and assuring continuing full employment.

The best current reconstruction is therefore **not**:

```text
wartime manpower shortage
→ war ends
→ postwar unemployment problem begins
```

The sources instead support an overlap and change of forecast horizon:

```text
1941–43 wartime manpower estimates
required output → future labor requirements → available labor supply

        ↓ same statistical / labor-force infrastructure remains active

1944 anticipatory reconversion
future contract cutbacks + demobilization
→ workers / places / industries likely to become surplus
→ job transfer + retraining + reemployment + unemployment risk

        ↓ legislative problem broadens

Oct 1944 transition administration
cutbacks + civilian reconversion + released manpower
+ retraining / reemployment + agency coordination

        ↓ further reformulation inside the same congressional workstream

Dec 1944 full-employment planning
war demand will disappear
→ what economic activity will replace it?
→ how can continuing full employment be assured?

        ↓ direct later legislative genealogy

1945 full-employment bill → Employment Act 1946
```

The continuity is strongest for **institutions, data, forecast practices, and legislative genealogy**. The governed problem changes substantially.

A provisional researcher-side description is **forecast-horizon reversal** or **mobilization-to-reconversion problem inversion**. These are analytical labels only, not actor vocabulary and not a schema proposal.

## 1. Evidence layers and source control

### 1.1 BLS, `Factors Determining Postwar Job Transfers and Unemployment` (February 1944)

Contemporaneous bibliographic record:

- U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, `Factors Determining Postwar Job Transfers and Unemployment`, *Monthly Labor Review* 58(2), February 1944, pp. 269–279;
- separately reprinted as BLS serial R.1620;
- prepared for the Subcommittee on War Contract Termination of the Senate Committee on Military Affairs.

Contemporaneous Monthly Catalog entry:

- <https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP3-2770294c3bb6dfebdc8928f4fdd1401f/pdf/GOVPUB-GP3-2770294c3bb6dfebdc8928f4fdd1401f.pdf>
- SuDoc `L 2.6/a:P 847/2`;
- Library of Congress card `L44-37`.

A later California planning digest provides a useful content map:

- <https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/82245/files/C114007914.pdf>

The digest says the BLS study organized expected postwar transfers/unemployment around four moving components:

1. military demobilization;
2. demobilization of war industry;
3. withdrawals from the labor force;
4. reconversion and expansion of civilian production.

It also summarizes a BLS scenario in which large munitions cutbacks could displace millions of workers in metal, chemical, and rubber manufacturing, with secondary effects on suppliers, transport, and war-boom communities.

**Evidence strength in this packet:**

- **very high** for existence, date, title, pagination, agency authorship, reprint identity, and commission by the Senate subcommittee;
- **medium** for detailed substantive claims taken only from the later digest until the original pp. 269–279 are recovered and page-audited.

**Boundary:**

The Berkeley digest is not the BLS original. It is used as a source map, not as a substitute for exact actor wording.

The original MLR issue has been identified in FRASER, but this run did not obtain stable page-level full text for pp. 269–279. Do not freeze quotations from the article from the digest.

### 1.2 BLS Bulletin 831 (May 1945) retrospectively locates the 1944 study inside BLS's own programme

U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, *Fact-Finding Activities of the Bureau of Labor Statistics*, Bulletin 831, 1945:

- <https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/fact-finding-activities-bureau-labor-statistics-4303/fulltext>

This is later than the February 1944 article but still a contemporaneous institutional self-description produced before demobilization was complete.

Under `Employment and Occupational Outlook`, BLS says its wartime analysis had centered on manpower requirements and labor supply, while it was using its experience in employment measurement to anticipate future developments. It then identifies the February 1944 job-transfer/unemployment article as an example of the broad analysis underlying its postwar industry and occupational outlook work.

The same section says occupational studies were being expanded for counselors and agencies assisting demobilized soldiers and war workers, with information on employment opportunities, training requirements, wages, and conditions.

Immediately afterward, Bulletin 831 describes the wartime manpower-requirements programme: future labor needs estimated from official war-production schedules and compared with available labor supply.

This juxtaposition makes the infrastructure reuse actor-visible:

```text
wartime:
future production schedules
→ future labor requirements
→ labor supply

same Bureau / overlapping employment data

postwar anticipation:
future cutbacks + demobilization
→ future employment opportunities
→ transfers / retraining / reemployment
```

Evidence strength: **very high / contemporaneous official BLS self-description**.

Boundary: Bulletin 831 is a May 1945 synthesis. It proves that BLS itself placed the 1944 study inside a broader employment/outlook programme; it does not by itself prove what every analyst intended in February 1944.

### 1.4 War Contracts Subcommittee, `Demobilization and Post-War Adjustment Legislation` (12 May 1944)

The report was printed directly in the *Congressional Record*, Senate, 12 May 1944, beginning around Congressional Record p. 4380.

Official Congressional Record scan/indexed text:

- <https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1944-pt4/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1944-pt4-1-1.pdf>

This is the strongest actor-side source for the **widening of the congressional problem**.

The subcommittee explicitly reconstructs its own trajectory. It says that since it began studying war-contract termination, the broader economic problems generated by termination had become increasingly evident.

The report distinguishes:

- settlement of termination claims;
- disposal of surplus war property;
- war-production cutbacks and civilian-production resumption;
- the `human aspects` of demobilization and postwar adjustment;
- retraining;
- unemployment compensation;
- transportation / return home;
- placement and reemployment;
- interagency coordination.

This is not a later historian inferring scope expansion from a sequence of bill titles. The subcommittee itself says its original object proved too narrow.

The report also presents an anticipatory mass-unemployment problem: war-production cutbacks had already created small pools of unemployment, and the end of military demand threatened much larger dislocation. It treats the absence of clear national policy as an obstacle not only to postwar planning but, psychologically, to the ongoing war effort.

Evidence strength: **very high / direct congressional actor self-description**.

Boundary:

- the report speaks for a committee work product, not every witness, senator, labor organization, employer, or executive agency;
- its unemployment estimates are forecasts under uncertainty, not observations of what later occurred;
- the report's claim that the problem had broadened is strong evidence of actor-recognized reformulation, but it does not establish that the February BLS study caused that reformulation.

## 2. The same subcommittee explicitly outgrows its original contract-termination problem

The May report is especially valuable because it provides a direct internal genealogy without requiring a historian to infer one.

The starting object was concrete and administrative:

```text
Government cancels a war contract
→ how is the contractor's claim settled?
→ how are inventories removed?
```

By May 1944, the subcommittee says this cannot be handled in isolation.

Contract termination changes production. Production cutbacks release facilities and workers. Released workers raise questions of unemployment compensation, retraining, placement, migration/return, and the geography of war-boom communities. Surplus-property decisions affect how rapidly civilian production can resume. Each of these streams creates coordination problems among agencies.

The actor-side expansion can be represented provisionally as:

```text
contract settlement
        ↓ actor-explicit insufficiency
industrial demobilization
        ↓
released facilities + released workers
        ↓
reemployment / retraining / unemployment protection
        ↓
civilian reconversion + agency coordination
```

This is a strong case for:

```text
same committee
!= same problem
```

and more specifically:

```text
institutional jurisdiction can expand
because an initially bounded problem is judged insufficient
```

The historical relation is stronger than mere chronology because the committee explicitly says the broader scope became evident during its work.

But this still does not imply that every component became one conceptually unified problem. Contract settlement, surplus property, retraining, unemployment compensation, and macro employment could remain distinct even while one committee coordinated them.

Therefore:

```text
one legislative workstream
!= one historical problem object
```

## 3. 1944 turns the wartime forecast horizon around before the war is over

The previous wartime packet established the characteristic BLS problem:

```text
known / planned military output
→ how much labor will it require?
→ is enough labor available?
```

The February–May 1944 materials use the same kinds of labor-force, industry, production, and locality knowledge under a different future scenario:

```text
war demand / contracts will contract
→ how much labor will be released?
→ where will released workers be located?
→ what civilian production can absorb them?
→ who needs transfer / training / income support?
```

The change is not simply from `present` to `future`; both wartime and reconversion work are forecasts.

The more precise distinction is **the direction of the counterfactual shock**:

```text
wartime requirements forecast:
more required war output → labor scarcity risk

reconversion forecast:
less war output / demobilization → labor surplus / transfer risk
```

This is why `same forecasting technique` cannot establish problem identity.

A candidate method fixture is:

```text
same forecast apparatus
+ reversed exogenous scenario
!= same historical problem
```

The researcher's shorthand `forecast-horizon reversal` means only that an anticipatory state apparatus began using related data to estimate the consequences of unwinding the condition it had previously helped create.

It should not be treated as an actor term.

## 4. The February BLS commission and the May Senate report create an institutional link—but not yet a causal one

The February BLS study was explicitly prepared for the Senate War Contract Termination Subcommittee.

By May, that same subcommittee publicly explained why contract termination had to be embedded in a wider demobilization / adjustment problem.

This yields strong evidence for:

```text
BLS postwar-transfer analysis
↔ Senate subcommittee information environment
```

and for a direct **commissioning relation**.

It does **not** yet yield:

```text
BLS February study
→ caused the May reformulation
```

To prove that stronger edge, the next source should be:

- the original BLS report as submitted to the subcommittee;
- hearing references to the report;
- staff memoranda or correspondence;
- explicit citations in subcommittee drafts.

This distinction matters because `information commissioned by an institution` is weaker than `information adopted as the institution's causal model`.

Fixture:

```text
commissioned research
!= adopted formulation
!= causal influence
```

## 5. Competing 1944 adjustment programmes should remain separate

The May report itself records multiple pending institutional designs rather than one settled answer.

It discusses at least:

- S. 1730, associated with an Office of Demobilization and industrial-demobilization policy;
- S. 1823 / its successor route, proposing a broader war-mobilization/postwar-adjustment machinery with worker training, placement, and unemployment protection;
- contract-settlement legislation as a narrower stream.

The report also lists testimony from actors representing labor, business, agriculture, and public service.

Therefore the proper unit is a **contested legislative problem field**, not `Congress believed X`.

The exact differences among these bills and witnesses remain a next-source task.

Until those texts are compared article-by-article:

```text
same hearing docket
!= same formulation
```

and:

```text

!= witness consensus
```

## 6. The War Mobilization and Reconversion Act of 1944 gives a page-audited enacted boundary

Public Law 458, 3 October 1944, 58 Stat. 785, has been checked against the official GovInfo Statutes at Large scan:

- <https://www.govinfo.gov/link/statute/58/785>

Relevant printed pages:

- 58 Stat. 785: Office of War Mobilization and Reconversion; transition planning;
- 58 Stat. 786: studies, manpower-agency review, cooperation, reporting;
- 58 Stat. 787: demobilization/reconversion policies and explicit limits on preserving wartime activity for employment;
- 58 Stat. 788–789: released manpower / facilities and Retraining and Reemployment Administration.

This source is crucial because it shows which part of the broad spring 1944 problem field became enacted administrative authority.

### 6.1 Transition itself becomes a statutory object

Section 101 requires plans for problems arising from the transition from war to peace and authorizes coordination of executive agencies around those plans.

The act therefore does not wait for peace to make postwar dislocation governable. Anticipation is embedded in the administrative mandate.

### 6.2 `Manpower` appears as an administrative-coordination field, not yet the 1961–63 manpower object

Section 101(c)(6) orders a specific study of existing executive-agency functions `in the field of manpower` and a programme for reorganizing/consolidating those agencies as far as practicable.

This is a strong early administrative use of `manpower`.

It must **not** be silently identified with the 1961–63 manpower problem recovered elsewhere in the repository.

In 1944 the immediate setting is wartime mobilisation and reconversion administration. In 1961–63 the recovered object includes continuous national appraisal of manpower requirements/resources, technological change, skill forecasting, training and utilization under MDTA and the Manpower Administration.

Fixture:

```text
same administrative label across decades
!= same governed object
```

The 1944 occurrence is evidence for vocabulary/institutional prehistory, not automatic problem continuity.

### 6.3 The statute explicitly refuses one possible unemployment answer: preserve unnecessary war activity

The most revealing provisions are sections 201 and 202.

The War and Navy Departments may not keep people in the armed forces merely to prevent unemployment or while they await jobs.

Contracting agencies likewise may not continue war-production contracts no longer needed for the war merely to provide business and employment.

This is a remarkably strong answer-space boundary.

The statute recognizes unemployment risk while rejecting:

```text
keep soldiers mobilized to hide unemployment
```

and:

```text
keep unnecessary war contracts running to preserve jobs
```

Instead, its enacted adjustment logic turns toward transition, civilian reconversion, release/reuse of manpower and facilities, retraining, reemployment, and unemployment-system support.

This supports:

```text
recognition of unemployment risk
!= acceptance of every employment-preserving instrument
```

and:

```text
problem continuity cannot be inferred from a stable desired outcome
because the legitimate answer space can be sharply redefined
```

### 6.4 Cutbacks are to be synchronized with nonwar production

Section 203 requires curtailment of war production / termination of war contracts to be integrated with expansion, resumption, or initiation of other war production and, where compatible with prosecution of the war, production for nonwar use.

The act also requires advance reporting of anticipated production changes and consultation concerning effective use of facilities and manpower released by expected cutbacks.

This turns `released manpower` into an anticipatory allocation problem:

```text
anticipated cutback
→ anticipated release of facilities / workers
→ civilian / other production possibilities
```

The object is not merely a worker who becomes unemployed after a closure. The law asks agencies to manage the transition before release occurs.

### 6.5 Retraining and reemployment are institutionally separated from general reconversion coordination

Title III establishes a Retraining and Reemployment Administration to coordinate existing federal activities in:

- retraining;
- reemployment;
- vocational education;
- vocational rehabilitation;

and to coordinate with State/local programmes.

This matters for Problem History because the statute does not collapse all adjustment into one office or one intervention.

A broad transition problem is administratively decomposed.

Fixture:

```text
one statute
!= one problem
```

and:

```text
administrative decomposition
can coexist with political problem integration
```

## 7. October 1944 is not yet the same thing as a full-employment statute

Later official Joint Economic Committee reconstruction (1966) is useful here as a source map:

- <https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/twentieth-anniversary-employment-act-1946-economic-symposium-1199/fulltext>

The JEC history characterizes the October 1944 act as narrower than the broader employment proposals circulating around Kilgore's reconversion legislation and says important employment provisions were stripped from the enacted measure.

This is **later authoritative reconstruction**, not October 1944 actor wording.

The enacted text itself supports the narrower interpretation in a different way: it supplies a transition/coordination/retraining/reemployment apparatus but does not impose the later Employment Act's continuing macro responsibility for maximum employment, production, and purchasing power.

Therefore:

```text
reconversion administration
!= full-employment macro policy
```

Even when both respond to an anticipated postwar employment difficulty.

This distinction is essential for the next step in the chain.

## 8. By December the same subcommittee moves from adjustment to an aggregate-demand / full-employment problem

The War Contracts Subcommittee's year-end report, `Legislation for Reconversion and Full Employment`, was entered into the Congressional Record in December 1944.

Official Congressional Record package:

- <https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1944-pt7/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1944-pt7-15.pdf>

The searchable Congressional Record text identifies the report as recommendations on industrial demobilization and on further legislation to develop a postwar economy assuring continuing full employment.

A later official JEC reconstruction confirms that this report contained the first published draft in the legislative line that became the Full Employment Bill of 1945.

The report's famous diagnostic—preserved in later congressional reprints and archival copies—is that the 78th Congress had not yet dealt with the need for an **economic substitute for war production**.

That is a substantial problem reformulation.

The May object had been strongly transition-specific:

```text
contracts stop
→ facilities and workers are released
→ coordinate cutbacks
→ retrain / place / insure / reconvert
```

The December object reaches beyond the transition:

```text
wartime public demand disappears
→ total peacetime economic activity may be insufficient
→ continuing full employment requires an economy-wide policy framework
```

The `economic substitute` formulation is not equivalent to `find another job for each displaced war worker`.

It changes:

- **scale**: worker / plant / locality → aggregate economy;
- **causal object**: specific demobilization event → adequacy of total peacetime activity/demand;
- **time horizon**: transition period → continuing employment responsibility;
- **responsibility**: coordination of existing agencies → continuing federal macro-policy responsibility;
- **answer space**: retraining / placement / compensation / reconversion → production, investment, expenditure, purchasing power, and full-employment policy.

This is the most important transformation in the packet.

## 9. Same committee + direct genealogy still does not prove one unchanged problem

The sequence is unusually well suited to the repository's identity method because historical transmission is strong.

It is not a retrospective construction that unrelated actors happened to use similar words.

The same Senate subcommittee:

1. begins with contract termination;
2. explicitly says that object is too narrow;
3. investigates demobilization / postwar adjustment;
4. operates within the legislative workstream that yields the October reconversion statute;
5. publishes a December full-employment proposal.

Later JEC history gives a direct legislative genealogy from the December draft to S.380 and eventually the Employment Act debate.

Yet the problem changes materially.

Therefore this packet supplies another strong fixture:

```text
same institution
+ explicit actor genealogy
+ short time interval
!= same problem
```

This is methodologically stronger than distant century-scale analogies because all the usual continuity cues are present—and still do not settle identity.

## 10. A possible three-stage 1944 transformation

The evidence currently supports a three-stage reconstruction more strongly than one seamless `postwar employment problem`.

### Stage A — forecasted transfer / unemployment

Actor-side question:

```text
When military and war-industry demand falls,
how many people will leave the armed forces / war jobs,
where will job losses appear,
and how much civilian expansion / labor-force withdrawal
will absorb them?
```

Primary institutional marker: BLS February study for the Senate subcommittee.

### Stage B — managed reconversion / adjustment

Actor-side question:

```text
How should contract cutbacks, released facilities/manpower,
civilian reconversion, retraining, reemployment,
unemployment protection, and multiple agencies be coordinated?
```

Primary markers: May subcommittee report; October Public Law 458.

### Stage C — continuing full employment

Actor-side question:

```text
What economy-wide substitute for wartime demand
can sustain employment and production after reconversion,
and what continuing federal responsibility is required?
```

Primary marker: December subcommittee full-employment report, followed by 1945 legislation.

These stages overlap. They should not be forced into discrete dates as though one instantly replaced another.

## 11. Overlap is more accurate than epoch replacement

The sources make a clean periodization especially dangerous.

In 1944:

- wartime labor scarcity remains real in important industries;
- military mobilization is still continuing;
- BLS is still estimating wartime labor requirements;
- agencies are simultaneously anticipating demobilization;
- contract cutbacks already create localized unemployment;
- legislators are designing retraining / reemployment machinery;
- macro full-employment proposals emerge before the war ends.

Thus:

```text
wartime manpower scarcity problem
and
postwar unemployment problem
coexist
```

The shift is not a national economy flipping from one equilibrium to another on V-E Day or V-J Day.

Fixture:

```text
problem emergence
!= predecessor disappearance
```

and:

```text
future problem can become governable
before its predicted causal condition fully arrives
```

This is one of the strongest additions from the 1944 chain.

## 12. The law's prohibition on job-preserving war activity clarifies what `full employment` will later have to mean

Public Law 458's sections 201–202 are especially important when read next to the December turn.

Congress rejects two simple ways of keeping measured employment high:

- retain servicemen to keep them out of civilian unemployment;
- keep unnecessary war contracts alive merely to provide employment.

That means the later full-employment problem cannot be reduced to preserving the wartime structure of jobs.

A genuine postwar answer has to confront:

- civilian production;
- investment / demand;
- worker transfer;
- business conversion;
- new job creation;
- aggregate purchasing power and production.

The statute therefore helps explain the **negative boundary** of the emerging full-employment problem.

This is not a claim that Public Law 458 already contains the 1946 Employment Act's theory.

It is narrower:

> by October 1944, enacted policy explicitly refused to make unnecessary military retention or unnecessary war production the employment solution.

This negative answer-space evidence is historically valuable because problem identity depends on acceptable answers as well as labels.

## 14. `Manpower` in 1944 must not be back-projected from 1961–63

The repository now contains two strong manpower clusters:

- wartime / reconversion administration, 1941–45;
- automation / retraining / national manpower planning, 1961–63.

Their shared vocabulary is dangerous.

In Public Law 458, manpower is embedded in:

- war mobilization and demobilization;
- agency functions;
- release and reallocation during contract cutbacks;
- consolidation of wartime agencies.

By 1961–63, the recovered federal manpower object includes:

- future manpower requirements and resources;
- occupational / skill forecasting;
- automation / technological change;
- training and retraining;
- utilization and mobility;
- a comprehensive peacetime Manpower Program.

There may be institutional and vocabulary genealogy. This packet does not prove problem identity across that gap.

Fixture:

```text
same term + federal administrative use
!= same problem across decades
```

A future source chain should test whether 1944 manpower-agency reorganization work was remembered, cited, or institutionally inherited by the 1950s–60s manpower planners.

## 18. Hindsight risks / failure fixtures

### 18.1 `war shortage → peace unemployment` as clean succession

False simplification.

Both problems are already being worked simultaneously in 1944.

```text
problem emergence != predecessor disappearance
```

### 18.2 BLS forecast = realized postwar unemployment

False.

The February study is an anticipatory conditional analysis made under wartime uncertainty.

Forecast object and realized outcome must remain separate.

```text
forecasted problem != later realized magnitude
```

### 18.3 Commissioned BLS research = congressional causal model

False until uptake evidence is recovered.

```text
research commissioned by actor != actor adopts all research conclusions
```

### 18.4 Same Senate subcommittee = same problem

False.

The committee itself says its initial contract-settlement scope became inadequate.

```text

```

Directly contradicted by PL 458's answer-space boundary.

```text
full employment objective != freeze incumbent production structure
```

### 18.7 `manpower` 1944 = `manpower` 1963

Unsupported.

```text
stable policy vocabulary != stable governed object
```

### 18.8 December full employment = February job-transfer study scaled up

Too simple.

## 21. Provisional conclusion

The 1944 evidence materially strengthens the long technology/labour chain because it supplies a transition that is both institutionally connected and substantively discontinuous.

BLS and federal agencies did not simply stop forecasting labor requirements when postwar planning began. A durable labor-force / production / employment information apparatus was turned toward a different anticipated shock.

The February BLS study asks how workers will move and where unemployment may appear when wartime demand unwinds. The May War Contracts Subcommittee report explicitly says a bounded contract-termination problem has become a broader demobilization / human-adjustment / coordination problem. Public Law 458 turns part of that field into transition administration while rejecting preservation of unnecessary military service or unnecessary war production as an employment solution. By December, the same subcommittee is asking the larger question of what can replace wartime production strongly enough to assure continuing full employment.

The strongest current historical statement is therefore:

```text
research / legislative genealogy = strong
problem identity = not stable
```

More specifically:

```text
wartime manpower scarcity forecasting
→ anticipatory demobilization / transfer forecasting
→ managed reconversion / retraining / reemployment
→ aggregate continuing full-employment planning
```

The chain is real, but the problem is being reformulated while the chain is being built.

That is exactly the kind of case for which Problem History needs to distinguish transmission from identity.
