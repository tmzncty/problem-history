# 2000: selective Humphrey–Hawkins reenactment and the monetary-range accountability split

Status: **M2 implementation/disappearance test / not a frozen Problem Episode**

Pilot: technology–labour

This packet extends:

- `1994-2000-humphrey-hawkins-monetary-ranges-to-reporting-afterlife.md`;
- `1981-1982-humphrey-hawkins-required-path-to-benchmark.md`;
- `1979-1983-humphrey-hawkins-goals-to-forecasts-implementation-drift.md`.

The previous packet established a strong structural fact:

```text
1978
semiannual Fed reporting
+ monetary/credit growth ranges
+ relation to employment/inflation/economic goals
+ revision/deviation explanation

        ↓

1994
ranges formally survive
but are recast as long-run benchmarks

        ↓

May–July 2000
old periodic-reporting requirement expires
FOMC stops setting ranges

        ↓

December 2000
Congress restores semiannual monetary-policy hearings/reports
but does not restore mandatory monetary/credit ranges
```

That supported a researcher-side description of **selective statutory reconstruction**, but one major source debt remained:

> Was the missing monetary-range machinery merely an omission produced by sunset / bill consolidation, or did contemporaneous actors explicitly understand the 2000 redesign as preserving congressional monetary-policy oversight while discarding the range requirement?

Fresh primary evidence now answers that question much more strongly.

Two actor-side sources independently separate the observatory from the range mechanism:

1. the **28 June 2000 FOMC transcript**, where Alan Greenspan anticipates renewed semiannual reports without annual ranges and argues that the ranges are no longer useful as policy guides, while Alfred Broaddus warns that removing them also removes a form of quantitative institutional accountability;
2. **Senator Phil Gramm’s 7 December 2000 floor statement**, immediately before Senate passage of H.R. 5640, where the Banking Committee chairman explicitly says the bill renews the Federal Reserve monetary-policy report while eliminating outdated indicators, naming money-supply measures as an example.

The enacted statute then implements exactly that selective architecture:

```text
old §2A first sentence retained
+ new §2B semiannual hearings/reports
+ broad employment / production / income / productivity / prices observatory

BUT

- statutory monetary/credit growth ranges
- range-based revision/deviation explanation machinery
```

The strongest new finding is therefore narrower and better sourced than a generic claim that “Humphrey–Hawkins expired”:

> **By late 2000, important actors on both the Federal Reserve and congressional sides explicitly separated the value of continued monetary-policy oversight from the usefulness of mandatory monetary-growth ranges. The final statute enacted that separation.**

This is strong evidence for **actor-recognized instrument obsolescence inside a surviving problem/oversight field**.

Researcher-side shorthand below includes `selective observatory reconstruction`, `indicator de-legitimation`, and `accountability-function split`. These are analytical descriptions, not historical actor vocabulary and not schema proposals.

## 1. Source control

### 1.1 Federal Open Market Committee transcript, 27–28 Jun 2000

Federal Reserve official transcript PDF:

- https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/FOMC20000628meeting.pdf

Relevant transcript pages:

- pp. 81–83 (PDF pages around the 28 Jun morning session);
- later discussion pp. 84–90 remains useful for competing replacement ideas.

Evidence strength: **very high / direct internal deliberation / page-image checked**.

Why this is stronger than the July 2000 Monetary Policy Report already used in the prior packet:

The July report tells us that the FOMC did not establish ranges because the legal requirement had expired and because ranges had not been useful policy benchmarks for years.

The June transcript adds three things:

1. **prospective institutional knowledge** — Greenspan says the likely renewal design will preserve semiannual reports but omit annual ranges;
2. **an actor-level causal diagnosis** — he ties the loss of usefulness to difficulties in identifying a stable monetary object amid changing financial products, velocity relationships, and productivity/price-stability uncertainty;
3. **internal disagreement about what is lost** — Broaddus accepts that the ranges no longer matter operationally but argues that they had supplied a form of quantitative institutional accountability.

This is therefore not merely a record of technical non-use. It is evidence that actors were debating what institutional function would disappear when the ranges disappeared.

Use:

The FOMC transcript in fact preserves a direct counter-formulation from Broaddus.

## 2. Fresh finding A: before reenactment, Greenspan already expected “reports without ranges”

At the start of the 28 June 2000 FOMC discussion, Greenspan distinguishes three different statuses that are easy to collapse retrospectively:

```text
money as an economically relevant object
!=
monetary aggregates as useful observed indicators
!=
mandatory growth ranges as useful policy/accountability instruments
```

His argument is not that money has become irrelevant.

He explicitly says monetary aggregates may still contain information and should continue to be monitored.

The difficulty is instead that the FOMC can no longer confidently map the available statistical aggregates onto a stable underlying concept of money in a way that supports policy based on those measures.

His causal explanation includes:

- proliferating financial products;
- changing composition of money and near-money;
- unstable / uncertain velocity relationships;
- uncertainty about long-run productivity growth;
- uncertainty about what precise numerical condition should count as price stability.

The actor problem is therefore not:

```text
“money no longer matters”
```

but closer to:

```text
monetary causation may remain important
+
monetary aggregates remain observable
BUT
available aggregate measures no longer provide a sufficiently stable control object
for the statutory range mechanism
```

This is a much stronger disappearance mechanism than simple policy fashion.

## 3. Fresh finding B: Greenspan distinguishes use-value from legal/formal survival

Greenspan says the FOMC had not used the ranges to guide policy for many years.

That means the June 2000 legal sunset did **not** create the functional hollowing.

Instead, the sequence is:

```text
operational non-use develops first
        ↓
formal requirement continues
        ↓
requirement expires
        ↓
FOMC chooses not to continue ranges voluntarily
```

This reinforces the prior packet’s distinction:

```text
function weakens
!=
formal obligation disappears
```

But the transcript makes the causal transition more actor-explicit.

Greenspan is not merely taking advantage of a technical lapse. He says it is uncomfortable to keep formally establishing ranges that the Committee does not actually employ for policy formulation.

So for him:

```text
formal reporting without operative policy use
→ institutional mismatch
→ sunset creates an opportunity to remove the mismatched mechanism
```

This is an actor-side rationale for discontinuation.

## 4. Fresh finding C: the June 2000 FOMC already anticipates the final selective-reenactment architecture

Greenspan says that the likely outcome of renewal legislation will preserve semiannual monetary-policy reports to Congress but **not** require annual monetary ranges.

This matters for genealogy.

The December 2000 result was not an institutional design that appeared from nowhere at final enactment.

By late June the Fed chairman understood a prospective legislative solution in essentially the same structural terms:

```text
Congress–Fed semiannual reporting relation survives
+
monetary-range obligation does not
```

Current evidence does **not** yet establish:

- who first drafted this exact split;
- whether Greenspan was describing a specific circulating bill text, staff negotiation, or informed expectation;
- whether Federal Reserve preferences causally produced the congressional language.

Therefore:

```text
actor anticipates later statutory architecture
!=
actor authored that architecture
```

This is a high-value transmission question for the next archival / legislative-history pass.

## 5. Fresh finding D: Broaddus shows that “operationally obsolete” did not mean “institutionally costless to remove”

Alfred Broaddus agrees that the operational significance of the monetary targets had steadily diminished.

But he explicitly warns against treating their disappearance as a non-event.

His historical interpretation is that the ranges had served as:

- an implicit Congress–Fed agreement about Federal Reserve responsibility for long-run inflation control;
- a regular occasion for strategic discussion of long-run policy objectives;
- a weak but real form of quantitative institutional accountability.

He worries that after the statutory requirement lapses, the FOMC becomes more formally discretionary.

This gives an unusually clean same-room competing formulation:

```text
Greenspan:
range mechanism is no longer a credible policy guide;
its formal continuation despite non-use is hard to justify

Broaddus:
range mechanism is operationally weak
BUT
still carries an accountability / institutional-anchor function
```

Therefore:

```text
same actors agree on operational non-use
!=
same actors agree on institutional irrelevance
```

This is important for disappearance tests.

A mechanism can lose one function while retaining another.

If the historian only asks whether the FOMC “used M2 targets to set policy,” the accountability function disappears from view before the statute does.

---

## 6. Fresh finding E: Greenspan accepts the accountability loss but proposes functional substitution rather than range restoration

Greenspan does not simply reject Broaddus’s concern.

He agrees that some institutional-anchor issue remains, but argues that the Committee can address it without restoring the statutory range procedure.

He proposes using the recurring pre-testimony meeting time for a more generic discussion of long-run inflation conditions / objectives.

Broaddus wants something stronger: a more formal institutional substitute, and raises explicit inflation targeting as one possibility.

This creates another problem-history distinction:

```text
old mechanism loses credibility
→ actors may disagree whether to:

A. remove it and preserve only generic deliberation;
B. replace it with a new formal anchor;
C. retain it because its accountability value exceeds its policy-guide weakness.
```

No single “instrument became obsolete” formulation captures all three possibilities.

The debate is about **which function must be replaced**, not only whether the old indicator still predicts well.

## 7. Fresh finding F: Senator Gramm supplies the missing congressional rationale

On 7 December 2000, immediately before Senate passage of H.R. 5640, Banking Committee chairman Phil Gramm describes the Federal Reserve provisions in two parts.

First, he treats the twice-yearly Federal Reserve appearance/report as important congressional oversight.

Second, he says the legislation removes older required economic indicators and identifies measures of the money supply as an example of indicators that he considers no longer useful.

This is substantially stronger than inferring congressional purpose from the enacted omission.

The history can now be reconstructed as:

```text
not merely:
old ranges absent from new statute
→ historian infers selective deletion

but:
Banking chairman explicitly says:
restore report/oversight
+ eliminate old money-supply indicator requirements
→ statute does so
```

That is **actor-explicit selective reconstruction**.

Important boundary:

Gramm’s formulation is not proof that Congress collectively accepted a settled economic theory of monetary aggregates.

It is, however, high-strength evidence that at least one principal legislative manager understood the design as intentional selection rather than accidental omission.

## 8. Enacted answer: broad observatory retained, intermediate range object removed

Public Law 106-569 §1003 makes the structural distinction unusually visible.

### Retained / restored

- the first sentence of §2A, which retains the long-run monetary/credit-aggregate mandate language and links it to maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates;
- semiannual appearances before Congress;
- written monetary-policy reports;
- consideration of employment and unemployment;
- production;
- investment;
- real income;
- productivity;
- exchange rates;
- international trade/payments;
- prices.

### Not restored

- announced annual ranges for monetary and credit aggregates;
- the old current-year / prospective-range reporting structure;
- the old requirement to explain revisions / deviations from those ranges.

So the relevant historical object is not:

```text
Federal Reserve accountability: present → absent
```

It is:

```text
one range-centered accountability architecture
→ a broader narrative/observatory reporting architecture
without that intermediate target object
```

This is a much more precise transformation/disappearance statement.

## 9. Actor formulations must remain separate

### 9.1 Greenspan / Fed-chair formulation

Problem:

- statutory ranges have lost operational meaning because measured aggregates no longer supply a stable policy control object;
- continued formal range-setting risks ritualizing a mechanism the FOMC does not actually use.

Desired answer:

- stop setting mandatory ranges;
- keep monitoring money/credit as information;
- preserve broader long-run policy discussion and congressional reporting.

### 9.2 Broaddus formulation

Problem:

- even an operationally weak range process carries institutional accountability and anchors long-run inflation responsibility;
- dropping it increases discretion.

Desired answer:

- do not confuse operational obsolescence with disappearance of the need for an institutional anchor;
- consider a formal replacement, potentially an inflation target.

### 9.3 Gramm / Banking-chair formulation

Problem:

- Congress still needs recurring Federal Reserve monetary-policy oversight;
- some older required indicators, including money-supply measures in his formulation, are no longer useful.

Desired answer:

- renew semiannual Fed reporting;
- remove obsolete indicator requirements.

### 9.4 Enacted statute

The enacted law does not choose one actor’s full causal theory.

It does, however, institutionalize the shared structural split:

```text
semiannual oversight / broad economic observatory = yes
mandatory monetary-range intermediate target = no
```

Do not collapse that institutional result into unanimous agreement about why money ranges failed.

## 10. Relation judgment

### 10.1 1978 range mechanism → 2000 range mechanism

At the mechanism level, the strongest judgment is now **disappearance**, not mere reformulation:

- the mandatory annual range object is no longer required;
- its associated revision/deviation accountability relation is no longer required;
- the FOMC stops setting the ranges;
- Congress later reenacts the reporting relationship without them;
- a principal legislative manager explicitly frames money-supply measures as among the requirements being eliminated.

But this must remain dimension-specific.

It does **not** imply:

- disappearance of money as an economic object;
- disappearance of monetary aggregates as data;
- disappearance of Federal Reserve responsibility for maximum employment / price stability;
- disappearance of congressional monetary-policy oversight;
- disappearance of the larger full-employment / inflation problem field.

### 10.2 1978 §2A/§108 observatory → 2000 §2B observatory

This is better treated as a **selective institutional successor / reformulation candidate** rather than simple continuity.

The broad monitoring field and semiannual Congress–Fed interface survive, but the intermediate target / accountability architecture changes materially.

Do not freeze a whole-episode relation solely from this packet.

### 10.3 1994 benchmark reinterpretation → 2000 deletion

The new evidence strengthens the sequence:

```text
functional hollowing
→ actor debate about residual accountability value
→ legal expiry
→ voluntary non-continuation
→ actor-explicit selective reenactment
```

This is stronger than a simple “sunset caused disappearance” model.

## 12. Why this matters for Problem History

The case supplies a particularly clean answer to a recurring methodological problem:

> How can a historical problem or mechanism disappear when its vocabulary, institution, measurements, and some surrounding procedures remain visible?

Here, almost every superficial continuity marker survives:

- Federal Reserve Act §2A survives in part;
- maximum employment and price stability remain;
- monetary-policy reports continue;
- congressional hearings continue;
- money and credit continue to be observed;
- the Fed continues to discuss inflation and employment;
- the old Humphrey–Hawkins label remains available historically.

Yet one specific relation becomes unaskable as a legal requirement:

```text
What monetary/credit growth range will the FOMC announce,
and how must it explain later revision/deviation from that range?
```

That question is no longer part of the mandatory semiannual architecture after the 2000 reconstruction.

This is therefore a stronger disappearance criterion than lexical disappearance.

A problem/mechanism can disappear when a previously obligatory relation among:

- measurement;
- target;
- actor responsibility;
- reporting;
- correction / explanation;

is no longer institutionally required—even though all of the component nouns remain in circulation.

## 14. Hindsight risks

### 14.1 Do not narrate a simple monetarism → post-monetarism victory story

The June transcript is more complicated.

Broaddus and Poole can regard money targets as operationally weak while still valuing the discipline / inflation-accountability function attached to them.

Greenspan can reject current range-setting without saying monetary causation is irrelevant.

The historical problem is a reorganization of measurement and accountability, not a clean ideological substitution.

### 14.2 Do not retroactively call §2B a modern “dual-mandate report” and stop there

That label hides what was removed.

The useful comparison is clause-level:

```text
old observatory
+ range object
+ deviation explanation

versus

new observatory
- range object
- deviation explanation
```

### 14.3 Do not convert Gramm’s “outdated” into an objective research verdict

His statement proves a legislative actor’s reason for deletion.

It does not settle the empirical debate over monetary aggregates.

### 14.4 Do not infer Fed → Congress causation from agreement

The June FOMC discussion and December statute align closely.

That is not enough to prove that Greenspan, Kohn, or Fed staff drafted §1003 or caused Gramm’s formulation.

Direct correspondence, draft circulation, committee staff records, or sponsor statements are still needed.

### 14.5 Do not mistake a surviving first sentence of §2A for survival of the whole 1978 accountability architecture

The law deliberately strikes everything after that sentence.

Clause survival is therefore dimension-specific.

```text

+ monetary/credit ranges

+ employment/inflation/economic observatory

        ↓

1994
ranges remain legally required
but are recast as long-run benchmarks
rather than current-policy guides

        ↓

late 1990s
formal reporting continues
operational range role is already weak

        ↓

May 2000

        ↓

        ↓
```

The best present judgment is therefore:

> **This is not a case where the policy problem, the data, and the institution disappear together. It is a case where a specific intermediate target/accountability relation loses operational credibility, becomes contested as an institutional anchor, passes through legal sunset, and is then deliberately omitted when the surrounding oversight system is rebuilt.**

That is a much stronger Problem History disappearance case than simple vocabulary loss.
