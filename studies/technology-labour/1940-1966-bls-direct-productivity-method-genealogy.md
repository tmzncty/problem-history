# 1940–1966 BLS direct-productivity measurement: inherited infrastructure, changing problem

Status: **M2 source packet / methodological genealogy / not a frozen Problem Episode**

Pilot: technology–labour

This packet follows a direct source lead left by `1966-crossman-technology-skill-manpower-measurement.md`.

Crossman, Laner, Davis, and Caplan explicitly say in 1966 that their skill-profile procedure was **based broadly** on Bureau of Labor Statistics `direct productivity` studies of the late 1940s and early 1950s. That statement gives the repository something unusually valuable: an **actor-recognized methodological genealogy**.

The question here is narrower than a general history of U.S. productivity statistics:

> What problem did the BLS direct-productivity apparatus actually organize in the 1940s–early 1950s, and what changed when Crossman et al. reused that apparatus for technological-change / skill / manpower research in 1966?

The main result is that the recovered genealogy is real, but it does **not** support simple problem continuity.

A provisional reconstruction is:

```text
1940 continuing statutory mandate
for productivity + labor-cost studies
        ↓
1941 defense / post-defense resource problem
        ↓
1947 postwar Direct Productivity Reports
product-level unit man-hours + explanatory factors
        ↓
1948 machine-tools update:
unit labor can change because of volume / reconversion /
production organization, not only technology
        ↓
1966 Crossman et al.
explicitly inherit the direct-productivity technique
but redesign it to isolate technological causation
and measure skill/manpower effects
```

This yields several method fixtures:

```text
research-mandate continuity
!= problem continuity

measurement-infrastructure continuity
!= causal-object continuity

same metric
!= same explanandum

actor-recognized methodological inheritance
!= inherited problem identity
```

## 1. Source control and evidence layers

### 1.1 1940 statutory authorization — direct legal primary source

**Joint Resolution, 7 June 1940, ch. 267, 54 Stat. 249**, now codified at 29 U.S.C. § 2b.

Official U.S. Code text:

- <https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=2023&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-2023-title29-section2b>

Convenient Cornell LII text:

- <https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/2b>

The resolution authorizes and directs BLS to make **continuing studies of productivity and labor costs** in manufacturing, mining, transportation, distribution, and other industries. The original enactment also required an annual report to Congress; that reporting clause was repealed in 1954.

Evidence strength: **high / direct statutory primary evidence**.

Boundary: the law creates a durable research responsibility. It does not itself prove that every later BLS productivity study asked the same problem, used the same causal theory, or retained the same policy stakes.

### 1.2 FY1941 Secretary of Labor annual report — contemporaneous administrative actor statement

**Twenty-Ninth Annual Report of the Secretary of Labor for the Fiscal Year Ended June 30, 1941**, especially the section `Productivity and Technological Changes`, printed p. 88.

GovInfo record:

- <https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/GOVPUB-L-45bde0cf4832dac12e23fb02bdbf9278>

The report states that the Productivity and Technological Developments Division had been created under congressional authorization during the preceding year. It frames increasing labor productivity as part of the problem of relating **human and material resources** and says productivity information had immediate importance under the defense program and would matter again for post-defense readjustment.

Evidence strength: **high / contemporaneous official primary source**.

Boundary: this is Department-level administrative formulation. It must not be retrojected as the motive of every individual BLS investigator, union, employer, or legislator involved in the 1940 authorization.

### 1.3 `The BLS Program for 1947-48` — contemporaneous postwar programme statement

**Monthly Labor Review**, October 1947, `The BLS Program for 1947-48`, pp. 409–414.

FRASER full text:

- <https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/monthly-labor-review-6130/october-1947-610261/fulltext>

The programme statement emphasizes product specification and comparison by factors including:

- plant size;
- capacity utilization;

### 1.4 FY1947 Secretary of Labor annual report — official corroboration of the Direct Productivity Reports launch

GovInfo scan/search record for the Department's annual report describes BLS field work for a new series of **Direct Productivity Reports** based on company records rather than the usual aggregate secondary production/man-hour data.

GovInfo PDF:

The report says the new approach collected output and man-hours for specified products from representative companies together with information on technological and other factors affecting productivity. Evidence strength: **high / official contemporaneous programme description**.

### 1.5 Kaplan 1948 machine-tools update — direct example of how the method explained a measured change

Benjamin D. Kaplan, **`Unit Man-Hour Requirements, Selected Machine Tools, 1939-47`**, *Monthly Labor Review* 67(6), December 1948, pp. 615–617.

FRASER PDF:

- <https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/bls_mlr/bls_mlr_194812.pdf>

The article identifies itself as the first current-update series for industries previously studied under the BLS Direct Productivity Reports programme.

It is especially valuable because it shows what the metric did **not** mean automatically. Unit man-hours rose after 1945, and Kaplan attributes the change principally to lower production volume and reconversion conditions: falling demand, increased competitive pressure, greater design/processing care, personnel retained above current requirements, and modification of wartime standardized mass-production methods.

Evidence strength: **high / contemporaneous primary BLS research article**.

Boundary: this source demonstrates one concrete explanatory use of direct-productivity measurement. It does not prove that every report in the programme used the same causal decomposition.

### 1.6 BLS Bulletin 1226 (1958), `Productivity: A Bibliography` — official retrospective map of the report family

U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, **Productivity: A Bibliography**, Bulletin No. 1226, 1958.

FRASER PDF:

- <https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/bls/bls_1226_1958.pdf>

Printed pp. 40–41 list the direct-productivity report family and supplements. The entries include, among others:

- construction machinery, 1939–45, with later supplements;
- machine tools, 1939–45, with updates through 1949–50;
- metal-forming machinery, 1939–49;
- industrial equipment, 1939–45, with supplements;

Evidence strength: **high for publication genealogy / official BLS retrospective bibliography**.

Boundary: a 1958 bibliography is not a substitute for each underlying report's actor problem. It establishes the report series and its stated measurement design, not a single shared theory across all reports.

### 1.7 Crossman et al. 1966 — direct actor-recognized inheritance and redesign

Edward R. F. W. Crossman, Stephen Laner, Louis E. Davis, and Stanley H. Caplan,
**Evaluation of Changes in Skill-Profile and Job-Content Due to Technological Change: Methodology and Pilot Results from the Banking, Steel and Aerospace Industries**, U.S. Department of Labor Contract No. 81-04-05, October 1966.

ERIC scan:

- <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED015326.pdf>

The summary of methodology says the new skill-profile method was based broadly on BLS direct-productivity studies of the late 1940s and early 1950s.

Crossman then adds requirements that are crucial to the new explanandum:

- direct labor man-hours per unit are distributed across skill levels;
- old and new processes are matched on product / material characteristics;
- transient changeover effects are excluded by using steady-state processes;
- matched process comparisons are replicated in different firms;
- statistical analysis is used to distinguish technological-level effects from skill, firm, and interaction effects.

The authors explicitly say the controls are required if the comparison is to reveal manpower and skill differences **due to process technology rather than irrelevant differences**.

Evidence strength: **very high / direct primary research report / actor-recognized methodological genealogy**.

## 2. 1940 creates a continuing research mandate, not a timeless problem

The 7 June 1940 resolution is unusually useful for separating **institutional continuity** from **problem continuity**.

It establishes a durable federal obligation:

```text
BLS
→ continuing studies
→ productivity + labor costs
→ across multiple industries
```

That is a strong administrative and statutory genealogy.

But the law is broad. It does not define one enduring question such as:

```text
Does technology destroy jobs?
```

Nor does it require one permanent causal model of productivity.

The same statutory authorization can support research that, at different moments, is organized around:

- unemployment and displacement;
- defense production and scarce-resource utilization;
- postwar reconversion;
- product-level unit labor requirements;
- productivity comparison;
- later technological-change / manpower analysis.

Therefore:

```text
same statutory mandate
!= same historical problem
```

This is stronger than a generic methodological warning because the later programme actually remains under a recognizable BLS productivity lineage while its policy environment and research questions change.

## 3. 1941: productivity is re-situated inside defense and post-defense resource allocation

The FY1941 Secretary of Labor report gives a contemporaneous formulation at the moment the new Productivity and Technological Developments Division is being described.

The report does not organize the object only around unemployment caused by labor-saving machinery. Instead it places labor productivity inside a larger relation between **human and material resources**.

The immediate stakes are now:

```text
national defense transformation
+
knowledge of worker / industry productivity
+
future post-defense readjustment
```

This is important because the repository already has a 1930s chain in which productivity and technology were investigated against mass unemployment, reemployment opportunities, worker displacement, and indirect technological unemployment.

The 1941 report shows that even before the direct-productivity report series appears, the administrative research object has acquired a new policy setting.

### What this proves

It proves that by 1941 the Department publicly justified the productivity programme through defense and anticipated post-defense readjustment as well as longer-running interest in productivity.

### What this does not prove

It does **not** prove:

```text
1937 technological-unemployment problem
→ solved
→ replaced by defense productivity
```

Nor does it prove that every earlier research concern disappeared.

The safer relation is:

```text
shared / inherited research infrastructure
+
changed policy stakes and use context
```

Whether the problem itself should be classified `reformulated`, `displaced`, `nested`, or something else requires a tighter pairwise actor comparison.

## 4. 1947: the postwar Direct Productivity Reports define a measurement object

By October 1947, BLS describes a distinct postwar direct-data programme.

The critical contrast is methodological:

```text
older aggregate / secondary-source productivity indexes
vs
establishment records for specified products
```

The new reports collect and compare product-level labor input and organize variation by observable dimensions such as plant size, production methods, capacity utilization, and region.

The direct reports therefore make the research object more granular:

```text
specified product
+
unit man-hours
+
plant / method / utilization conditions
+
industry-economic context
```

The programme statement presents this as a more useful way to measure productivity change than relying only on broad secondary aggregates.

### Actor problem reconstructed conservatively

A safe reconstruction is:

> How can BLS measure changes in labor required to produce specified products with enough establishment-level context to distinguish meaningful productivity trends from differences in production conditions?

This is **not yet** Crossman's 1966 question.

The 1947 programme does not, in the evidence recovered here, make its central task:

> Hold nontechnological factors constant so that the causal effect of a new technology on skill requirements can be estimated.

That distinction matters.

## 5. The 1948 machine-tools case: the same unit-labor metric can move for nontechnological reasons

Kaplan's December 1948 machine-tools update is the strongest concrete result in this packet.

Measured unit man-hours increased between the end of the war and 1947. A naive retrospective reading could treat this as a `productivity decline` and then look for a technological explanation.

The BLS article instead interprets the movement through postwar production conditions.

It points to:

- sharply lower output volume;
- declining demand after 1945;
- greater competition, encouraging more care in design and processing;
- firms holding labor above immediate production requirements;
- retreat from some wartime standardized mass-production methods.

The article says the rise in unit man-hours from 1945 to 1947 was traceable to lower output volume.

This yields an unusually good historical control for the 1966 Crossman redesign.

The older BLS metric is:

```text
man-hours per unit product
```

But in 1948 BLS itself demonstrates that movement in that metric can be caused by a bundle of conditions:

```text
demand / production volume
+
capacity use
+
product / processing choices
+
retained labor
+
production organization
+
technology / technique
```

Therefore:

```text
same unit-labor metric
!= technology effect
```

This is not a later econometric criticism imposed on the source. It is visible in the BLS researchers' own explanation of their postwar data.

## 6. 1958 bibliography: the direct-report family is an actual research infrastructure

The 1958 BLS bibliography confirms that the Direct Productivity Reports were not a single isolated machine-tools experiment.

Across several machinery branches, BLS repeatedly published and updated measurements of man-hours per unit, often carrying the series from the 1939 baseline through the late 1940s and into 1949–50.

This gives the later Crossman statement an identifiable institutional referent.

When Crossman says his method is based broadly on late-1940s / early-1950s BLS direct-productivity studies, there really is a report family with stable operational features:

```text
specified products
+
direct plant reports
+
unit labor requirements
+
plant / production-method / utilization comparisons
```

But the bibliography does not establish a stable `problem` merely by showing a stable report format.

A report series can preserve:

- variables;
- forms;
- comparison practices;
- personnel routines;
- institutional authority;

while the **reason for measuring them** changes.

This is exactly the distinction the repository needs to preserve.

## 7. 1966 Crossman: inherited method becomes a causal-identification and skill-distribution problem

Crossman's 1966 methodological summary makes the genealogy explicit and then immediately changes what the method is required to do.

The inherited direct-productivity backbone is still recognizable:

```text
unit product
→ direct labor man-hours
→ comparison across processes / establishments
```

But Crossman adds a new dimension:

```text
man-hours
→ distributed by skill level
→ skill profile
```

and a stronger causal requirement:

```text
older process vs newer process
must be matched so that
product specifications / raw materials / transient changeover /
firm variation
are not mistaken for the effect of technology
```

Replication and analysis of variance are then used to test differences associated with technological level.

The research question has therefore shifted from measuring / explaining productivity change to estimating something more specific:

```text
technology level
→ direct labor requirement
→ distribution of skill requirements
→ manpower / training / forecasting consequences
```

This is a **research re-specification**, not merely a new application label pasted onto an unchanged indicator.

## 8. Pairwise identity check: 1947–48 BLS vs 1966 Crossman

### 8.1 Continuity evidence

Strong continuity exists at the level of research infrastructure and method family:

1. Crossman explicitly names BLS direct-productivity studies as a methodological basis.
2. Both use direct labor input per unit of product / process as a core measurement object.
3. Both prefer disaggregated product/process evidence to reliance on economy-wide aggregates.
4. Both recognize that establishment / process conditions matter to interpretation.
5. Both use comparison across production settings rather than treating one aggregate productivity number as self-explanatory.

This is enough for a high-confidence **methodological genealogy**.

### 8.2 Discontinuity evidence

The problem structure is materially different.

#### Target / explanandum

**1947–48 BLS direct reports**

```text
unit man-hour trends
+
productivity differences
+
factors affecting those trends
```

**Crossman 1966**

```text
causal effect of technological change
on labor requirements + skill distribution
+
manpower adjustment / prediction
```

#### Skill

The recovered postwar BLS programme description does not make a quantitative skill-profile distribution the central dependent variable.

Crossman does.

#### Causal isolation

The 1948 BLS article explains observed changes through contextual factors and production conditions.

Crossman designs matched comparisons precisely to prevent product, firm, changeover, and other variation from being attributed to technology.

#### Policy role

The direct-productivity programme is organized as continuing productivity measurement and industry analysis.

Crossman is issued under MDTA and tied to manpower projection, training/adjustment, and structural-mismatch questions.

### 8.3 Provisional relation verdict

Do **not** freeze a standard Problem Episode edge from the method alone.

The strongest current statement is:

```text
methodological inheritance = high / direct / actor-recognized
research-infrastructure continuity = high
problem identity = not established
problem repurposing / reformulation = strong researcher hypothesis
```

If a relation type is later needed, `reformulated` or a method-specific non-problem edge may fit better than `continuous`, but the schema should not be expanded on this single case.

## 9. Connection to the 1930s chain: a stronger bridge, still not a frozen problem lineage

The repository already contains a 1932–1937 NBER/BLS/WPA/National Research Project chain around technological unemployment, productivity, worker displacement, and reemployment.

Current BLS institutional history says:

- BLS sought WPA funds for productivity studies in 1935;
- BLS cooperated with the WPA National Research Project;
- Congress authorized continuing productivity studies in 1940;
- the wartime programme shifted toward scarce-labor / defense requirements;
- postwar industry studies resumed and included direct employer data.

The 1958 BLS bibliography also preserves older nearby items, including Witt Bowden's **Technological Changes and Employment in the Electric-Lamp Industry** (BLS Bulletin 593, 1933), described as examining the problems of estimating technological-change effects on employment.

These facts make a **research-infrastructure bridge** from the 1930s into the postwar BLS programme increasingly plausible.

But this packet deliberately stops short of declaring:

```text
1933 / 1937 problem
→ 1940 mandate
→ 1947 Direct Productivity Reports
→ 1966 Crossman
= one continuous problem
```

The missing step is not chronology. It is **problem identity**.

Primary legislative history for the 1940 resolution, wartime programme records, and the original direct-productivity report introductions are still needed to see which earlier questions were explicitly retained, suspended, or repurposed.

## 10. Conditions of problem reorientation visible in the source chain

The sources suggest several historically specific conditions under which the same measurement apparatus could be attached to a different problem.

### 10.1 Institutional authorization survives changing policy environments

The 1940 resolution authorizes continuing studies broadly enough that the same office lineage can survive different national priorities.

### 10.2 Defense changes the stakes of productivity knowledge

The 1941 annual report explicitly places productivity information in the defense transformation and anticipated post-defense adjustment.

### 10.3 Postwar reconversion makes utilization / output conditions analytically unavoidable

The 1948 machine-tools data show that unit labor changes can reflect demand and volume rather than simple technological progress.

### 10.4 Federal manpower policy later demands causal and skill-specific prediction

By 1966, MDTA-era research does not merely need to know that labor requirements changed. It needs evidence about **which technological changes alter which skills and manpower requirements**.

That policy need helps explain why Crossman takes an inherited productivity technique and adds matching, skill scales, replication, and causal controls.

### Researcher-side synthesis

A cautious researcher formulation is:

```text
measurement infrastructure persists
because institutions, mandates, and metrics persist;

problem formulation changes
because policy stakes and required decisions change.
```

This is not actor vocabulary and should remain labelled as researcher analysis.

## 11. What this adds to the disappearance / transformation problem

This case offers a different kind of non-disappearance test than the OMAT→OMPER office-name episode.

Here the **measurement apparatus** survives while the problem role changes.

Therefore:

```text
method survives
!= problem survives unchanged
```

and the inverse also matters:

```text
problem vocabulary changes
!= all older research infrastructure disappears
```

A historical problem can lose one organizing form while leaving behind:

- datasets;
- survey designs;
- office expertise;
- legal authority;
- comparison techniques;
- publication series.

Those remnants can then be recruited into a later problem.

This is a concrete mechanism by which historical genealogy and problem identity can diverge.

## 13. Hindsight risks

### 13.1 Productivity is not a timeless single problem

A continuous `productivity` label can cover unemployment/displacement, defense resource use, industrial efficiency, reconversion, labor-cost measurement, growth, or manpower forecasting.

### 13.2 Unit man-hours are not a transparent technology variable

Kaplan's 1948 interpretation directly demonstrates that demand, output volume, staffing, and production organization can move the metric.

Do not rewrite the direct reports as if they were already estimating a clean causal `technology coefficient`.

### 13.3 Better measurement does not imply later causal design was already latent

Crossman's 1966 matching and statistical control should not be projected backward into the 1947 programme merely because both use direct plant/product evidence.

### 13.4 Statutory continuity is not conceptual continuity

29 U.S.C. §2b is excellent evidence that the federal research responsibility persisted. It is poor evidence, by itself, that the historical problem remained identical.

### 13.5 Institutional history is not actor testimony

Current BLS history is valuable for locating WPA/BLS and wartime transitions, but primary records must control claims about what 1935, 1940, or wartime actors thought they were solving.

### 13.6 `Direct productivity` should not be silently equated with modern causal productivity research

It is a historically specific programme and publication label. Its methodological meaning must come from its own reports.

### 13.7 Crossman is not the whole 1966 federal problem field

The contract report is one research formulation near the National Commission and OMPER, not the Department's sole doctrine.

## 15. What remains uncertain

1. **1940 legislative genealogy.** The statute is direct, but the hearings / reports / union lobbying that converted 1930s productivity-displacement research into continuing statutory authority have not been directly recovered here.
2. **1937→1940 personnel and document transfer.** Later BLS institutional history says the bridge existed; this packet does not yet page-bind the primary administrative chain.
3. **Wartime transformation.** FY1941 gives direct evidence that defense and post-defense readjustment mattered, but a full wartime set of BLS productivity/labor-requirement programme documents has not been read.
4. **First 1947 machine-tools bulletin.** The June 1947 report is identified and summarized through later/current material, but this packet directly analyzes the December 1948 update rather than the full first bulletin.
5. **Variation across industries.** Machine tools may not represent how shoe, leather, industrial-equipment, construction-machinery, or mining-machinery studies explained unit-labor changes.
6. **Crossman citation specificity.** The 1966 report names the BLS study family broadly. It remains unclear which exact BLS bulletins the research team used operationally when designing the skill-profile method.
7. **Personnel continuity.** This packet does not establish a direct personnel chain from the 1930s BLS/WPA work to the Crossman project.
8. **Problem-edge label.** `reformulated` / `repurposed method` are plausible researcher descriptions, but no Problem Episode relation is frozen.

## Bottom line

The source debt left by the 1966 Crossman packet is now materially reduced.

Crossman et al.'s statement of methodological inheritance can be tied to a real BLS research infrastructure created under a continuing 1940 mandate and operationalized after the war through product-level **Direct Productivity Reports**.

The new evidence also shows why that genealogy must not be mistaken for problem identity.

In the 1947–48 programme, unit man-hours are a productivity measurement whose movement is interpreted through plant, utilization, demand, reconversion, production-method, and other contextual factors. The 1948 machine-tools case explicitly attributes a postwar rise in unit labor largely to lower output and reconversion conditions.

By 1966, Crossman takes that measurement family and redesigns it for a different burden of proof: isolate the effect of **technological level** on labor requirements and **skill distribution** closely enough to support manpower prediction and adjustment policy.

The strongest supported relation is therefore:

```text
BLS direct-productivity infrastructure
        ↓ actor-recognized methodological inheritance
Crossman skill-profile method

with

research-infrastructure continuity = strong
methodological genealogy = direct
problem identity = not established
causal object / policy use = materially re-specified
```

This gives the technology–labour pilot a concrete historical example in which a technique and statutory research lineage survive while the question that the technique is being asked to answer changes.
