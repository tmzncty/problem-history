# 1962–1963 OAM → OMAT → comprehensive manpower-program transition

Status: **M2 institutional-transition source packet / not a frozen Problem Episode**

Pilot: technology–labour

This packet follows the repository's 1961 OAM and retraining→manpower work. Its purpose is narrower than a general history of the Manpower Development and Training Act (MDTA): establish what can be proved about the administrative and problem-structural transition after OAM, without treating office names or bureaucratic succession as automatic evidence of problem continuity.

The main result is a three-stage contemporaneous administrative chain:

```text
March 1962
OAM remains an explicitly supervised Department of Labor office
under the Assistant Secretary for Employment and Manpower

        ↓ official DOL evidence, but exact June transition still partly unresolved

June 1962
OAM abolished; its functions assigned to newly created OMAT
(official FY1962 Department report; page-image binding still pending)

        ↓

April 1963
Secretary's Order 4-63 cancels Order 17-62 but explicitly says
existing offices remain while their functions/responsibilities are modified;
OMAT is embedded in a "comprehensive Manpower Program"
```

This gives direct evidence for **administrative persistence + function transfer + later explicit functional modification**. It does **not** by itself prove that one stable historical problem persisted across the three offices/orders.

---

## 1. Why this advances the problem-history question

The existing source note had already established a source-critical problem:

- Department of Labor chronology: OAM created by General Order No. 110 on 20 April 1961;
- National Archives administrative history: OAM established by Secretary's Order 5-61 on 25 October 1961;
- NARA then says OAM was superseded by the Office of Manpower, Automation, and Training (OMAT) under Secretary's Order 17-62 on 15 June 1962.

What remained missing was positive evidence about the **transition conditions**. In particular:

1. Did OAM demonstrably remain an operating organizational unit after October 1961?
2. Did contemporary Department evidence describe the June 1962 change as mere renaming, abolition, transfer of functions, or something else?
3. Did the next administrative reorganization preserve the office while changing its functions?
4. By 1963, what object did the Department explicitly say the manpower apparatus was governing?

New contemporaneous sources answer the first and third questions directly, materially strengthen the second, and answer the fourth in considerable detail.

## 2. March 1962: OAM is still an explicit operating unit

### Primary source

U.S. Department of Labor, **Secretary's Order No. 6-62**, dated 9 March 1962, effective 12 March 1962, published in the *Federal Register*, 26 May 1962, p. 4977, under the heading **"Top Staff in Department of Labor—Delegation of Authority and Assignment of Responsibilities."**

Federal Register daily issue:

<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1962-05-26/pdf/FR-1962-05-26.pdf>

Page image checked 15 September 2026 (UTC+8), printed p. 4977.

### What the order establishes

The order assigns the **Assistant Secretary for Employment and Manpower** responsibility for Department manpower activities and states that he shall supervise:

- the Office of Automation and Manpower;
- the Bureau of Employment Security;
- the Bureau of Apprenticeship and Training.

This is important because it is later than the National Archives' October 1961 establishment date and only days before the MDTA became law on 15 March 1962.

The safest conclusion is therefore:

```text
Secretary's Order 5-61, 25 Oct 1961
cannot be read as evidence that the April-created OAM had ceased to exist.

By 12 Mar 1962, OAM was still an expressly supervised Department unit.
```

This does **not** explain the April→October relation. It simply prevents one possible false reconciliation of the two dates.

### Evidence strength

**High / direct administrative evidence.**

Boundary: Order 6-62 tells us where OAM sat in the Department's chain of supervision. It does not supply a complete statement of OAM's substantive programme or prove what changed under Secretary's Order 5-61.

## 3. June 1962: official Department evidence describes abolition + transfer of functions

### Primary / official source

U.S. Department of Labor, *Annual Report, 1962* (GovInfo digital carrier):

<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-L-abcf8df506bbc89385b4db8e9a000d38/pdf/GOVPUB-L-abcf8df506bbc89385b4db8e9a000d38.pdf>

The official searchable record reports that:

- Seymour L. Wolfbein was named Director of OAM on 5 February 1962;
- OAM was **abolished by Secretary's Order of 15 June 1962**;
- its functions were assigned to the newly created **Office of Manpower, Automation, and Training**;
- the corresponding OMAT position became effective 1 July 1962.

### What this adds beyond the NARA lineage

The existing NARA administrative history supports:

```text
OAM → superseded by OMAT
```

The Department's own FY1962 account adds a more specific administrative claim:

```text
OAM abolished
+
functions assigned to newly created OMAT
```

That is stronger evidence of **function transfer** than a successor-office name alone.

### Evidence boundary

**High as official Department evidence, but exact quotation/page binding remains pending.**

The full GovInfo PDF was not stably page-renderable in this run. Until the scan page is checked directly, do not freeze wording that depends on punctuation or a precise printed-page locator. The proposition is strong enough for the source map and transition hypothesis, but the signed Secretary's Order 17-62 remains the best target for exact functional comparison.

Most importantly:

```text
functions transferred
!=
all functions unchanged
!=
problem identity preserved
```

The transfer establishes administrative inheritance, not the identity of the problem being governed.

## 4. April 1963: institutional persistence with explicit functional modification

### Primary source

U.S. Department of Labor, **Secretary's Order No. 4-63**, published in the *Federal Register*, 25 April 1963, under **"Delegation of Authority and Assignment of Responsibilities Under the Department's Manpower Program."**

Federal Register daily issue:

<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1963-04-25/pdf/FR-1963-04-25.pdf>

Page images checked 15 September 2026 (UTC+8), especially printed pp. 4111–4112.

### A direct method fixture: order cancelled, office remains, functions modified

Order 4-63 explicitly cancels a group of earlier orders including **Secretary's Order 17-62**. It then states that the bureaus and offices established under those cancelled orders **remain in existence**, while their **functions and responsibilities are modified** as provided by the new order.

This is unusually useful evidence because the historical actor's own administrative instrument distinguishes three things that are often collapsed in later summaries:

```text
administrative order continuity
!=
office continuity
!=
functional continuity
```

Here the prior order ends, the office survives, and functions are explicitly modified.

That gives the repository a real-source counterexample to any rule that would infer problem identity from institutional persistence.

### Evidence strength

**High / direct / page-bound primary administrative source.**

## 5. What the 1963 Department explicitly calls the governed object

Order 4-63 does more than rearrange boxes on an organization chart. Its background section says the Department needs a **comprehensive Manpower Program** capable of meeting manpower problems generated by a changing and expanding economy and by national emergencies.

The programme is organized around several linked knowledge/action objects.

### Manpower forecasting and research

The Department is to estimate:

- manpower supply;
- manpower requirements;
- unmet manpower requirements;
- probable labor-market, industrial, and general economic developments affecting manpower and employment.

This makes **anticipatory knowledge** an explicit part of the policy problem.

### Manpower development

The programme includes:

- retraining employed or underemployed workers whose skills are becoming obsolete;
- retraining unemployed workers;
- preparing new generations for occupations expected to exist in the future.

Training is therefore not one stable remedy for one stable problem. It answers multiple populations and temporal horizons.

### Manpower distribution

The Department treats occupational choice, labor-market exchange, and placement as distinct parts of the manpower problem.

### Manpower utilization

The programme includes fuller use of scarce skills, expansion of job opportunities, and fuller use of unemployed and underemployed workers.

### Manpower administration and programme support

The order assigns responsibilities for policy development, programme operation, training, testing/counseling, placement, determining occupational supply and demand, evaluation, and allocation of resources across programmes.

The governed object is thus no longer well described by a single question such as:

> Does automation cause unemployment?

A closer actor-grounded reconstruction is:

```text
What manpower will the economy require?
What skills and workers are available?
Where will shortages, obsolescence, displacement, and underutilization occur?
How can the Department anticipate, develop, distribute, and utilize manpower?
```

These are researcher paraphrases of the order's administrative structure, not quotations placed in historical actors' mouths.

## 6. Automation remains present, but its problem role changes

The manpower-research section of Order 4-63 instructs the Department to identify the dimensions of national manpower problems and to provide leadership in anticipating the **benefits and problems created by automation, technological progress, and other changes in the structure of production and demand**, evaluating their impact on national manpower resources and developing solutions through the operating bureaus.

This matters because it blocks two opposite hindsight stories.

### False story A: automation disappears into manpower

It does not. Automation is still named as something whose consequences require anticipation and evaluation.

### False story B: the old automation problem simply continues unchanged

The 1963 order embeds automation inside a broader object that also includes:

- technological progress generally;
- changes in production and demand;
- labor-force supply;
- future requirements;
- occupational shortages;
- skill obsolescence;
- worker development;
- distribution and mobility;
- utilization and underemployment.

Therefore the best current hypothesis is **problem-role migration**, not disappearance and not simple identity:

```text
named automation/employment concern
        ↓ institutional inheritance
manpower + automation + training apparatus
        ↓ explicit functional modification
comprehensive manpower programme
in which automation is one causal input / research object
among wider production, demand, supply, skill, mobility,
and utilization problems
```

The exact point at which this reformulation occurred remains open. It may have been partly present in OAM before June 1962, written into Order 17-62, developed during MDTA implementation, or formalized by the 1963 reorganization. Without Order 17-62 and programme records, do not assign the transformation to a single date.

## 9. Hindsight and source-critical risks

1. **Office succession = problem continuity.** Transfer of functions proves institutional inheritance, not one unchanged target/stakes/causal model.
2. **Renaming = problem transformation.** `OAM` → `OMAT` is an archival clue; only functional/problem evidence can establish transformation.
3. **Cancelled order = abolished office.** Order 4-63 explicitly supplies the opposite case: an order can be cancelled while offices remain and functions are modified.
4. **Function transfer = function identity.** The FY1962 report says functions moved; it does not prove no functions were added, removed, or reweighted.
5. **1963 comprehensive manpower programme = 1961 OAM's original formulation.** That is backprojection unless intermediate evidence supports it.
6. **Automation nested inside manpower = automation problem disappeared.** Order 4-63 still names automation explicitly.
7. **Automation remains named = same automation problem survives.** Its administrative role has changed: it appears as one causal/research input among a wider manpower field.
8. **MDTA enactment automatically caused the June reorganization.** Chronology is suggestive, but direct causal language is still needed.
9. **A bureaucracy's category = workers' own problem formulation.** The manpower programme is a federal administrative object, not a transparent representation of displaced workers' language.

## 11. Remaining uncertainties

The most important missing document is still **Secretary's Order 17-62 (15 June 1962)** itself. Until it is recovered, we cannot state exactly:

- which OAM functions were transferred unchanged;
- which new functions were added to OMAT;
- whether the order explicitly cites MDTA or Area Redevelopment Act responsibilities;
- whether `training` in the new office title reflected a substantive jurisdictional expansion or only consolidation;
- how authority was divided among OMAT, BES, BAT, HEW, and other units at the moment of transition.

The exact text of **Secretary's Order 5-61 (25 October 1961)** also remains necessary to explain the April/October establishment-date discrepancy.

Finally, the FY1962 annual-report passage must still be bound to its scan page before exact quotation is frozen.

## 13. Current provisional judgement

The strongest new conclusion is not that the same automation problem became the manpower problem.

It is narrower and better supported:

> By March 1962, OAM was still an explicitly supervised Department of Labor unit. Contemporary Department evidence then describes OAM as abolished in June 1962 with its functions assigned to a newly created OMAT. In April 1963, Secretary's Order 4-63 cancelled the order that had established OMAT while explicitly retaining the office and modifying its functions, embedding it within a comprehensive manpower programme organized around forecasting, development, distribution, utilization, research, and administration. Automation remained a named concern, but now occupied one causal/research role inside a broader manpower field.

For Problem History, this provides positive evidence that **institutional inheritance can coexist with explicit functional modification and a changed problem role**. The surviving administrative chain therefore strengthens historical genealogy while simultaneously weakening any inference of simple problem identity.
