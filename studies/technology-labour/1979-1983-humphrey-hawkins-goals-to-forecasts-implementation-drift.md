# 1979–83: Humphrey–Hawkins goals, forecasts, timetable deferral, and implementation drift

Status: **M2 forward implementation / soft-disappearance test / not a frozen Problem Episode**

Pilot: technology–labour

This packet continues:

- `1946-1978-full-employment-revival-and-redefinition.md`;
- `1974-1976-hawkins-job-right-to-balanced-growth-reformulation.md`;
- `1975-march-h50-layered-job-right-and-national-purposes-budget.md`.

The repo already has strong evidence that the Full Employment and Balanced Growth Act of 1978 explicitly strengthened and supplemented the Employment Act of 1946 while simultaneously redefining the governed field around unemployment, inflation, productivity, structural disadvantage, monetary policy, and balanced growth.

The next Problem History question is not simply whether the 1978 Act was implemented.

It is narrower:

> Once the Act's numerical full-employment and price-stability goals entered annual Executive and Federal Reserve reporting, did they function as policy commitments that should force corrective action, or did they become forecasts / planning reference points whose nonattainment no longer generated the same obligation to change policy?

This matters for disappearance analysis because the statute, vocabulary, reports, committees, targets, and institutional machinery can all survive while the operative relation between **observed divergence** and **required corrective action** weakens.

A provisional researcher-side description is:

**goal-to-forecast drift inside a surviving statutory observatory, with timetable deferral first authorized by the Act itself and the meaning of compliance becoming openly contested by 1983.**

That phrase is analytical. It is not actor vocabulary and it is not a frozen schema relation.

---

## 1. Why this is a high-value failure / disappearance test

A naïve disappearance model would search for a clear terminal event:

```text
law repealed
term disappears
agency abolished
reports stop
```

The 1979–83 Humphrey–Hawkins implementation history produces a harder case.

The legal and reporting skeleton remains visible:

```text
1978 Act
+ Economic Report
+ CEA
+ congressional review
+ Federal Reserve reporting
+ unemployment / inflation goals
```

But the operative policy relation becomes contested:

```text
numerical goal
        ↓
actual / forecast divergence
        ↓
must policy change enough to restore the path?

or

numerical goal
        ↓
forecast / projection / long-run reference
        ↓
policy can remain unchanged despite divergence?
```

If the second relation becomes dominant, the historical problem may be weakened without disappearing lexically or institutionally.

Research rule candidate:

```text
institutional persistence
!=
problem-governance persistence
```

and more specifically:

```text
numerical target survives
!=
target survives as a correction rule
```

## 3. 1979: the first implementation already separates short-term “goals” from medium-term statutory goal paths

The first Economic Report under the new Act does not treat every numerical figure in the same way.

The report describes the 1978 statute as creating an important new framework, enlarging the Employment Act of 1946, establishing fuller employment opportunity as a national objective, adding an explicit national commitment to reducing inflation, and imposing new reporting / coordination requirements on the President, Congress, and the Federal Reserve.

That is strong actor-side evidence that the Carter Administration did **not** interpret the statute as empty symbolism at the outset.

But the same report makes a distinction that is crucial for later implementation drift.

For 1979 and 1980, the report says the short-term economic goals **represent a forecast**.

For 1981–83, the figures are instead projections of the economic performance required to reach the statutory 1983 goals, including:

- overall unemployment of 4 percent;
- adult unemployment of 3 percent;
- CPI inflation of 3 percent.

This gives a layered first-year implementation:

```text
1979–80 figures
= short-run forecast / current-policy outlook

1981–83 figures
= path required to reach statutory medium-term goals
```

Therefore:

```text
“goal” vocabulary in a table
!=
all horizons have the same normative status
```

And, importantly:

```text
goal-to-forecast drift
cannot be narrated as something invented only by the Reagan Administration
```

The forecast treatment is already present in the first Carter implementation.

### Problem-History significance

The 1978 Act had strengthened explicit goal machinery. Yet its first Executive implementation immediately had to map statutory goals onto ordinary forecasting practice.

That mapping creates an ambiguity:

```text
forecast
= expected outcome under current / proposed policy

statutory goal
= outcome toward which policy should be adjusted
```

These are not the same object.

A forecast can be wrong without implying that policy violated its purpose. A goal can be missed in a way that creates an obligation to revise policy. Once the same reporting table contains both functions, Problem History should track whether actors continue to preserve that distinction.

## 4. 1980: Carter postpones the timetable, but does so through the statute's own contingency mechanism

The 1980 Economic Report is a second high-value test because the economy had changed sharply after enactment.

The Administration foregrounds:

- the second major oil-price shock;
- accelerated inflation;
- the risk that strong aggregate stimulus would intensify inflation;
- the risk that drastic restraint aimed at rapid disinflation would generate a deep and extended recession.

Carter states that the original combination of 4 percent unemployment and 3 percent inflation by 1983 is no longer practicable under the changed conditions.

He therefore uses the statute's authority to modify the timetable.

The new path postpones:

- the 4 percent unemployment target to 1985;
- the 3 percent inflation target to a later horizon, effectively three years after the unemployment target.

This should **not** be coded as straightforward abandonment.

The better actor-grounded structure is:

```text
statutory goals survive
        +
statute contains a revision mechanism
        +
Administration invokes that mechanism
        +
changed causal environment is offered as justification
```

Research fixture:

```text
statutory target deferral
!=
statutory target disappearance
```

### But the meaning of “short-term goal” continues to shift toward forecast

The 1980 report again says the short-term goals for 1980 and 1981 represent a forecast.

The medium-term figures remain projections of the performance required to reach the postponed statutory targets.

This means the 1980 architecture still preserves two layers:

```text
short horizon
forecast

medium horizon
required path toward statutory target
```

The problem has not yet collapsed into pure forecasting.

### Actor formulation: aggregate demand is no longer sufficient as the central answer space

The report also says that simultaneous progress on inflation and unemployment cannot come from aggregate-demand management alone. Supply-oriented and structural measures become more important.

This is consistent with the repo's earlier finding that by 1978 full employment was already embedded in a broader unemployment–inflation–productivity–structural-policy field.

The 1980 shock makes that causal re-specification operational rather than merely statutory.

Do not translate this into a researcher claim that “Keynesian full employment ended in 1980.”

The actor claim is narrower:

> changed oil / price conditions make the original timetable impracticable and make exclusive reliance on aggregate demand inadequate.

## 5. 1980 Federal Reserve reporting imports the Administration's forecast treatment into the interinstitutional observatory

The 1978 Act did more than require an annual Presidential report. It created a recurring reporting relationship involving Federal Reserve monetary plans and the Administration's employment / inflation outlook.

The first 1980 monetary-policy reporting material explicitly reproduces Administration short-term figures and warns that they should be viewed as **forecasts rather than expressions of desired outcomes**.

That wording is especially important.

The distinction has now moved from one Executive document into the Fed–Congress reporting interface.

So by 1980:

```text
Administration short-term “goals”
        ↓
described as forecasts
        ↓
Fed oversight material reproduces that status
```

This is stronger evidence of institutional practice than finding the word `forecast` in one table.

At the same time, the reporting machinery itself is functioning. Monetary-growth ranges, Administration economic projections, hearings, and comparison tables continue to be produced.

Hence:

```text
reporting compliance
!=
substantive goal-binding
```

and:

```text
observatory persistence
can coexist with
weaker corrective obligation
```

## 6. Do not collapse the Carter deferral and Reagan-era problem hierarchy into one event

By 1983, the Reagan Administration's Economic Report contains a different causal and normative ordering.

It calls unemployment the most serious current economic problem and acknowledges the extraordinary scale of joblessness after the 1981–82 recession.

But it also describes the inflation inherited at the beginning of the Administration as the most urgent problem that had to be reversed, and treats some slowdown / recession cost as probably unavoidable in restoring price stability.

The preferred route back toward employment growth is organized around:

- sustained recovery;
- private investment;
- saving and capital formation;
- reduced regulatory burdens;
- price stability;
- improved labor-market functioning for structural unemployment.

Cyclical unemployment is treated as severe but substantially reversible through recovery; structural unemployment is separately framed around labor-market matching / training / incentives and related mechanisms.

This is not the same causal hierarchy as the strongest 1974–78 job-right / timetable / last-resort-employment formulations.

But it is also not a simple case in which unemployment ceases to be a problem.

Research rule:

```text
priority reversal
!=
problem disappearance
```

The stronger candidate is a change in **problem hierarchy and causal ordering**:

```text
1970s sponsors:
full employment obligation / timetable
+ inflation constraint
+ structural policy

1983 Administration:
price stability / recovery / private investment
→ durable employment gains
with structural unemployment handled separately
```

That is a transformation candidate, not yet a frozen successor relation.

## 7. 1983 Joint Economic Committee: “goal” versus “forecast” becomes an explicit actor-level dispute

The 1983 Joint Economic Report provides the strongest evidence that the implementation ambiguity had become historically salient to actors themselves.

The committee majority argues that the Act's short-term goals should operate as anchors / beacons for policy and says that **two Administrations** since enactment had misconstrued them by presenting forecasts for which the Administration did not assume responsibility.

This is a crucial correction to a partisan or presidential-period narrative.

The JEC's complaint is not simply:

```text
Reagan abandoned Humphrey–Hawkins
```

It is:

```text
Carter + Reagan implementation practice
converted short-term goals toward forecasts
without adequate responsibility for attainment
```

That is an actor-level diagnosis of a change in the operative function of the statutory reporting object.

The committee's preferred relation is approximately:

```text
statutory objective
        ↓
short-term policy goal
        ↓
actual performance diverges
        ↓
policy should be reconsidered / changed
```

What it criticizes is closer to:

```text
statutory objective
        ↓
Administration forecast
        ↓
actual performance diverges
        ↓
forecast was wrong; policy responsibility is weaker
```

This is the best current primary evidence for what a researcher-side phrase like **goal-to-forecast drift** is trying to describe.

The phrase itself remains ours. The dispute is actors'.

## 8. Hawkins's 1983 additional views are stronger than the committee majority and must remain actor-specific

Representative Augustus Hawkins's additional views in the same 1983 Joint Economic Report go further.

He argues that the February 1983 Economic Report violates statutory mandates because it does not set out policies adequate to move unemployment and inflation to the numerical Humphrey–Hawkins levels by the statutory horizon.

He contrasts statutory figures with the Administration's much weaker forecast path and criticizes the absence of an alternative program capable of closing the gap.

This is extremely useful evidence for how a principal sponsor understood the relation among:

- numerical objective;
- timetable;
- policy plan;
- responsibility for correction.

But it is not neutral legal adjudication.

Do not write:

```text
Reagan legally violated Humphrey–Hawkins
```

as a settled repo fact from Hawkins alone.

Write instead:

```text
Hawkins argued that the Administration's forecast treatment
and policy path violated the Act's goal-setting mandate.
```

This actor separation is mandatory because later Federal Reserve institutional history gives a materially narrower reading of what the Act legally required from the Fed and of how binding the Administration's macroeconomic objectives were on monetary policy.

## 9. Hawkins's July 1983 retrospective statement reveals the intended answer structure he wanted to preserve

In the July 1983 House monetary-policy hearings, Hawkins returns to the design of the Act.

His retrospective explanation is unusually useful because he does **not** say Congress had prescribed one mandatory policy instrument.

His account is closer to:

```text
we specify the destination:
~4% unemployment
~3% inflation
within a timetable

President / Congress / Fed retain instrument choice

but policies should be harmonized enough to reach the destination
```

That is consistent with the earlier Humphrey–Hawkins evolution already recovered in the repo: the campaign increasingly loosened particular institutional remedies while preserving numerical / planning commitments.

It also clarifies the type of weakening Hawkins believed had occurred after enactment.

The alleged loss was not simply:

```text
wrong instrument chosen
```

It was:

```text
instrument discretion
becomes
outcome non-accountability
```

Again, this is Hawkins's 1983 retrospective formulation, not uncontested statutory meaning.

## 10. Later Federal Reserve history corroborates practice drift while disputing a stronger binding interpretation

David Lindsey's later Federal Reserve institutional history is useful precisely because it does two things at once.

First, it reconstructs the reporting regime and says that, over time, Administration short-term projections changed from a path toward the Act's numerical objectives into ordinary forecasts for the economy.

That strongly corroborates the practice change identified by the 1983 JEC.

Second, it emphasizes that the Act did not legally bind the Federal Reserve to make its macroeconomic objectives identical to the President's short- or medium-term objectives. On this reading, Congress sought coordination and avoidance of strongly contradictory directions rather than a single command hierarchy.

This produces a valuable source-critical pair:

```text
1983 JEC / Hawkins:
forecast practice weakens statutory accountability

later Fed reconstruction:
forecast drift is real,
but Fed was never simply bound to hit Presidential macro targets
```

Do not solve this by declaring one side “correct” without legal / legislative-history work.

Research fixture:

```text
later institutional history
can identify practice drift
!=
neutral adjudication of contemporaneous statutory dispute
```

## 11. Relation judgment: do not freeze one edge yet

### 11.1 1978 Act → 1979 first implementation

Current candidate: **`reformulated` / implementation specification**.

Why continuity is strong:

- direct statutory implementation;
- same Executive reporting machinery;
- same explicit 1983 numerical objectives;
- medium-term path still organized around reaching those objectives.

Why it is not simple continuity:

- short-term statutory “goals” are already operationalized as forecasts;
- reporting practice introduces a status distinction not obvious from lexical continuity alone.

### 11.2 1979 → 1980 timetable postponement

Current candidate: **`reformulated`**, not disappearance.

Reasons:

- Carter uses an authority built into the Act;
- the numerical goals survive;
- the causal environment is said to have changed through oil shock / inflation;
- the timetable is re-specified rather than eliminated.

Research fixture:

```text
authorized contingency mechanism
!=
problem abandonment
```

### 11.3 1980 → 1983

Current status: **undetermined / stronger transformation or implementation-decoupling candidate**.

Reasons for stronger transformation:

- Administration causal hierarchy changes;
- the original timetable loses practical centrality;
- JEC explicitly complains that goals have been converted into forecasts without responsibility for attainment;
- Hawkins says the statutory correction relation has been broken.

Reasons not to freeze:

- the Act remains in force;
- Economic Reports and Fed reports continue;

## 12. A new candidate failure mode: observatory survives while the correction rule weakens

The repo already has several warning fixtures such as:

```text
term disappears
!= problem disappears

agency disappears
!= research object disappears

measurement discontinuity
!= problem disappearance
```

This case supplies the reverse pattern:

```text
term survives
+ law survives
+ agency/report survives
+ measurement survives
!=
operative problem survives unchanged
```

A provisional researcher-side label is:

**procedural hollowing / commitment-to-observatory drift**.

Do not add this to the schema yet.

The phenomenon to preserve is narrower than the label:

> A governance system can keep observing, forecasting, reporting, and debating a problem while weakening the rule that observed failure should trigger sufficient policy correction.

That can be a form of **soft disappearance** of a problem's former answer obligation even when the problem remains fully askable as a statistic or policy topic.

This distinction may become useful across other episodes:

- pollution standards whose monitoring survives after enforcement changes;
- safety targets that become performance indicators rather than hard constraints;
- fiscal rules converted into forecasts;
- technological-risk reporting that survives after mandatory mitigation weakens.

No cross-domain generalization should be frozen until tested against additional episodes.

## 13. Fifty-year chain implications

The technology–labour pilot now has a long enough genealogy that the project should stop treating “full employment” as one continuous object.

A safer provisional chain is:

```text
1930s–40s
employment / displacement / absorption research
        ↓
1941–45
wartime labor-requirements problem
        ↓
1944
reconversion / released-labor forecasting
        ↓
1944–45
full-employment planning and aggregate-gap problem
        ↓
1946
Employment Act observatory / continuing-responsibility settlement
        ↓
1960s
automation / manpower / technological-adjustment research
        ↓
1974–76
job right + enforceability + national-planning campaign
        ↓
1978
full-employment revival inside inflation / productivity /
structural-policy / Fed-coordination field
        ↓
1979–80
short-term goals represented as forecasts
+ medium-term statutory path
+ authorized timetable deferral
        ↓
1983
open dispute over whether goal reporting still imposes
adequate policy-correction responsibility
```

What appears continuous across this chain is not one invariant problem.

More stable elements include:

- labor-market observation;
- aggregate forecasting;
- institutional responsibility debates;
- the repeated use of employment levels as a national-policy object.

Less stable elements include:

- harmed subject;
- causal diagnosis;
- desired direction of productivity change;
- policy trigger;
- enforcement / remedy;
- responsibility distribution;
- whether numerical values are forecasts, goals, thresholds, or correction rules.

That is exactly why pairwise identity tests remain necessary.

## 15. Hindsight risks

### Risk A: “Reagan killed Humphrey–Hawkins”

Too simple.

The first Carter report already calls short-term goals forecasts, and Carter postpones the timetable in 1980 using the statute's own authority.

### Risk B: “Carter violated the Act by postponing the targets”

Not supported by current evidence.

The postponement was presented as use of a statutory contingency mechanism. Whether the substantive justification was good policy is a different question.

### Risk C: “Forecast” is merely neutral terminology

Potentially misleading.

By 1983 the JEC explicitly treats forecast status as a problem because it weakens responsibility for attainment. Therefore the semantic / procedural distinction is historically actor-salient.

### Risk D: “The Fed ignored the law”

Too strong without further legal-history evidence.

Hawkins makes a strong accountability claim. Later Fed history reads the law more weakly. Preserve the dispute.

### Risk E: “Unemployment stopped being a problem under Reagan”

False to the actor evidence.

The 1983 Economic Report explicitly calls unemployment a very serious problem. What changes is the causal hierarchy, policy route, and relation to disinflation.

### Risk F: “If reports continue, the problem continues unchanged”

This is precisely what the episode tests against.

Observation can survive while answer obligation changes.

## 19. Provisional conclusion

The strongest result from 1979–83 is not that Humphrey–Hawkins immediately failed, nor that it remained intact until a later political repudiation.

The evidence instead shows a staged implementation problem:

```text
1978
explicit statutory goals + timetable + reporting system

1979
short-term figures already treated as forecasts,
while medium-term figures remain a path to statutory goals

1980
shock-driven, statute-authorized timetable deferral;
forecast / target dual structure survives

1983
Executive causal hierarchy has changed,
and Congress openly disputes whether “goals” have become
forecasts for which no one assumes sufficient responsibility
```

This produces a high-value Problem History distinction:

> **A problem's institutional observatory can survive while its former policy-correction relation becomes weaker, disputed, or partially hollowed out.**

That is more precise than either “the full-employment problem disappeared” or “the same Humphrey–Hawkins problem continued unchanged.”
