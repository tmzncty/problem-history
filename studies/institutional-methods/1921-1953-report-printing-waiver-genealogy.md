# 1921–1953: report-printing waiver genealogy and the separation of reporting from publication

Status: **M2 source-control / institutional-method candidate / not a frozen Problem Episode**

## 1. New finding

The repository's existing reporting packets begin their main report-pruning genealogy with the 1928 mass repeal of statutory reports. A separate but closely related institutional line begins earlier: Congress had already authorized executive departments to stop **printing** annual or special reports in order to stay within printing-and-binding appropriations.

The key distinction is crucial:

```text
report obligation
!=
printing / distribution obligation
```

The 1921–1953 mechanism did not generally repeal the underlying report or information-production duty. It authorized discontinuance of printing while requiring an original copy to remain available for public inspection.

## 2. Direct primary evidence: 1921

The Second Deficiency Act, fiscal year 1921, approved 16 June 1921, authorized heads of executive departments and Government establishments, in order to keep FY1922 printing and binding expenditures within appropriations, to discontinue printing annual or special reports under their jurisdiction. If printing was discontinued, the original copy had to remain on file for public inspection.

Primary source:
- 42 Stat. 64 (16 June 1921), GovInfo scan: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-42/pdf/STATUTE-42-Pg64.pdf

Evidence: **very high / enacted statute**.

A 1933 Public Printer retrospective states that this authorization was first included in an appropriation act for **fiscal year 1921** and thereafter repeated annually. The currently recovered direct statute is the June 1921 provision governing FY1922, so the exact FY1921 enactment remains source debt; the Public Printer's statement is strong contemporaneous institutional evidence that an earlier iteration existed.

## 3. Repetition became a durable administrative routine

Direct Statutes at Large evidence shows the same architecture in later annual appropriations. For example, the FY1925 and FY1926 provisions again authorized departments to discontinue printing annual/special reports while preserving originals for public inspection.

Primary examples:
- FY1925 provision, 43 Stat. 592: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-43/pdf/STATUTE-43-Pg578.pdf
- FY1926 provision, 43 Stat. 1301: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-43/pdf/STATUTE-43-Pg1286-2.pdf

Evidence: **very high** for repeated statutory use.

The 1933 Annual Report of the Public Printer makes the administrative history unusually explicit. It says the authority had been repeated annually since its first appearance for FY1921 and reports that departments had saved **more than $2,000,000** in annual-report printing over the preceding thirteen fiscal years.

Primary source:
- *Annual Report of the Public Printer, fiscal year ended June 30, 1933*, GovInfo scan/search result: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP-3c116e218db45ca98f0865a0e8589182/pdf/GOVPUB-GP-3c116e218db45ca98f0865a0e8589182.pdf

Evidence: **high / contemporaneous administrative retrospective**.

This supplies an actor-level outcome measure that the 1928 report-repeal packet did not have: the printing waiver was not merely formal statutory boilerplate; the Public Printer attributed substantial cumulative savings to its use.

## 4. Historical actor problem

The safe actor-level formulation is narrower than "Congress wanted fewer reports."

The statutes repeatedly state the problem as keeping **printing and binding expenditures within appropriations**. Their solution preserved the informational object while permitting its mass-production/distribution medium to disappear:

```text
report exists / may still be required
        ↓
printing cost presses against appropriation
        ↓
executive head may discontinue printing
        ↓
original copy remains on file
for public inspection
```

This is a different problem from the 1928 question of whether a recurring statutory report should exist at all.

## 5. 1939–1941: an observed use, not just enabling law

The Public Printer's FY1940 annual report reproduces the annual appropriation proviso and explains that, under pressure of work, the Government Printing Office proposed to use it to eliminate printing of the Public Printer's own annual report, while still preparing the information and keeping it on file for public inspection.

Primary source:
- *Annual Report of the Public Printer, fiscal year ended June 30, 1940*: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP-3cd63d7b6eacf6547906bba0ee832880/pdf/GOVPUB-GP-3cd63d7b6eacf6547906bba0ee832880.pdf

Evidence: **high / contemporaneous administrative use**.

This is a useful counterexample to any inference that disappearance of a printed report necessarily means disappearance of the underlying information-production practice.

## 6. 1953: annual proviso becomes permanent authority

Public Law 83-178, approved 1 August 1953, changed the temporal architecture. Section 103 removed the fiscal-year limitation by providing that department and establishment heads were **"hereafter"** authorized to discontinue printing annual or special reports in order to keep printing and binding expenditures within appropriations, again preserving originals for public inspection.

Primary source:
- Pub. L. 83-178, §103, 67 Stat. 332: https://www.congress.gov/83/statute/STATUTE-67/STATUTE-67-Pg318.pdf

Evidence: **very high / enacted statute**.

The provision later became the source of 44 U.S.C. §1117 when Title 44 was enacted as positive law in 1968. The current section still preserves the same basic distinction between discontinuing printing and retaining the original for public inspection.

Official codification:
- 44 U.S.C. §1117, GovInfo/House revision notes: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title44/html/USCODE-2024-title44.htm

## 7. Transformation chain

```text
FY1921/FY1922 era
annual appropriation waiver emerges
        ↓
1920s–1940s
repeated annually
printing may disappear
original information remains inspectable
        ↓
1928
parallel but different reform:
128 statutory reporting duties repealed
+ targeted congressional request mechanism
        ↓
1945
Congress explicitly revisits report-obligation pruning
and subjects proposed repeals to jurisdictional objection
        ↓
1953
printing waiver changes from annual proviso
into permanent "hereafter" authority
        ↓
1954
another package repeals statutory report duties,
including BLS productivity annual reporting,
while substantive research authority survives
        ↓
1968
permanent printing waiver codified as 44 U.S.C. §1117
```

This is not one unchanged Problem Episode. It is a genealogy of **separable information layers**: production, statutory submission, printing, distribution, archival retention, public inspection, and demand-triggered retrieval.

## 8. Historical actors / later reconstruction / researcher reconstruction

### Historical actors

The 1920s annual appropriations framed a printing-and-binding budget problem and authorized suppression of printing while preserving an original for inspection. By 1933 the Public Printer described the authority as an annually repeated economy mechanism and credited it with more than $2 million in cumulative savings. In 1953 Congress made the waiver permanent rather than fiscal-year-specific.

### Later reconstruction

The 1968 positive-law codification reconstructed the 1953 permanent provision as 44 U.S.C. §1117. Modern codification notes therefore expose the permanent statutory ancestry, but they obscure the much earlier annual-appropriation genealogy unless the older appropriations and Public Printer reports are recovered separately.

### Researcher reconstruction

Useful analytical labels, not actor vocabulary:

- **medium/obligation separation**: stopping printed distribution is not the same as ending information production or statutory reporting.
- **annual-to-permanent capability transition**: a repeatedly renewed fiscal-year exception becomes standing administrative authority.
- **information-layer decomposition**: production, submission, printing, distribution, retention, inspection and retrieval must be coded separately.

## 9. Why this changes the existing 1928–1954 interpretation

The existing `1928-1954-reporting-demand-shift.md` correctly treats 1928 as a major report-pruning and push-to-pull information redesign. But 1928 should not be described as the origin of federal concern with report burden in general.

By at least the FY1921/FY1922 period, Congress already had a recurring mechanism for reducing one cost component of reporting: **printing**. The 1928 reform attacked a different layer: **the statutory recurring-report obligation itself**.

Thus:

```text
pre-1928 reporting economy
already exists
        ↓
but mainly at publication/printing layer

1928
moves further upstream
into report-obligation design
```

That distinction makes the 1928 episode sharper, not weaker.

## 11. Hindsight risks

1. **"printing discontinued" = "report abolished"** — false. The statutes expressly preserved the original for public inspection.
2. **1928 invented report-cost concern** — false/overbroad. Printing-cost relief predates the 1928 mass repeal.
3. **public inspection = equivalent access** — unsafe. One original on file is not functionally equivalent to routine printed distribution to Congress or the public.
4. **$2 million savings proves all suppressed reports were useless** — false. The metric establishes printing savings, not informational value.
5. **1953 created the policy from scratch** — false. It converted a repeatedly renewed annual authority into a permanent one.
6. **one continuous Problem Episode, 1921–1968** — unsafe. The legal mechanism persists while the problems and affected information layers change.

## 14. Compact finding

> Federal report-pruning history has at least two distinct early lineages. Before Congress abolished 128 recurring reporting duties in 1928, it had already spent years allowing agencies to suppress the **printing** of annual and special reports to control printing-and-binding costs, while preserving originals for public inspection. The Public Printer said in 1933 that this authority began for FY1921, was repeated annually, and had saved more than $2 million. Congress converted the annual waiver into permanent authority in 1953, later codified as 44 U.S.C. §1117. The genealogy therefore requires an information-layer distinction: ending printed distribution, ending statutory submission, and ending information production are historically different events.
