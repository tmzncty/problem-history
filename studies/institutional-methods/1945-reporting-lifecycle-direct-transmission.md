# 1945: direct transmission and a reporting-lifecycle model

Status: **M2 source-control / institutional-method candidate / not a frozen Problem Episode**

This packet closes a source debt left by `1928-1954-reporting-demand-shift.md`. The 1945 report-pruning episode was not merely another chronologically similar attempt to eliminate stale statutory reports. House Report 79-311 explicitly identified H.R. 2504 as being "of the same character" as the Act of May 29, 1928, and reproduced the earlier push-to-pull logic in a more developed form. This upgrades the 1928→1945 edge from institutional genealogy to **documented actor-level transmission**.

## 1. New finding: 1945 actors explicitly invoked the 1928 precedent

House Report No. 311, submitted March 13, 1945 by Rep. John J. Cochran for the House Committee on Expenditures in the Executive Departments, explained H.R. 2504, a bill to discontinue 64 statutory reports. The committee stated directly that the proposal was "of the same character" as the Act of May 29, 1928 (45 Stat. 986), which had repealed 128 statutory reporting requirements.

This is not a later institutional reconstruction. It is an explicit contemporaneous citation by the committee responsible for the 1945 bill.

Primary source:
- U.S. House of Representatives, Committee on Expenditures in the Executive Departments, H. Rept. 79-311, *Discontinuing Certain Reports Now Required by Law*, March 13, 1945, pp. 1–2, reproduced in the legislative history of Public Law 79-615.

Evidence: **very high / contemporaneous committee report / explicit citation-transmission**.

## 2. Actor-level problem: reports have a lifecycle

The 1945 committee did more than say that some reports were obsolete. It described a recurring mechanism by which reporting requirements become obsolete.

The committee observed that when Congress establishes a new agency or expands an existing agency's functions, it naturally tends to require periodic reports so that Congress can remain informed. But after several years of operation, those same reports often become of relatively small value.

Safe actor-level reconstruction:

```text
new agency / expanded function
        ↓
uncertainty + oversight need
        ↓
Congress requires periodic report
        ↓
years of operation
        ↓
other procedures / institutional changes /
accumulated routine
        ↓
original report loses marginal value
        ↓
reporting requirement itself becomes
an administrative-efficiency problem
```

This is stronger than a generic "old reports become useless" claim. The committee described **report decay as an endogenous consequence of institutional maturation**.

Evidence: **very high**.

## 3. 1945 makes channel substitution explicit

The committee identified several mechanisms by which old reports had become redundant:

- new reporting procedures created under the Budget and Accounting Act of 1921;
- transfer or discontinuance of the underlying activities;
- duplication with budget estimates, annual reports, Treasury/GAO accounting, or other recurring publications;
- information remaining available in agency files and capable of being compiled when Congress actually needed it.

The general section then stated that information desired by Congress could be supplied on request, in the form desired for the particular purpose.

This is a direct actor-level version of the repository's provisional `push-to-pull information transition` fixture. It also adds a second mechanism: **channel substitution**. A dedicated statutory report can disappear because another reporting system now carries the same or better information.

Evidence: **very high for the generic committee rationale; item-specific strength varies and must be preserved item by item**.

## 4. Item-level evidence: different reports died for different reasons

H. Rept. 79-311 is unusually useful because it explains proposed repeal item by item. The reasons are heterogeneous.

### Duplicate fiscal information

For several Interior reports, the committee said the information was already present in Treasury/GAO accounting reports, budget estimates, or bureau annual reports; special statutory reports therefore required duplicate compilation. It added that information could be compiled on short notice if needed.

### A newer channel was more current

For a Navy public-works-contract report, the committee said the information was covered more fully and more currently by a monthly contract-awards report.

### Information remained in files and was available on request

For a Navy personnel report, the report required about 5,000 man-hours annually while duplicating more detailed information maintained in Bureau of Naval Personnel files and available to Congress. The committee recorded that the physical report had usually been stored in a vault and apparently not inspected.

### The underlying administrative object had changed or disappeared

Other items were obsolete because functions had been transferred, reorganized, or discontinued. In some cases the agency that had once been required to report was no longer institutionally positioned to produce the old report.

### The information existed in a different form

For Labor Department items 17 and 18, the committee said the activities were small and incidental and that the relevant information was available in appropriation estimates and departmental financial reports.

Methodological implication: **report disappearance must not be coded as one homogeneous event type**. At minimum distinguish duplication, superior substitute channel, on-demand retrievability, object disappearance/transfer, and low-use/high-cost decay.

## 5. 1928 → 1945 edge is now direct transmission

The prior packet correctly kept this relationship provisional. H. Rept. 79-311 resolves it:

```text
1928 Act
mass repeal of statutory reports
+ information-on-request architecture
        ↓
        │ explicit citation by 1945 committee
        ↓
1945 H.R. 2504 / H. Rept. 79-311
"same character" as 1928
+ developed lifecycle rationale
+ item-by-item substitution evidence
```

Classification:
- chronology: **certain**;
- institutional genealogy: **certain**;
- actor awareness of 1928 precedent: **certain**;
- direct citation/recommendation transmission: **high to very high**.

This means the 1945 episode is not evidence that Congress independently "rediscovered" the same problem. At least at committee level, actors consciously situated their work in an earlier report-pruning tradition.

## 6. Historical actors / later reconstruction / researcher reconstruction

### Historical actors

1945 House committee actors explicitly said:
- Congress tends to create periodic reporting requirements when agencies/functions are new;
- such reports can lose value after several years;
- many listed reports had been displaced by newer reporting procedures or institutional changes;
- desired information could instead be supplied on request and in a form fitted to the immediate purpose;
- H.R. 2504 belonged to the same class of reform as the 1928 Act.

### Later reconstruction

Later codification and oversight literature can legitimately connect the 1928 Act to modern congressional information mechanisms. But that modern reconstruction is no longer needed to prove the narrower 1928→1945 transmission edge: H. Rept. 79-311 itself supplies it.

### Researcher reconstruction

Useful analytical fixtures, not actor vocabulary:
- **reporting lifecycle**: a reporting obligation can be high-value at institutional birth and low-value after routines and alternative channels mature;
- **channel substitution**: a dedicated report can disappear while the underlying information survives in a newer or more general channel;
- **report decay is mechanism-specific**: duplication, low use, institutional transfer, superior alternative publication, and on-demand retrieval should not be collapsed into one `obsolete` label.

## 7. Problem transformation

The object of concern changes over time:

```text
at programme birth:
"How can Congress remain informed?"
        ↓
periodic report required
        ↓
institution matures
        ↓
"Why are we still compiling this particular report?"
        ↓
reporting obligation becomes the problem
        ↓
repeal / substitution / on-demand retrieval
```

This is a useful case where a **solution to an earlier information problem becomes a later administrative problem**.

Candidate fixture:

> `solution-to-burden transition`: a device institutionalized to solve Problem A persists after the conditions that made it valuable change, and the device itself becomes Problem B.

Do not promote this to validator status yet.

## 8. Hindsight risks

1. **1945 independently rediscovered 1928** — false at committee level; H. Rept. 79-311 explicitly cites the 1928 Act.
2. **all repealed reports were useless** — false; item-level rationales differ, and the bill itself was contested and amended.
3. **report repeal means information vanished** — often false in this packet; many items had substitute publications, files, budget/accounting channels, or on-demand compilation.
4. **on-demand information is functionally equivalent to automatic reporting** — unsafe. Pull systems require Congress to know what to ask for and shift agenda/search costs.
5. **the 1945 generic rationale proves the reason for every item** — false. Preserve item-level evidence.
6. **reporting lifecycle is actor terminology** — false. Actors described the mechanism; the label is ours.

## 12. Compact finding

> H. Rept. 79-311 converts the 1928→1945 relationship from a merely reconstructed institutional genealogy into documented actor-level transmission. The 1945 House committee explicitly described H.R. 2504 as being of the same character as the 1928 mass repeal, while adding a lifecycle account: Congress naturally requires reports when agencies or functions are new, but those reports can lose value as institutions mature and alternative information channels arise. The item-by-item evidence further shows that report disappearance has multiple mechanisms—duplication, superior substitute channels, on-demand retrievability, institutional transfer, and low-use/high-cost decay. A reporting requirement can therefore begin as a solution to an information problem and later become an administrative problem in its own right.
