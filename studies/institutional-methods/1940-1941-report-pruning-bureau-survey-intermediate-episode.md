# 1940–1941: a Bureau-of-the-Budget survey reopens statutory report pruning before the 1945 round

## Research question

The repository already has a 1928 → 1945 → 1954 genealogy of federal statutory-report pruning. Was 1945 really the next visible episode after the 1928 mass repeal, or is there an intermediate episode that changes the transmission story?

## Finding

There is a substantial intermediate episode in 1940–1941. House Report 77-1175, *Discontinuance of certain reports now required by law* (Aug. 13, 1941), accompanied H.R. 5508. The House subsequently passed H.R. 5508 and sent it to the Senate.

More importantly, the House floor explanation preserves actor-level provenance for the review itself. Representative John J. Cochran said that **in the previous Congress** the House Committee on Expenditures in the Executive Departments had asked the Bureau of the Budget to survey how many statutory reports could be dispensed with without impairing departmental efficiency or depriving Congress of necessary information. He said the resulting survey found roughly 250 reports required annually by law and criticized many as figure-heavy products from which useful information was difficult to extract without follow-up with departments.

This means the 1945 episode should not currently be narrated as the first post-1928 return of the report-pruning solution form. By 1940–1941, the same committee jurisdiction had already commissioned an executive-branch survey and moved a pruning bill through the House.

**Evidence strength:** high for report identity/date/existence. The PDF endpoint timed out during this pass, so the report's item-level contents have not yet been page-bound.

### 2. House floor debate supplies the review provenance and actor formulation

In the House debate on H.R. 5508, Cochran explained that the committee had asked the Bureau of the Budget in the **last Congress** to make a survey to determine which legally required reports could be eliminated without either:

1. impairing departmental efficiency; or
2. depriving Congress of necessary information.

He said the survey disclosed about 250 annual statutory reports. He characterized many as consisting mainly of figures and argued that useful information often required follow-up with departments anyway. In discussing a St. Elizabeths Hospital reporting requirement, he framed the burden as personnel being used to compile a report that no one read.

Source:

- Congressional Record, House, 1941, debate on H.R. 5508: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1941-pt7/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1941-pt7-3.pdf

**Evidence strength:** high. This is direct actor-level testimony about the origin and criteria of the review.

### 3. House passage and transmission to Senate are independently visible

The Senate Congressional Record records the House message announcing that it had passed **H.R. 5508, “to discontinue certain reports now required by law,”** and requested Senate concurrence.

Source:

- Congressional Record, Senate, 1941: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1941-pt7/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1941-pt7-4-1.pdf

**Evidence strength:** high for House passage and Senate transmission. This pass has **not** established enactment; do not silently convert House passage into a public law.

## Historical actors vs later reconstruction vs present analysis

### Historical actors

The recovered 1941 actor formulation is not “information lifecycle management.” Cochran's stated tests were practical and institutional:

- can the report be dispensed with without impairing departmental efficiency?
- would Congress lose necessary information?
- does preparing the report consume personnel/resources despite little observable use?
- is the report itself an effective information form, or does Congress still need departmental follow-up to understand it?

### Later reconstruction

The repository's broader 1928→1945→1954→1965 sequence is a reconstruction across episodes. The new 1941 evidence requires inserting an intermediate node but does not by itself prove that 1941 actors consciously treated the 1928 Act as their precedent.

### Present analytical shorthand

Useful researcher-side labels only:

- `statutory report stock review`
- `information value vs production burden`
- `report artifact utility != underlying information utility`
- `executive survey -> congressional pruning proposal`

Do not attribute these labels to Cochran, the Bureau of the Budget, or the committee.

## Problem-History payoff

The chronology should now be provisionally represented as:

```text
1928 mass repeal + demand-route preservation
        ↓ transmission still requires proof
76th Congress (1939–1940)
House Expenditures Committee requests Bureau of the Budget survey
        ↓
1941 H. Rept. 77-1175 / H.R. 5508
Bureau survey becomes House pruning bill
House passes; Senate receives
        ↓ outcome/enactment still unresolved
1945 H.R. 2504 round
        ↓
1954 H.R. 6290
        ↓
1958/1960 and 1965 recurring review rounds
```

This is a genuine correction to the previous sparse genealogy. It also exposes a potentially important distinction between **information** and the **periodic report artifact** carrying it. Cochran's formulation suggests that a report could be burdensome or ineffective even when Congress still needed the underlying information. That resembles, but must not yet be equated with, the explicit demand-route preservation built into the 1928 statute.

A second payoff is procedural. The 1941 episode visibly begins not with an isolated list of repeal clauses but with a committee-requested Bureau of the Budget survey. This provides an earlier instance of the review process itself becoming an object of institutional design.

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not claim direct 1928 → 1941 transmission yet.** The same problem family and committee jurisdiction are suggestive, but the recovered 1941 floor statement points explicitly only to a survey requested in the 76th Congress.
2. **Do not claim H.R. 5508 became law.** House passage and Senate receipt are established; final disposition remains source debt.
3. **Do not treat “no one reads it” as an objective audit result.** It is Cochran's floor formulation.
4. **Do not equate report elimination with information elimination.** The actor's own test explicitly included whether Congress would be deprived of necessary information.
5. **Do not infer the reason for each proposed repeal from the package-level floor rationale.** H. Rept. 77-1175 needs full-text recovery.
6. **Do not call 1941 a recurring cycle solely because later rounds exist.** What is established here is an intermediate survey-and-bill episode; recurrence as an actor-explicit concept is stronger in the later 1950s–1960s evidence.
