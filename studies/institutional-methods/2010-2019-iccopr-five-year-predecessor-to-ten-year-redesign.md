# 2010–2019 ICCOPR: the ten-year redesign had a five-year legislative predecessor

## Research question

The open ICCOPR chain has established that S.2297 as introduced on July 25, 2019 already contained a ten-year statutory research-plan update rule and a third National Academy function: organizational guidance regarding implementation. The remaining provenance problem was upstream: what earlier legislative solution forms existed between the original 1990 architecture and the 2019 bundle?

Fresh primary evidence identifies an important intermediate architecture in 2010–2011. House legislation responding to the long-stale ICCOPR plan proposed recurring updates **at least once every five years**, annual committee meetings, public input, and repeated National Academy review. This predecessor did **not** contain the later third NAS implementation-guidance function. It therefore prevents the 2019 design from being treated as a single-step jump from the 1990 baseline.

## Core finding

The legislative genealogy now has at least four distinguishable states:

```text
1990 OPA §7001
one implementation plan
+ NAS advice during development
+ NAS adequacy assessment
(no recurring statutory cadence)
        ↓
2009–2010 House reauthorization process
stale-plan / coordination problem explicitly reopened
        ↓
2010 H.R.2693 substitute / House-passed bill
recurring plan: periodically, at least every 5 years
+ annual committee activity review
+ public/advisory input during plan development
+ NAS post-submission review
        ↓
2011 H.R.1568 introduced
substantially repeats the five-year architecture
        ↓
2019 S.2297 introduced
10-year statutory updates
+ inherited NAS development advice
+ inherited adequacy assessment
+ NEW/expanded NAS implementation-organizational guidance
+ quarterly meetings/public summaries
```

The strongest transformation is therefore not simply `no recurrence -> ten-year recurrence`. A five-year recurring-plan proposal existed and passed the House in 2010, then reappeared in introduced legislation in 2011. The unexplained historical change is now more specific: **why did the later Senate architecture move from the earlier five-year legislative proposal to ten years, while also restoring NAS development advice and adding implementation guidance?**

## 1. 2010 House actors explicitly reopened the stale-plan problem

House Report 111-553 describes the federal oil-spill R&D program as needing a new approach. It notes that the 1997 ICCOPR plan had not been updated, that emerging threats included increased maritime transportation, remote/offshore energy development, aging infrastructure, and new fuel stocks/blends, and that appropriations had been small relative to perceived need. After Deepwater Horizon, the committee treated effective, coordinated oil-spill research as an especially visible problem.

Primary source:

- H. Rept. 111-553, *Oil Pollution Research and Development Program Reauthorization Act of 2010*: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-111hrpt553/pdf/CRPT-111hrpt553.pdf

Supporting committee activity summary:

- H. Rept. 111-698, House Science and Technology Committee activity summary: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-111hrpt698/html/CRPT-111hrpt698.htm

**Evidence strength:** very high for the committee's 2010 problem formulation and legislative history.

Important chronology: H.R.2693 was introduced June 3, 2009, before Deepwater Horizon. The committee's 2010 substitute and report were shaped in a later environment in which Deepwater Horizon sharply increased attention to oil-spill research. Do not project the post-spill rationale backward onto every clause of the original 2009 introduced bill.

## 2. The 2010 solution already proposed recurring plan updates every five years

The section-by-section analysis in H. Rept. 111-553 states that §7001(b)(1) would require ICCOPR to submit a new plan within 180 days and **periodically thereafter but not less than once every five years**.

The same legislative package also required the committee to coordinate research on an ongoing basis and meet no less than once per year to plan activities and determine whether the program was producing new or improved methods and technologies.

The House passed H.R.2693 on July 21, 2010 by voice vote; it was received in the Senate and referred to the Senate Commerce, Science, and Transportation Committee on July 22. No further legislative action occurred.

Primary / official sources:

- H. Rept. 111-553, section-by-section analysis, especially §3.
- H.R.2693, engrossed House text, July 21, 2010: https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-111hr2693eh
- H.R.2693, referred-in-Senate version, July 22, 2010: https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-111hr2693rfs
- H. Rept. 111-698 for final legislative disposition.

**Evidence strength:** very high.

This supplies a previously missing state:

```text
recurrence proposed and passed one chamber at 5 years
!=
recurrence enacted
```

## 3. The 2010 architecture reorganized external input rather than simply preserving the 1990 NAS role

The 2010 section-by-section analysis shows a more complicated review architecture than either the 1990 baseline or the later 2019 bill.

For plan **development**, the Chair was directed to solicit advice from an Oil Pollution Research Advisory Committee, NIST, and public comment. After submission, the Chair would contract with the National Academy of Sciences to review the plan, assess its adequacy, and report to Congress. The Chair was then directed to address and incorporate NAS recommendations as appropriate.

Thus the 2010 proposal should not be summarized as merely `1990 review + five-year updates`. It redistributed functions:

```text
1990
NAS development advice
+ NAS adequacy assessment

2010 House proposal
advisory committee / NIST / public input during development
+ NAS post-submission review/adequacy assessment
+ explicit response-to-recommendations step
```

Primary source:

- H. Rept. 111-553, section-by-section analysis of §§3(b)–(c).

**Evidence strength:** very high for the proposed architecture. It was not enacted.

This also means the 2019 architecture partly looks like a **recombination** rather than a simple linear expansion: it restores NAS advice during plan development, retains adequacy review, and adds implementation guidance.

`recombination` is researcher-side shorthand, not actor vocabulary.

## 4. 2011 H.R.1568 repeats the five-year cadence and review structure

On April 14, 2011, Rep. Lynn Woolsey introduced H.R.1568, the *Oil Pollution Research and Development Program Reauthorization Act of 2011*. Its text again required a research plan within 180 days and periodically thereafter, **not less than once every five years**.

It again separated plan-development input from NAS post-submission review:

- development advice from the Oil Pollution Research Advisory Committee, NIST, third-party standard-setting organizations, and a 30-day public-comment process;
- after submission, NAS review and adequacy assessment;
- Chair to address/incorporate recommendations as appropriate.

Primary source:

- H.R.1568, introduced Apr. 14, 2011, especially pp. 6–8: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-112hr1568ih/pdf/BILLS-112hr1568ih.pdf

CRS later lists H.R.1568 among the oil-spill proposals of the 112th Congress; it did not become law.

Secondary/high-quality reconstruction:

- CRS R41684, *Enacted and Proposed Oil Spill Legislation in the 112th Congress*: https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R41684.html

**Evidence strength:** very high for bill text; high for later legislative-status reconstruction.

The 2011 repetition matters because the five-year cadence was not merely a transient wording visible in one committee substitute. It survived into a new Congress as an introduced solution form.

## 5. What this changes about the 2019 provenance question

The current PR already establishes that S.2297 as introduced in July 2019 contained:

- ten-year statutory updates;
- NAS development advice;
- NAS adequacy assessment;
- NAS implementation/organizational guidance;
- quarterly meetings and public summaries;
- a refreshed content schema.

The 2010–2011 evidence means that at least two components had earlier legislative analogues in a different configuration:

| Mechanism | 1990 enacted | 2010/2011 House proposals | 2019 S.2297 introduced |
|---|---|---|---|
| recurring statutory plan updates | no | **≥ every 5 years** | **every 10 years** |
| NAS development advice | yes | shifted to advisory/public/NIST inputs | yes |
| NAS adequacy review | yes | yes, post-submission | yes |
| explicit response to NAS recommendations | not in same form | yes | not identical |
| NAS implementation/organizational guidance | no | no recovered clause | **yes** |
| committee meeting floor | none comparable recovered here | annual | quarterly |
| public input/visibility | not comparable | 30-day public comment | public meeting summaries |

This table is a version map, not a claim of direct textual borrowing from H.R.2693/H.R.1568 into S.2297. Documentary transmission remains unproved.

## Actor formulation / later reconstruction / present analysis

### Historical actors

Directly supportable:

- House Science and Technology actors in 2010 treated the oil-spill R&D program as needing reinvigoration and better coordination.
- The House-passed 2010 solution required recurring plan revision at least every five years.
- The proposal combined annual committee activity review, broader development-stage input, and NAS post-submission review.
- H.R.1568 in 2011 repeated the five-year cadence and substantially similar review structure.
- Neither proposal became law.

### Later reconstruction

CRS inventories the 2010–2011 proposals as part of the post-Deepwater-Horizon legislative field. That is useful for status/context but should not substitute for the bill/report text when identifying mechanism details.

### Present analysis

Candidate researcher-side labels only:

- `failed recurring-plan institutionalization`;
- `five-year predecessor architecture`;
- `review-function redistribution`;
- `later recombination of inherited and proposed mechanisms`.

Do not attribute these phrases to historical actors.

## Problem-History payoff

This episode adds an important negative/failed-solution layer to the ICCOPR genealogy:

```text
stale plan recognized
        ↓
recurrence solution proposed at 5 years
        ↓
passes House (2010)
        ↓
not enacted
        ↓
reintroduced (2011)
        ↓
not enacted
        ↓
ICCOPR later creates organizational six-year cycle (2015)
        ↓
2019 Senate bill proposes statutory 10-year cycle
        ↓
2021 enactment
```

The history therefore should not be narrated as if recurring planning first became thinkable in 2015 or 2019. A statutory recurrence solution had already been legislatively formulated and passed one chamber in 2010.

At the same time, this is **not yet evidence that the 2015 six-year organizational cycle descended from the 2010 five-year proposal**, nor that the 2019 ten-year rule was drafted by modifying the 2010/2011 language. Those transmission edges require evidence.

Methodologically:

```text
failed solution form
!= historical irrelevance
```

A failed proposal can define an available solution architecture that later episodes must be compared against, even when direct borrowing is not demonstrated.

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not say the 2019 ten-year rule was invented from nothing.** A recurring statutory plan-update proposal existed at five years in 2010–2011.
2. **Do not say the 2019 ten-year rule was copied from the 2010 five-year rule.** Similar object/function plus chronology is not documentary transmission.
3. **Do not say Deepwater Horizon caused H.R.2693's original introduction.** H.R.2693 was introduced in June 2009; the 2010 substitute/report belongs to the post-spill legislative environment.
4. **Do not say the 2010 House bill enacted a five-year cycle.** It passed the House but died after Senate referral.
5. **Do not collapse all NAS roles into one review variable.** Development advice, adequacy review, response to recommendations, and implementation guidance move differently across versions.
6. **Do not infer that the later six-year ICCOPR practice implemented the failed five-year bill.** No transmission evidence recovered here establishes that relation.
7. **Do not infer why 2019 chose ten rather than five or six years.** The version genealogy sharpens that question but does not answer motive.

## Compact finding

> The 2019 ICCOPR ten-year update rule had an important failed legislative predecessor. H.R.2693 as passed by the House in 2010 required a new oil-pollution research plan and recurring revisions at least once every five years; it also required ongoing coordination, annual committee review of program activity, development-stage advisory/public input, and post-submission National Academy review. H.R.1568 reintroduced substantially the same five-year architecture in 2011. Neither became law. The ICCOPR genealogy therefore contains `stale plan -> failed five-year statutory recurrence -> later six-year organizational recurrence -> 2019 ten-year statutory proposal -> 2021 enactment`. The new source problem is no longer whether recurring statutory planning existed before 2019, but how and why the available cadence changed from five years to ten and the review functions were recombined and expanded.
