# 1946–1974: from consuming submitted reports to commissioning evaluation

## Research question

The branch already reconstructs the 1946 Legislative Reorganization Act's `continuous watchfulness` rule: standing committees were instructed to watch administration of laws within their jurisdiction and, for that purpose, study pertinent executive-branch reports and data submitted to Congress. That is an important information-consumption mandate. But it should not be frozen as the final form of congressional oversight.

This packet traces the next two statutory transformations, in 1970 and 1974. The result is a useful distinction between three different information architectures:

```text
1946: study reports/data that agencies submit
1970: continuously review application/administration/execution + report committee activity
1974: perform/contract evaluation or require an agency to evaluate and furnish a report
```

The strongest payoff is methodological: `reports as oversight input`, `committee reports as oversight-accountability output`, and `reports commissioned as evaluation instruments` are different institutional objects and should not be collapsed into a generic category of "reporting."

## 1. Baseline: 1946 made executive reports/data inputs to continuous watchfulness

Section 136 of the Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946 required each House and Senate standing committee to exercise "continuous watchfulness" over execution by administrative agencies of laws within its jurisdiction and, for that purpose, to study pertinent reports and data submitted to Congress by executive agencies.

This creates a clear directional structure:

```text
executive agency
  -> submits report/data to Congress
  -> jurisdictional standing committee studies it
  -> continuous watchfulness over execution of law
```

The branch's separate packet on the 1946 debate shows that `watchfulness` itself was a negotiated actor term: the Senate replaced proposed `surveillance` language after concern that the latter could imply legislative superintendence of administration.

Evidence strength: **very high** for the statutory rule; **high** for the actor-level wording dispute as documented in the Congressional Record and existing branch research.

## 2. 1970: Congress rewrites the duty from watchfulness to continuing legislative review

The Legislative Reorganization Act of 1970, Pub. L. 91-510, §118(a)(1), 84 Stat. 1156, replaced the 1946 formulation with a more explicit duty: standing committees were to "review and study, on a continuing basis" the application, administration, and execution of laws within their jurisdiction.

Official codification history preserves the amendment lineage from the 1946 §136 to the 1970 amendment. CRS likewise describes the 1970 Act as transforming the older `continuous watchfulness` function into `legislative review`.

Primary / official source:

- GovInfo, historical codification of former 2 U.S.C. §190d/related provision and amendment notes: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2021-title2/pdf/USCODE-2021-title2.pdf

High-quality institutional synthesis:

- CRS, *Guide to Committee Activity Reports: Purpose, Rules, and Contents*, R45104, updated June 2, 2025: https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45104/R45104.7.pdf

Evidence strength: **very high** for the statutory transformation.

### Actor language vs present analysis

`continuous watchfulness` and the 1970 statutory `review and study ... application, administration, and execution` are actor/legal language. `information-consumption mandate` and `legislative-review regime` are present analytical shorthand.

Do not write that Congress in 1970 repudiated the 1946 idea. The evidence establishes replacement/rephrasing and expansion of the formal duty, not an actor-explicit declaration that the old rule had failed.

## 3. 1970 also turns oversight itself into a periodic reporting object

The same 1970 reform required most House and Senate standing committees to report periodically on their activities under the continuing-review provision. The requirement began with reports due in odd-numbered years after the close of a Congress.

H. Rept. 91-1215 supplied an actor-explicit rationale: the periodic activity report was intended to give the House an additional means to appraise the results of legislation it had approved and to emphasize the importance of legislative review. CRS preserves this rationale and identifies the report precisely (House Committee on Rules, H. Rept. 91-1215, p. 74).

Source:

- CRS R45104, pp. 2–3, citing H. Rept. 91-1215, p. 74: https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45104/R45104.7.pdf

Evidence strength: **high** for the committee-report rationale through CRS's precise quotation/citation; **source debt remains** to retrieve and archive the original H. Rept. 91-1215 page directly.

This creates a second information direction:

```text
executive reports/data -> committee oversight
committee oversight -> activity report -> chamber
```

The important point is that these are not the same kind of report. The first is an executive-origin information input; the second is a legislative-origin account of oversight activity.

## 4. 1974: evaluation can be produced by the committee, contracted out, or demanded from an agency

The Congressional Budget Act of 1974 amended the same continuing-review provision. The official U.S. Code amendment note states that committees were authorized to carry out required analysis, appraisal, and evaluation themselves, by contract, **or to require a Government agency to do so and furnish a report to Congress**. It also authorized techniques including pilot testing, cost-benefit analysis, and evaluation after a defined period.

Primary / official source:

- GovInfo, U.S. Code Title 2, amendment history to the continuing-review provision, recording Pub. L. 93-344, §701, 88 Stat. 325: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2021-title2/pdf/USCODE-2021-title2.pdf

CRS independently summarizes the same 1974 expansion in its *Congressional Oversight Manual*.

Evidence strength: **very high**.

This changes the information architecture again:

```text
1946:
agency creates/submits report -> committee studies it

1970:
committee continuously reviews administration
+ committee periodically reports its own oversight activity

1974:
committee may itself evaluate
OR contract evaluation
OR require agency evaluation -> agency furnishes report to Congress
```

The 1974 route is particularly important for Problem History because the report is no longer merely an inherited periodic artifact or a voluntarily/routinely submitted source. It can be the **output of an evaluation obligation activated by Congress**.

## 5. Problem-History payoff: classify report objects by causal position, not by document label

A generic `report` field hides historically important differences. A more useful schema is:

```text
report_role:
  routine_statutory_push
  requested_information
  oversight_input
  committee_activity_account
  commissioned_evaluation_output

producer:
  executive_agency
  congressional_committee
  contractor
  mixed

trigger:
  calendar/statute
  member_or_committee_request
  committee_review_duty
  committee_evaluation_order

recipient:
  chamber
  jurisdictional_committee
  Congress generally
  named member(s)

use_claim:
  information supply
  legislative review
  appraisal/evaluation
  accountability for oversight activity
```

These are researcher-created fields, not historical vocabulary.

This matters directly for the branch's report-pruning episodes. A historical actor may judge one **routine periodic push report** obsolete while simultaneously strengthening Congress's ability to demand **targeted information or evaluation reports**. Therefore:

```text
repeal of a reporting requirement
!=
withdrawal of congressional information capacity
```

and, conversely:

```text
more reporting authority
!=
more routine periodic reports
```

## 6. Relation to the 1928→1946→1970→1974 chain

Existing branch research has already recovered the 1928 hybrid architecture in which Congress repealed 128 routine reporting mandates while adding a committee-member demand route for information. The later oversight statutes should not be presented as a proven linear institutional inheritance from that 1928 mechanism; no transmission evidence recovered here establishes that claim.

But as an object-level transformation chain, the evidence now supports a longer comparison:

```text
1928: prune routine reports + preserve targeted committee-member demand route
1946: jurisdictional committees must study pertinent submitted reports/data
1970: continuing legislative review + periodic committee activity reports
1974: committees may generate, contract, or require evaluation and an agency report
```

This is **not** one actor's continuous project and should not be narrated teleologically. It is a sequence of distinct statutory architectures that progressively makes visible several separable functions of information in oversight.

## 7. Hindsight risks

1. **Do not call 1928 §2 an ancestor of the 1974 evaluation power without transmission evidence.** Functional resemblance is not genealogy.
2. **Do not treat every document called a report as the same institutional object.** Producer, trigger, recipient, and use differ.
3. **Do not infer that 1970 replaced 1946 because continuous watchfulness had failed.** The legal wording changed; failure as motive requires actor evidence.
4. **Do not project modern program-evaluation vocabulary backward onto 1928 or 1946.** By 1974, `analysis, appraisal, and evaluation` is statutory language; before then, use period-specific wording.
5. **Do not infer that repeal of routine reports reduced oversight capacity.** That must be tested for each episode against surviving request, evaluation, and committee-review routes.

## 9. Remaining uncertainty / next candidates

Highest-value next step is to recover H. Rept. 91-1215 directly and inspect the surrounding pages, rather than relying on the precise CRS quotation alone. Questions:

- Did the House Rules Committee explicitly diagnose weaknesses in the 1946 `continuous watchfulness` regime?
- Did it distinguish incoming executive reports from committee activity reports?
- Did it explain why biennial reporting was the chosen accountability mechanism?

A second candidate is the 1968 Intergovernmental Cooperation Act. CRS records that it required jurisdictional committees to study grant-in-aid programs against explicit questions—whether purposes had been met, assistance remained necessary, programs were adequate, and changes were needed. That may be the missing bridge between the broad 1946 watchfulness mandate and the explicit evaluation architecture of 1970/1974. It should be researched separately with the statute and committee history before being inserted as a causal bridge.
