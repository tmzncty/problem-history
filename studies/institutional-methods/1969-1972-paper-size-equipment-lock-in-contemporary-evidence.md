# 1969–1972 paper-size standardization: equipment lock-in as contemporary evidence

## Why this packet exists

The existing 1921–1980 paper-size chain establishes that the Federal Government's 8 × 10.5 inch correspondence standard survived for decades, was explicitly reopened as a standardization problem in 1969, and was replaced for bounded classes of correspondence/copying material effective 1980. The remaining source debt is causal: what made an inherited size standard increasingly costly or awkward by the late 1960s–1970s?

This packet adds a contemporary North American standards source that identifies a concrete mechanism — **equipment designed around inherited paper sizes can make later size conversion costly or inefficient** — while deliberately refusing to treat that source as proof of the Joint Committee on Printing's 1978 motive.

## New evidence

### 1. The 1969 U.S. meeting had already widened the object beyond paper alone

The June 30, 1969 *Report of Informational Discussion Meeting: Standardizing Paper Sizes*, sponsored by NARS/GSA and the National Bureau of Standards, assembled not only paper and printing interests but organizations concerned with data processing, microfilm, envelopes, printing equipment, and other information-handling systems. The existing packet correctly treats this as evidence that paper size had become a standards-network coordination problem, not as proof that any one technology caused the eventual 1978 decision.

Primary source already recovered in this PR:

- *Report of Informational Discussion Meeting: Standardizing Paper Sizes*, National Archives Building, June 30, 1969, sponsored by National Archives and Records Service, General Services Administration, and National Bureau of Standards. CIA FOIA copy: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP74-00390R000300360002-4.pdf

**Evidence strength:** high for the scope of the 1969 problem-formulation network; not sufficient for 1978 causation.

### 2. Dunn's contemporary paper-size study identifies machine-size lock-in explicitly

Arthur D. Dunn's *Notes on the Standardization of Paper Sizes* (original work dated 1968; later circulated/issued in revised form) provides a near-contemporary North American standards analysis. In its printing-and-publishing discussion, Dunn argues that North American printing machinery had been designed around traditional paper sizes. Using such machines with international standard sizes could therefore reduce the effective working area or capacity. He distinguishes machine types:

- letterpress and offset equipment could sometimes use alternative sizes, though inefficiently;
- **rotary presses were a major obstacle because cylinder circumference was tied to sheet size and was not easily modified**;
- smaller offset presses used for letter/legal work could accept slightly narrower international-standard paper more readily.

Dunn also reports that standardization could reduce paper inventories and storage requirements and improve production economy, while emphasizing that the benefits and conversion costs depended on the surrounding machinery and workflow.

Source:

- Arthur D. Dunn, *Notes on the Standardization of Paper Sizes*, pp. 23, 27–29 in the accessible scan: https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~mgk25/volatile/dunn-papersizes.pdf

The report is independently catalogued in the 1975 ERIC bibliography as a 54-page work introducing international paper sizes and discussing envelopes, microfilm, and printed-booklet sizes. Library and Archives Canada's Arthur D. Dunn fonds also preserves a dedicated 1967–1972 paper-standardization correspondence/report series, including government-department responses and international-standard material.

Supporting archival lead:

- Library and Archives Canada, Arthur D. Dunn fonds finding aid, vol. 27: paper standardization correspondence/reports (1967–1972), government-department responses (1968), and international-standard/Federal Identity Program material (1970–1971): https://data2.archives.ca/pdf/pdf001/p000000304.pdf

**Evidence strength:** high that equipment lock-in and inventory/production interactions were explicitly recognized in contemporary North American paper-standardization analysis. Medium/low for any inference about U.S. Federal decision-makers specifically, because Dunn is not evidence of what JCP members themselves relied upon.

## What this changes in the Problem History

The earlier chain can now be refined without inventing a causal edge:

```text
1920s
paper heterogeneity framed as waste
        ↓
8 × 10.5 government standard as economy solution
        ↓
long-lived standard becomes embedded in equipment/workflows
        ↓
1969 U.S. reopening of paper size as a cross-system standards problem
        ↓
contemporary North American analysis explicitly recognizes
paper-size ↔ machine-capacity / inventory / workflow coupling
        ↓
1978 bounded U.S. Federal migration decision
        ↓
1980 implementation
```

The important addition is not "machines caused the 1978 switch." It is the historically available mechanism:

```text
standard longevity
        → complementary equipment/workflow adaptation
        → switching cost / compatibility constraint
```

A paper standard is therefore not merely a nominal dimension. Once presses, envelopes, filing, copying, procurement, and other routines are organized around it, the standard becomes part of a **complementary technical system**.

## Three-layer distinction

### Historical actors / contemporary problem formulations

**1969 U.S. actors:** paper-size standardization was explicitly treated as involving multiple standards activities and information-handling constituencies; further factual analysis and classification were still required.

**Dunn, 1968/early 1970s:** a contemporary North American standards analyst explicitly described machinery designed for inherited sizes as a constraint on conversion and distinguished easy and difficult machine classes.

Neither source, by itself, says: "the U.S. Government should replace 8 × 10.5 with 8.5 × 11 because of photocopiers."

### Later reconstruction

Later popular accounts often explain the U.S. Federal change as a response to photocopying or commercial 8.5 × 11 dominance. Those accounts are useful as search leads but should not be allowed to overwrite the still-incomplete 1969–1978 U.S. decision record.

### Present analytical language

Candidate project terms:

- `complementary-equipment lock-in`
- `standard-as-infrastructure`
- `conversion-cost asymmetry`

These are present-day analytical labels, not historical actors' vocabulary.

## Methodological payoff

### Standards should be modeled with complements, not as isolated artifacts

For a durable standard, record at least:

- the nominal specification;
- machinery designed around it;
- consumables and inventories;
- adjacent standards (envelopes, forms, folders, storage);
- workflow assumptions;
- replacement/retooling horizon;
- which components can tolerate a new standard and which cannot.

This helps explain why a solution can survive after its original problem formulation disappears: complementary investments can give the solution an independent persistence mechanism.

### Contemporary mechanism evidence is not decision-causation evidence

A particularly important evidence rule emerges here:

```text
mechanism known in the period
        !=
mechanism proved to have caused a particular decision
```

Dunn upgrades "equipment compatibility is a modern plausible story" to "equipment-size coupling was explicitly recognized by a contemporary standards analyst." It does **not** upgrade it to "JCP switched sizes because of equipment compatibility."

## Hindsight risks

1. **Photocopier monocausality.** Do not convert later claims about photocopying into the 1978 decision rationale without a JCP/GSA/NBS source.
2. **International-size substitution.** Dunn advocates international sizes; the U.S. Federal 1978 decision instead selected commercial 8.5 × 11. His analysis is evidence for the mechanism of technical lock-in, not evidence that U.S. actors shared his preferred solution.
3. **All equipment behaves alike.** Dunn explicitly distinguishes rotary presses from smaller offset equipment. "Equipment compatibility" must remain object-specific.
4. **Standards determine technology in one direction.** The evidence is compatible with reciprocal adaptation: paper sizes shape machines, while installed machines constrain later paper-size choices.

## Remaining uncertainty

The central U.S. causal gap remains open. We still need direct 1969–1978 evidence showing which factors JCP/GSA/NBS actually weighed when choosing 8.5 × 11: copying equipment, private-sector procurement, envelope/form compatibility, inventory, filing/storage, data processing, or other considerations.

Dunn's archival fonds creates a concrete new retrieval route because it contains 1967–1972 paper-standardization correspondence and government-department responses. Those records may preserve exchanges with U.S. standards actors or evidence of the wider North American debate, but the finding aid alone does not establish their contents.
