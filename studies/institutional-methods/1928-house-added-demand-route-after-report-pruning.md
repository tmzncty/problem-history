# 1928: House committee adds a demand-route safeguard to mass report pruning

## Research question

The branch now reconstructs the upstream 1926–1928 Bureau of Efficiency review that generated a mass-repeal proposal. A remaining design question is whether the final 1928 solution was simply the Bureau's repeal package, or whether Congress materially transformed the solution architecture while considering it.

Legislative-history evidence quoted by CRS, the D.C. Circuit, and the U.S. House's Bipartisan Legal Advisory Group identifies one concrete congressional transformation: the House committee added the information-request provision that later became 5 U.S.C. §2954 in order to preserve access to information contained in reports proposed for abolition.

This creates a more precise sequence:

```text
Bureau review + repeal proposal
        ↓
House committee considers abolition of periodic artifacts
        ↓
House adds request-based information safeguard
        ↓
128 periodic reporting requirements repealed
        +
committee/minority-member demand route enacted
```

The important historical point is not merely that repeal and information access coexisted in the final statute. The House report explicitly describes the access provision as an **addition to the bill** made to address a problem created by abolishing the reports.

## 1. Actor-level formulation in H. Rept. 70-1757

Later official/legal sources reproduce the relevant House report language. H. Rept. 70-1757 explains that, to avoid doubt about the House's right to receive information contained in reports proposed for abolition, a provision had been added requiring such information to be furnished to the House Committee on Expenditures in the Executive Departments or on request of any seven of its members.

The report also states that when a Member or committee wants information on a particular subject, it can be better obtained through a specifically framed request designed to elicit the information desired.

Sources reproducing/citing the 1928 report:

- *Maloney v. Murphy*, 984 F.3d 50 (D.C. Cir. 2020), discussing the 1928 Act and quoting H. Rept. 70-1757: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/18-5305/18-5305-2020-12-29.html
- U.S. House Bipartisan Legal Advisory Group, amicus brief in *Waxman v. Thompson*, quoting H. Rept. 70-1757 and citing 69 Cong. Rec. 10613: https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/house-amicus.pdf
- Congressional Research Service, *Congressional Oversight Manual*, discussing H. Rept. 70-1757 and S. Rept. 70-1320: https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20041021_RL30240_29af6713ac3cab571a0a72e9fc3bf5fe5863dbd2.pdf

**Evidence strength: high** for the committee-added safeguard and its stated rationale. The quoted language is consistently attributed to H. Rept. 70-1757 by a federal appellate opinion, the House's own legal office, and CRS. The report itself should still be recovered in full for repository-grade primary-source completeness.

## 2. This is a solution transformation, not just a companion clause

The branch's Bureau-of-Efficiency packet establishes an upstream administrative sequence of inventory review, item assessment, and repeal drafting. The House report now adds a distinct legislative design move: Congress did not merely accept a binary `keep report / repeal report` choice. It coupled termination of fixed periodic artifacts with a mechanism for obtaining the underlying information when needed.

Observed design sequence:

```text
problem framing: recurring reports judged obsolete / valueless / burdensome
solution candidate: repeal fixed reporting requirements
secondary problem: repeal may jeopardize congressional access to useful information
solution modification: add request-based access route
final architecture: periodic artifact termination + demand-triggered information access
```

The labels `secondary problem`, `solution modification`, and `demand-triggered route` are present-day analytical shorthand. The historically attributable core is narrower: the House report says the provision was added to preserve the right to obtain information contained in the reports being abolished and argues that particular information can be better secured through specifically framed requests.

## 3. Historical actor vs later reconstruction

### Historical actor claims

Supportable through the quoted House report:

- the House committee recognized a potential information-access issue when abolishing reports;
- it added a provision to preserve access to information from those reports;
- it considered targeted requests a potentially better way to obtain particular information than inherited periodic reports.

The Senate report, as summarized and quoted by CRS, described the provision somewhat more narrowly: it would make it possible to require a discontinued report to be resubmitted when its necessity became evident. This difference matters. The House language supports a more flexible targeted-information logic, while the Senate explanation emphasizes recoverability of discontinued reports.

### Later reconstruction

Modern courts and CRS discuss the 1928 provision because it became the ancestor of current 5 U.S.C. §2954. Their modern disputes concern standing, enforceability, and scope. Those later constitutional/justiciability controversies must not be projected backward as the 1928 actors' problem.

### Present analytical language

Researcher labels only:

- `artifact termination + demand route`;
- `solution transformation`;
- `secondary problem generated by the proposed solution`;
- `fixed periodic push -> targeted pull`.

## 4. Why this matters for Problem History

This episode demonstrates that a proposed solution can itself create a newly articulated problem during legislative processing.

A flat episode record might say:

```text
problem: useless reports
solution: repeal 128 reports
```

But the recovered legislative sequence is richer:

```text
P1: inherited periodic reports impose cost / have low value
        ↓
S1: abolish them
        ↓
P2: abolition raises a congressional information-access concern
        ↓
S2: preserve a request-based route to the underlying information
        ↓
combined enacted solution
```

This suggests a useful schema distinction between:

- `initial_problem_formulation`;
- `proposed_solution`;
- `solution_generated_problem`;
- `solution_revision`;
- `final_solution_architecture`.

These are analytical fields, not actor vocabulary.

The same distinction is directly relevant to later report-pruning rounds in the repository. When later actors repeal, reduce frequency, rescue, or replace reports, the researcher should ask not only why an inherited object became problematic, but whether the proposed remedy generated a new information-loss, jurisdiction, accountability, or implementation problem that changed the final design.

## 5. Hindsight risks

1. **Do not say the Bureau of Efficiency designed the final request mechanism unless the Bureau draft is recovered and proves it.** The current actor-level evidence says the House report described the provision as having been added to the bill.
2. **Do not equate 1928 targeted requests with modern FOIA or modern data APIs.** `push -> pull` is analytical shorthand only.
3. **Do not read modern §2954 enforcement litigation back into 1928.** The historical problem documented here is preservation of congressional information access while reports were being abolished.
4. **Do not collapse the House and Senate rationales.** House language emphasizes specifically framed requests for desired information; the Senate report is described as emphasizing the ability to require a discontinued report again if necessity becomes evident.
5. **Do not infer that every one of the 128 repealed reports remained reproducible on demand.** The statute created an information-request mechanism; object-level availability and record retention require separate evidence.

## Compact finding

The 1928 report-pruning episode was not simply `Bureau recommends repeal -> Congress repeals`. H. Rept. 70-1757 explicitly describes an information-access safeguard as having been added to the bill so that abolishing periodic reports would not eliminate the House's ability to obtain the information they contained; the report further argues that particular information may be better obtained through specifically framed requests. The episode therefore contains a recoverable solution transformation: `repeal inherited periodic artifacts -> recognize information-access problem created by repeal -> add demand-based safeguard -> enact combined architecture`. This is a useful Problem History pattern because it captures a problem generated by the proposed solution itself rather than treating enactment as a one-step response to the initial problem.
