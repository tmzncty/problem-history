# 1980–1983: from stationery conversion regime to permanent rule

## Why this note exists

Earlier work in this branch recovered the 1978 JCP decision, the 1979 FPMR Temporary Regulation B-5 conversion regime, and its 1980 extension. That left a source debt: what replaced the temporary conversion machinery, and when did 8.5 × 11 become embedded in the permanent Federal Property Management Regulations?

This note closes that edge with Federal Register evidence.

## New finding

The temporary conversion regime did **not** simply expire into an undocumented settled practice. GSA/NARS opened a permanent rulemaking while the conversion regime was still active, and the final permanent rule did not arrive until July 1983.

The sequence is:

- **1979-10-02** — FPMR Temporary Regulation B-5 authorizes mixed use during conversion, requires economical depletion of 8-inch-wide stocks, and prohibits new printing/procurement of 8 × 10.5 stationery after 1979-12-31 absent written NARS approval. Original expiration: 1980-07-15. 44 FR 56699.
- **1980-08-01** — Supplement 1 extends B-5 rather than allowing the temporary regime to lapse.
- **1980-10-01** — GSA/NARS publishes a **proposed permanent rule**, 45 FR 64978. It explicitly says the proposal would govern 8.5 × 11 stationery, increase agency responsibility for economical selection/use, and cancel B-5 and Supplement 1. Comments were due 1980-12-01.
- **1981-04-30** — GSA's regulatory agenda still lists the stationery-standards project and identifies the October 1980 proposal; the project therefore remained an active rulemaking rather than a completed replacement.
- **1983-07-06** — GSA publishes the final rule, FPMR Amendment B-52, 48 FR 31033–31037. It expressly cancels B-5 and Supplement 1 and rewrites 41 CFR 101-11.603 as permanent mandatory stationery standards.
- **1983-08-26** — GSA publishes corrections to the final rule, 48 FR 38824.

This produces a cleaner endpoint model than treating 1980-01-01 as “the” date on which the old standard died.

## Actor-level problem formulations

### 1979 conversion problem

B-5 framed the immediate problem as how to move from one stationery size to another without wasting stocks or disrupting correspondence, mail, forms, copying, directives, and word-processing practices. Its instructions allowed mixed-size use and explicitly compared redistribution costs with replacement/distribution costs.

This was a **migration-management problem**, not yet simply a permanent codification problem.

### 1980 permanent-rule proposal

The October 1980 proposal reframed the regulatory task. Its summary says the proposed rule would:

1. provide specifications for 8.5 × 11 stationery authorized by JCP;
2. increase agency authority and responsibility for economical and efficient stationery selection and use;
3. delegate authority over envelope markings/designs;
4. revise letterhead design standards; and
5. cancel the temporary B-5 regime and its supplement.

The problem object had therefore expanded from *conversion between sizes* to *ongoing stationery governance*.

### 1983 permanent rule

The July 1983 final rule no longer reads primarily as a conversion instruction. Section 101-11.603 establishes mandatory standards for selection and use of blank and printed stationery and defines stationery as an administrative category. It includes procurement/stocking, letterhead, envelopes, forms, and waivers.

Especially revealing is §101-11.603-4: agencies must maintain economical inventory levels, avoid deterioration and obsolescence, generally limit stocks to about a one-year supply, and limit stationery styles and sizes to the minimum needed for efficient program operations.

Thus the durable regulatory object after conversion was not merely `8.5 × 11 paper`; it was a broader **stationery-management regime**.

## Problem transformation

A more accurate chain is now:

```text
1920s
heterogeneous stationery / material economy
    -> 8 × 10.5 government standard

1969–1978
legacy divergence becomes a standards-coordination object
    -> JCP selects 8.5 × 11 for bounded stationery/correspondence uses

1979–1980
conversion problem
    -> dual-size coexistence, stock depletion, workflow adaptation,
       waiver and procurement cutoff

1980–1983
conversion machinery itself becomes temporary legacy
    -> proposed permanent rulemaking remains open

1983
permanent FPMR stationery-management regime
    -> B-5 and Supplement 1 explicitly canceled
```

This is stronger than a simple `old standard -> new standard` narrative.

## Historical actor / later reconstruction / present analysis

### Historical actors

Safe actor-level statements:

- JCP had authorized the new stationery size before the 1979 conversion regulation.
- NARS/GSA explicitly managed depletion, mixed use, procurement cutoff and waivers.
- GSA/NARS explicitly proposed permanent specifications in October 1980.
- GSA's 1981 agenda still treated stationery standards as an active regulatory project.
- GSA explicitly canceled the temporary regime when it promulgated the permanent rule in July 1983.

### Later reconstruction

Later shorthand often compresses this history into a statement such as “the Federal Government adopted Letter size in 1980.” That shorthand hides at least four distinct dates/events: JCP authorization, conversion regulation, procurement/printing cutoff, and permanent FPMR codification.

### Present analytical vocabulary

The following are project labels, **not** historical actors' own terms:

- `migration regime`
- `temporary-to-permanent institutionalization`
- `multi-date endpoint`
- `problem-object expansion`
- `stationery-management regime`

## Methodological payoff

### 1. A replacement date is not necessarily a codification date

The January 1980 cutoff was operationally important, but permanent regulatory settlement occurred later. A Problem Episode schema should be able to record separately:

- decision date;
- conversion start;
- old-stock depletion period;
- procurement/production cutoff;
- temporary-rule expiration/extension;
- permanent-rule proposal;
- permanent codification;
- correction/maintenance.

### 2. Temporary regimes can outlive the nominal transition

Once a migration begins, the transition machinery may itself require maintenance. The 1980 supplement and the still-open 1981 rulemaking show that “new standard in force” did not imply “transition institutionally finished.”

### 3. Problem objects can broaden after the headline problem is solved

By 1983, the durable rule governed not just paper dimensions but inventories, procurement, design, envelopes, standard forms and waivers. A narrow size-conversion episode had been absorbed into a broader administrative-management object.

This is a useful warning against following only the headline variable that originally led us into an episode.

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not say Reagan made Letter size the Federal standard in 1980.** The recovered primary chain begins with a 1978 JCP decision and 1979 GSA/NARS implementation; the permanent FPMR rule came in 1983.
2. **Do not treat 1980-01-01 as a universal institutional endpoint.** It was a production/procurement threshold inside a managed transition.
3. **Do not infer why the final rule took until 1983.** The elapsed time is documented; the cause of the delay is not yet established.
4. **Do not infer that every old-size sheet vanished by 1983.** The rules establish governance and procurement standards, not empirical exhaustion of every agency stockpile.
5. **Do not collapse JCP and GSA/NARS into one actor.** JCP authorization and executive-branch implementation/codification are distinct institutional steps.

## Remaining uncertainties

- What comments did GSA receive on the October 1980 proposed rule?
- Why did final promulgation take until July 1983?
- Did the final rule materially change any contested provisions in response to agency or industry comments?
- Are there surviving waiver requests for post-1979 procurement/printing of 8 × 10.5 stationery?
- Can agency-level records show when mixed old/new stocks actually disappeared in practice?
