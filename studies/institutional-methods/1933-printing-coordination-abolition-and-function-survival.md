# 1933: abolition of the Permanent Conference on Printing and the difference between organizational death and problem death

## Why this note exists

The 1921 printing-economy research recovered the creation of the Permanent Conference on Printing and the widening of the problem from a narrow appropriation ceiling to publication utility, duplication, production coordination, standards, and distribution. That evidence leaves a later question: what happened when the coordinating machinery itself disappeared?

A 1934 primary institutional report plus Executive Order 6166 and National Archives record-group history now permit a bounded answer. The Permanent Conference on Printing did not simply fade away. It was caught in the abolition of the Federal Coordinating Service in 1933. At the same time, the larger reorganization transferred many procurement/coordinating functions into a new Treasury Procurement Division. This is therefore useful negative evidence against equating organizational disappearance with disappearance of the underlying problem or all associated functions.

## New findings

### 1. The conference was an operating institution for roughly thirteen years, not merely a 1921 planning proposal

The Public Printer's report for fiscal year 1934 retrospectively states that the Permanent Conference on Printing held monthly meetings at the Government Printing Office for 13 years. It identifies the conference as the first coordinating agency of the Bureau of the Budget, says it was organized at the Public Printer's suggestion with the Budget Director's approval on July 22, 1921, and records that at the first meeting on August 4, 1921 the Public Printer was selected as permanent chairman.

The conference included representatives from executive departments and independent establishments, including the District of Columbia. The same report gives its purpose as investigating and proposing uniform standards, businesslike methods, and economies in public printing/binding and distribution.

**Evidence strength:** very high for the institutional self-description and chronology; primary annual report published immediately after abolition.

Primary source:
- *Annual Report of the Public Printer, 1934*, 73d Congress, 2d Session, Government Printing Office / GovInfo serial-set scan: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-09803_00_00-002-0092-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-09803_00_00-002-0092-0000.pdf

### 2. The 1934 report gives a contemporary performance claim: about $325,000 average annual savings

The same report says the conference effected many economies and reports average annual savings of approximately $325,000. This should be preserved as an **actor/institutional claim**, not silently converted into an independently audited estimate.

It matters because abolition therefore cannot safely be narrated as the obvious termination of an institution contemporaries regarded as useless. The Public Printer's own retrospective account instead presents a functioning body with claimed savings and adopted procedures.

**Evidence strength:** high that the Public Printer made the claim; medium for the underlying economic magnitude until its accounting basis is independently reconstructed.

### 3. Executive Order 6166 explicitly abolished the Federal Coordinating Service

Executive Order 6166 of June 10, 1933, issued under the March 3, 1933 reorganization authority, states in section 17: `The Federal Coordinating Service is abolished.` Section 1 simultaneously created a Procurement Division in the Treasury Department and transferred government-wide policy/method functions for procurement, warehousing, and distribution into it. Section 19 supplied general rules for records, property, personnel, successor agencies, and disposed functions.

The Public Printer's 1934 report explicitly says the Permanent Conference on Printing was included in the Federal Coordinating Service abolished by the June 10 order.

The effective date was not instantaneous. Executive Order 6239 of August 2, 1933 deferred abolition of the Federal Coordinating Service to October 10, 1933, while permitting earlier partial abolition by presidential order.

**Evidence strength:** very high; direct executive-order text plus immediate institutional report.

Primary sources:
- National Archives codification of Executive Order 6166: https://www.archives.gov/federal-register/codification/executive-order/06166.html
- Executive Order 6239 (August 2, 1933), American Presidency Project transcription: https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-6239-organization-executive-agencies
- *Annual Report of the Public Printer, 1934* (above).

### 4. Abolition of the umbrella service did not mean blanket destruction of every coordination function

The National Archives' administrative history for Record Group 137 describes the Federal Coordinating Service as established in 1921 and abolished by Executive Order 6166 in 1933. Crucially, it states that **most functions**, including those of the Federal Traffic Board, were transferred to the Treasury Procurement Division.

This does **not** yet prove that the Permanent Conference on Printing's own functions were transferred intact to Procurement. The Public Printer report says the conference was abolished; the NARA statement is aggregate-level evidence about the Federal Coordinating Service. The safe inference is narrower: the 1933 reorganization separated the fate of the organizational shell from the fate of at least many functions formerly housed within the same coordinating system.

**Evidence strength:** high for the aggregate transfer; unresolved for printing-specific functional succession.

Source:
- National Archives, RG 137, Records of the Federal Supply Service: https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/137.html

## Actor-level problem formulation

### What 1921 actors had actually problematized

By late 1921 the Bureau of the Budget and Permanent Conference treated printing as a coordination/economy problem involving standards, rush work, duplication, distribution, annual reports, and the relation between public benefit and expense. See the companion package `1921-budget-bureau-printing-economy-problem-formulation.md`.

### What 1933 actors actually did

The Roosevelt reorganization order framed its action at a higher administrative level: regrouping, consolidation, transfer, and abolition of executive agencies/functions for economy and organization. It abolished the Federal Coordinating Service and created/consolidated procurement authority elsewhere.

The available evidence does **not** show that Roosevelt or the drafters specifically concluded that the printing-coordination problem had disappeared, nor that they separately evaluated the Permanent Conference's claimed savings.

## Later reconstruction

The National Archives later reconstructs the Federal Coordinating Service as a 1921–33 institution whose **most functions** moved to Procurement. GPO institutional history reconstructs the Permanent Conference itself as abolished in 1933 after years of monthly operation and claimed savings.

These are useful institutional genealogies, but they should not be substituted for a printing-specific 1933 actor rationale that has not yet been recovered.

## Present-day analytical payoff

Candidate distinction:

```text
organizational death
        !=
problem death
        !=
function death
        !=
solution-form death
```

A second candidate fixture is **`reorganization-induced solution displacement`**: a solution organization can disappear because a higher-level administrative architecture is being rebuilt, even where there is no recovered evidence that its underlying object-level problem was judged solved or unreal.

This remains a research label, not historical vocabulary.

## Why this matters for Problem History

This case warns against a common endpoint error. If a timeline sees `Permanent Conference on Printing, 1921–1933`, it is tempting to encode 1933 as the end of the printing-coordination problem. The evidence currently supports only the end of this particular organization.

A robust Problem Episode therefore needs separate terminal fields for at least:

- organization ended;
- statutory/administrative authority ended;
- function transferred;
- practice continued elsewhere;
- problem formulation disappeared;
- problem object materially disappeared.

Those endpoints can diverge.

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not infer failure from abolition.** The Public Printer contemporaneously claimed sustained savings; abolition occurred inside a much broader executive reorganization.
2. **Do not infer success from the savings claim.** `$325,000/year` is currently an institutional self-report whose accounting basis has not been reconstructed.
3. **Do not infer printing-specific transfer from NARA's `most functions` statement.** That is aggregate Federal Coordinating Service evidence.
4. **Do not call 1933 the disappearance of the printing-economy problem.** The present evidence establishes organizational abolition, not problem extinction.
5. **Do not project the New Deal reorganization rationale backward into the 1921 creation episode.** These are different actor-level problem settings.

## Uncertainties / source debt

Highest-value unresolved question: **what happened specifically to the Permanent Conference on Printing's functions after abolition?**

Targets:

1. Treasury Procurement Division orders and organization charts from October 1933 onward for printing/publications coordination;
2. Public Printer correspondence during June–October 1933 about the conference's termination;
3. Federal Coordinating Service subject files in NARA RG 137 for printing/conference disposition;
4. Bureau of the Budget records explaining why the conference itself was not retained or transferred;
5. accounting records behind the Public Printer's ~$325,000 average annual savings claim.
