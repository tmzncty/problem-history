# 1954–1965: report pruning becomes a recurring review cycle

## Research question

Did the 1954 elimination of statutory reports remain an isolated cleanup episode, or did later historical actors treat report-pruning as a repeatable administrative/legislative process?

## Finding

Fresh primary evidence supports the second answer. The 1954 action sits inside a recurring review cycle that was actor-explicit by the early 1960s and procedurally more selective by 1965.

This does **not** prove one uninterrupted bureaucracy or a single stable Problem Episode from 1928 onward. It does show that later actors explicitly remembered earlier rounds and reused the same general solution form: review inherited statutory reports, solicit agency/committee judgments, then repeal or reduce only selected requirements.

## 1. 1954: H.R. 6290 was already an item-by-item usefulness review

The April 27, 1954 House floor explanation of H.R. 6290 is direct actor-level evidence. Committee chairman Clare Hoffman said the bill would remove statutory reporting provisions that “experience has demonstrated to be unnecessary.” In response to Rep. Brooks, Hoffman accepted the summary that the bill discontinued reports “of no use to the Congress.” He also framed the review as a comparison between usefulness/savings and preparation cost.

The floor exchange points members to the committee report for the item-level detail. A newly recovered archival finding aid independently identifies that missing document as **House Report No. 1193, _Discontinuing Certain Reports Now Required by Law_**, preserved in the Richard J. O’Melia Collection at Notre Dame together with H.R. 6290 material. This does not substitute for reading the report, but it resolves the report identity and gives a concrete archival retrieval target.

Primary / archival sources:

- Congressional Record, House, Apr. 27, 1954, p. 5511: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1954-pt4/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1954-pt4-17-2.pdf
- University of Notre Dame Archives, Richard J. O’Melia Collection, CRJO 1/22, listing H.R. 6290 and House Report No. 1193: https://archivesspace.library.nd.edu/repositories/2/top_containers/58490

**Evidence strength:** high for the House floor formulation; high for archival existence/identity of H. Rept. 83-1193; report contents remain unrecovered.

## 2. 1960/early-1960s: Bureau of the Budget explicitly remembers the 1954 round

A later Bureau of the Budget transmittal, reproduced in congressional material, says that a similar proposal in **1954 resulted in repeal of 32 reporting requirements**. It also says that a **1958** draft, after review by the House and Senate Government Operations Committees, became **Public Law 86-533**, repealing 26 reporting requirements.

The same transmittal describes its own review method. Statutory reporting requirements were examined for whether they remained necessary, duplicated other requirements, could be simplified, or could be submitted less frequently. The proposed package was based on executive-agency recommendations. The Bureau explicitly said it did not intend to eliminate reports that remained useful; targets were reports made obsolete or excessive by changed conditions, new legislation, or other reasons, or reports more detailed/costly than current conditions justified.

Primary source reproduced in S. Rept. 89-545 / Serial Set 12662-4:

- GovInfo search-accessible text/PDF, _Discontinue or modify certain reporting requirements of law_: https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/SERIALSET-12662_04_00-017-0545-0000/

The 1960 enactment itself is Public Law 86-533 (June 29, 1960), 74 Stat. 245, “An Act To repeal certain provisions of law requiring the submission of certain reports to Congress, and for other purposes.”

**Evidence strength:** high for actor-explicit retrospective linkage 1954 → 1958/1960 and for the Bureau’s stated selection criteria. Do not convert the Bureau’s count of repealed requirements into an independently audited measure without item-level checking.

## 3. 1965: review acquires an explicit committee-jurisdiction filter

S. Rept. 89-545, entered into the Aug. 12, 1965 Congressional Record, describes another round. The draft was circulated to the chairmen of Senate standing committees to ask whether they retained an interest in reporting requirements proposed for repeal. Four provisions were deleted before S. 2150 was introduced; after introduction, committee recommendations caused another sixteen provisions to be deleted. Banking and Currency additionally proposed changing two quarterly reports to annual reports, and those modifications were incorporated.

The report then states that the bill was similar to legislation enacted “from time to time” to eliminate reports no longer needed. As amended, it repealed 45 reporting requirements and modified 11.

Primary sources:

- S. Rept. 89-545 metadata and official Serial Set object: https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/SERIALSET-12662_04_00-017-0545-0000/
- Congressional Record, Aug. 12, 1965, p. 20146 (reproducing the committee explanation): https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1965-pt15/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1965-pt15-3-1.pdf

**Evidence strength:** high. This is direct committee evidence for a selective review mechanism in which committees of jurisdiction could preserve candidate reports or alter frequency rather than accept an all-or-nothing repeal list.

## Actor formulations vs later reconstruction vs present analysis

### Historical actors

The recovered actors themselves formulate the problem in terms of:

- reports that experience shows are unnecessary or no longer useful;
- preparation cost relative to usefulness/savings;
- changed conditions and new legislation;
- duplication;
- excessive detail or frequency;
- continuing committee interest in particular information flows.

By 1965 the Senate committee itself also describes such legislation as something enacted “from time to time.”

### Later reconstruction

CRS’s 2012/2013 study of GPRAMA places these older efforts in a longer history of executive/congressional reexamination of statutory reporting requirements and contrasts them with later statutory review mechanisms. That is useful institutional reconstruction, not evidence that 1954 actors anticipated GPRAMA.

Secondary source:

- CRS, _Reexamination of Agency Reporting Requirements: Annual Process Under the GPRA Modernization Act of 2010 (GPRAMA)_, R42490: https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R42490.html

### Present analytical shorthand

Candidate researcher-side labels only:

- `episodic cleanup -> recurring review cycle`
- `report-lifecycle review`
- `jurisdiction-filtered pruning`
- `repeal-or-frequency-reduction choice`

Do not attribute these labels to historical actors.

## Problem-History payoff

The new evidence changes the genealogy from a sequence of isolated repeal statutes into a historically recognized repeated review form:

```text
1928 mass report repeal / demand-route preservation
        ↓ explicit precedent recovered elsewhere in repository
1945 report pruning
        ↓
1954 H.R. 6290 / PL 83-706
experience-based usefulness/cost review
32 requirements repealed (later Bureau retrospective)
        ↓
1958 executive draft
        ↓ committee review
1960 PL 86-533
26 requirements repealed
        ↓
early-1960s Bureau review
necessity / duplication / detail / frequency tests
        ↓
1965 S. 2150 review
standing-committee interest filter
candidate provisions rescued + frequencies modified
45 repeals + 11 modifications in amended bill
```

The important transformation is not merely “more reports were repealed.” The **review process itself becomes a reusable solution form**. By 1965, the process contains a visible veto/filter stage for committees that still value particular information flows.

This supplies a useful distinction:

```text
report judged obsolete by originating/executive agency
!=
report judged dispensable by committee of jurisdiction
!=
report finally repealed
```

It also strengthens an existing repository fixture from the 1945 work:

`administrative redundancy != political dispensability`

The 1965 evidence shows that this was not only an item-level rescue phenomenon: it could be built into the review procedure itself.

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not claim uninterrupted institutional continuity from 1928 to 1965.** Repeated solution forms and explicit retrospective links are not proof of one continuously operating program.
2. **Do not infer that every 1954 repeal was useless in an objective sense.** “Unnecessary” and “no use to Congress” are actor formulations attached to a legislative selection process.
3. **Do not infer the reasons for individual 1954 items from the package-level floor rationale.** H. Rept. 83-1193 must still be recovered and read.
4. **Do not equate agency recommendation with final legislative judgment.** The 1965 committee-filter evidence directly shows candidate repeals could be rescued.
5. **Do not collapse repeal and frequency reduction.** By 1965 these are explicit alternative dispositions.
6. **Do not project GPRAMA backward.** CRS can establish a later institutional genealogy, but GPRAMA’s annual statutory process is not the same mechanism as the 1950s–1960s packages.
