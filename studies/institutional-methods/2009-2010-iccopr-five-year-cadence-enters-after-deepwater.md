# 2009–2010: the five-year ICCOPR planning cadence enters in the post-Deepwater substitute

## Research question

When did the proposed statutory requirement to revise the federal oil-pollution research plan at least once every five years first enter H.R. 2693? Did it already exist before the Deepwater Horizon disaster, or was it part of the bill's post-disaster redesign?

## Finding

The version history resolves the ambiguity left by the earlier cadence genealogy.

The **June 3, 2009 introduced H.R. 2693 did not contain a recurring five-year plan-update requirement**. Its proposed section 7004 required a Federal oil-spill research plan not later than one year after the research assessment, but supplied no recurring revision cadence.

The **June 16, 2009 Energy and Environment Subcommittee markup** amended the bill, but the committee report's amendment history does not identify any five-year recurring plan rule. The subcommittee bill still belonged to the introduced bill's basic architecture: a rewritten committee/program, a new assessment, a new plan, extramural grants, and an annual report.

On **July 14, 2010**, after the April 20 Deepwater Horizon explosion and spill, the full House Science and Technology Committee considered a Woolsey **amendment in the nature of a substitute (ANS)** that struck and replaced the language of H.R. 2693. The reported/House-passed text then amended the existing OPA §7001(b)(1) deadline so that the research and technology plan would be submitted after enactment and revised `periodically thereafter, as appropriate, but not less than once every 5 years`.

Thus the evidence supports the narrower provenance claim:

```text
2009-06-03 introduced H.R. 2693: no recurring plan cadence
        ↓
2009-06-16 subcommittee amendments: no identified five-year cadence
        ↓
2010-04-20 Deepwater Horizon explosion
        ↓
2010-07-14 full-committee ANS: bill architecture replaced
        ↓
2010 reported / House-passed text: ≥5-year plan revision rule
```

This is a genuine advance over merely observing that the 2010 House-passed bill contained a five-year rule.

## Historical actor formulations

The 2009 actors framed the problem before Deepwater as an already-existing weakness in federal oil-spill R&D. At the June 4, 2009 hearing, committee actors called for a more robust R&D strategy and a reinvigorated program; the introduced bill itself responded with coordination, assessment, planning, grants, and annual reporting.

After Deepwater, committee actors explicitly intensified the problem formulation. The committee's later activity report says the disaster exposed the need for an effective and coordinated oil-spill research program. At the July 14, 2010 markup, Woolsey argued that spill-response technology had changed too little since Exxon Valdez, while the committee described the substitute as prioritizing research and ensuring ongoing development of methods and technologies.

These formulations establish that the problem was **not created by Deepwater Horizon**. It pre-existed the accident. The accident instead appears in the actor record as evidence that intensified and reshaped an existing R&D-coordination problem.

## Later reconstruction

The later legislative genealogy can now be stated more precisely:

```text
1990 OPA: initial research-plan requirement; no recurring statutory cadence
2009 introduced H.R. 2693: new assessment/plan architecture; no recurring cadence
2010 post-Deepwater full-committee substitute: ≥5-year statutory cadence
2011 H.R. 1568: repeats ≥5-year proposal
2015 ICCOPR plan: organizational six-year cycle
2019 S. 2297: ten-year statutory cadence already present at introduction
2021 enactment: ten-year statutory update rule
```

The important new fact is that the first located five-year rule belongs to the **post-Deepwater legislative stage**, not the original June 2009 proposal.

## Present analytical interpretation

Researcher-side shorthand only:

`pre-existing problem → focusing event → solution redesign`

This episode is useful because it prevents two opposite hindsight errors. Deepwater did not originate the oil-spill R&D problem or H.R. 2693, but neither should the post-Deepwater five-year cadence be projected backward into the pre-disaster bill.

A more exact transformation is:

```text
problem already recognized
    ↓
one-shot assessment + plan proposal
    ↓ focusing event
bill architecture reopened
    ↓
recurring plan-revision rule added
```

This suggests a useful Problem History field for major events: **effect on an already-open solution design**. A focusing event can change the proposed solution without originating the problem itself.

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not say Deepwater caused H.R. 2693.** The bill was introduced June 3, 2009, more than ten months before the April 20, 2010 explosion.
2. **Do not yet say Deepwater specifically caused the five-year number.** The timing is now established, but no actor statement has yet been recovered saying why five years was chosen.
3. **Do not infer that every element of the July 2010 substitute was a response to Deepwater.** The substitute also recombined or restored elements of the pre-existing OPA architecture.
4. **Do not claim the June 16, 2009 amended text lacked the cadence with absolute certainty until a clean post-markup bill print is recovered.** The amendment roster/report history gives strong negative evidence, but direct text would be stronger.
5. **Do not treat later 6-year and 10-year cadences as straightforward descendants of the 2010 five-year proposal without actor-level transmission evidence.** Similar solution forms do not by themselves establish textual borrowing.
