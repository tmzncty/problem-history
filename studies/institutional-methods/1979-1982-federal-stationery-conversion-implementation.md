# 1979–1982: Federal stationery conversion becomes a workflow-level migration problem

## Why this packet matters

The existing 1921–1980 paper-size chain reaches the Joint Committee on Printing's December 1978 decision to replace the 8 × 10.5 inch Government stationery standard with 8.5 × 11, effective January 1, 1980. The remaining implementation debt was concrete: what did GSA/NARS actually require agencies to do, and what does that reveal about the historical problem as actors encountered it?

FPMR Temporary Regulation B-5, published October 2, 1979, supplies that missing implementation edge. It shows that the conversion was not modeled as a single specification substitution. NARS treated it as a coordinated transition across correspondence, mail, directives, forms, copying, word processing, procurement, and inventory depletion. A 1980 supplement then extended the temporary regulation's expiration to July 15, 1982, showing that the implementation regime outlived the nominal January 1, 1980 switch date.

## New actor-level findings

### 1. The migration had an explicit dual-standard phase

B-5 authorized agencies, during conversion, to use both old and new stationery sizes. Until January 1, 1980, agencies received a waiver from existing 8-inch-wide size requirements provided that they used reasonable, orderly, and economical means to deplete existing stocks and replaced depleted stocks only with the new 8.5-inch-wide sizes. The Federal Supply Service could continue substituting old-size stock for agency orders until inventories were exhausted.

This is direct evidence against representing the transition as an instantaneous replacement event.

**Evidence strength:** very high.

### 2. Old-stock depletion was governed by relative transition cost, not simple disposal

The conversion procedures instructed agencies to use existing 8 × 10.5 stationery, but explicitly warned against redistributing old stock where redistribution would cost more than purchasing and distributing new 8.5-inch-wide stock. Agencies could even intermix old and new stationery if that was the only way to deplete old stock.

The historical actor problem was therefore not simply `eliminate old paper`; it included an explicit economic comparison among depletion, redistribution, replacement, and mixed use.

**Evidence strength:** very high.

### 3. Paper-size conversion propagated into multiple management systems

Attachment A separately specified conversion practices for:

- **correspondence:** use old stock, allow mixed sizes, and adjust margins when new bond paper was used with old carbon sets;
- **mail:** ensure return envelopes could accommodate the returned documents;
- **directives:** use a 50-percent revision threshold to decide whether to reissue an entire directive in the new format, allow mixed-size directives below that threshold, and reprint short directives under specified conditions;
- **forms:** consider the new size for new/revised forms commonly interfiled with correspondence, while using existing old-size stocks first; other form sizes remained use-dependent;
- **copy management:** consider 8.5 × 11 copy stock and use it when copying documents containing both sizes;
- **word processing:** readjust preset margins on some word processors so the wider paper could use a 6.5-inch rather than 6-inch line length.

The regulation therefore provides direct U.S. actor evidence for the mechanism that the prior research could only establish generically from contemporary standards literature: paper size was embedded in complementary equipment, consumables, document systems, and workflows.

**Evidence strength:** very high for these specified interactions. Do not generalize beyond the listed systems without further evidence.

### 4. The scope boundary is more nuanced than “stationery only”

The Federal Register summary says the JCP action directly affected office stationery stocks, but adds that forms, directives and similar publications, electrostatically copied reports, and copy-machine or duplicator stocks **may be affected also**. NARS therefore developed conversion procedures for correspondence, mail, directives, forms, copy, and word-processing management practices.

This sharpens the existing `layer-bounded reversal` finding. The JCP directive did not simply redefine all printed products, but implementation effects propagated beyond the nominal stationery category through adjoining workflows.

A useful distinction is:

```text
formal scope
    !=
implementation spillover
```

This is present analytical language; the underlying cross-workflow effects are explicit in B-5.

### 5. January 1, 1980 was a prohibition threshold, not the end of implementation

After December 31, 1979, agencies were prohibited from printing or procuring the printing of old-size stationery unless NARS approved a written waiver. But B-5 itself was a temporary regulation with an initial expiration of July 15, 1980.

On August 1, 1980, B-5 Supplement 1 extended the regulation's expiration to **July 15, 1982**. The supplement did not announce a reversal of the new standard; it extended the implementation instrument.

Thus the episode needs at least three dates:

```text
1978-12-18  decision
1979-10-02  implementation regime begins
1980-01-01  old-size new-production/procurement prohibition threshold
1982-07-15  extended expiration of temporary conversion regulation
```

**Evidence strength:** very high.

## Revised Problem transformation

The endpoint should now be represented as a migration process rather than a point event:

```text
1920s economy solution: 8 × 10.5
        ↓
long-lived Government-specific infrastructure
        ↓
1969 renewed standards-network problem
        ↓
1978 JCP bounded replacement decision
        ↓
1979 NARS/GSA conversion regime
        ↓
dual-standard coexistence + stock depletion
+ workflow adaptation + waiver mechanism
        ↓
1980 prohibition on new old-size stationery
        ↓
conversion administration remains live through 1982
```

The strongest methodological result is:

```text
adoption date
    !=
implementation date
    !=
legacy-stock exhaustion date
    !=
conversion-regime endpoint
```

## Historical actors / later reconstruction / present analysis

### Historical actors

Directly supportable claims from B-5:

- NARS/GSA regarded the conversion as requiring procedures across correspondence, mail, directives, forms, copying, and word processing.
- Existing old-size stocks were to be depleted economically rather than simply discarded.
- Mixed-size documents and stationery were explicitly tolerated during migration.
- Some word processors required margin adjustment for the new width.
- Envelope/document fit and carbon-set compatibility were explicit implementation concerns.
- NARS retained a written-waiver gate for old-size stationery after December 31, 1979.
- The temporary conversion regulation remained formally in force, by extension, until July 15, 1982.

### Later reconstruction

Later summaries often compress the event to “the Federal Government switched to 8.5 × 11 on January 1, 1980.” That is useful shorthand for the new-standard threshold but erases the actor-visible migration regime before and after that date.

### Present analytical labels

The following are project-level constructs, not actor terms:

- `dual-standard migration phase`
- `implementation spillover`
- `workflow-coupled standard transition`
- `multi-date endpoint`

## Methodological payoff

### Replacement events should be modeled as transitions

For durable standards, add fields or relations for:

- decision date;
- implementation-rule date;
- effective/prohibition threshold;
- legacy inventory policy;
- coexistence rules;
- waiver/exception authority;
- complementary workflows/equipment requiring adaptation;
- formal conversion-regime endpoint.

This prevents a standard change from being encoded as a single edge between two specifications.

### Direct implementation evidence can upgrade a generic mechanism

Earlier work recovered contemporary North American evidence that machinery and workflows can lock in paper sizes, but correctly refused to treat that as proof of U.S. Federal decision causation. B-5 now supplies direct U.S. Federal actor evidence for several concrete complementarities during implementation: word-processor margins, carbon sets, envelopes, copy stock, forms, directives, and interfiled records.

This still does **not** prove that any one of those complementarities caused the JCP's 1978 choice. It proves they were real conversion problems recognized by the implementing authority.

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not turn implementation difficulty into decision causation.** B-5 shows what had to be adapted after the JCP decision, not necessarily why JCP chose 8.5 × 11.
2. **Do not treat January 1, 1980 as an instantaneous physical conversion.** Old inventory could coexist during depletion, and the conversion regulation remained active afterward.
3. **Do not say agencies were ordered simply to discard old stocks.** The regulation explicitly sought economical depletion and even allowed old/new intermixing.
4. **Do not universalize stationery rules to all forms and printed products.** Forms remained partly use-dependent, while B-5 describes spillover effects rather than a blanket all-paper mandate.
5. **Do not infer that the 1982 extension means the old standard survived as the normal procurement standard.** The supplement extends the conversion regulation; it does not repeal the post-1979 prohibition on new old-size stationery.

## Remaining uncertainty / source debt

- Why exactly was B-5 extended to July 15, 1982? Supplement 1 states the extension but the recovered notice does not give a substantive reason.
- Recover the permanent FPMR provisions that replaced the temporary conversion instrument after 1982 and determine how the final stationery rule encoded 8.5 × 11.
- Recover agency-level implementation files to test how long old stocks actually persisted and how often NARS granted post-1979 waivers.
- Recover JCP deliberations before December 18, 1978 to distinguish decision drivers from implementation consequences.
