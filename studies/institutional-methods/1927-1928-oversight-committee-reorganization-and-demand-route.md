# 1927–1928: committee reorganization as institutional context for the report-pruning demand route

## Research question

The current branch has established that the 1928 mass repeal of statutory reports did not simply remove information flows. Section 2 of the Act of May 29, 1928 created a request-based route by which the House and Senate Committees on Expenditures in the Executive Departments—or seven House / five Senate members thereof—could demand information from executive departments and independent establishments.

A missing contextual question is why this substitute route was vested in these particular committees. New evidence shows that, on the House side, the safeguard was enacted immediately after a major reorganization and jurisdictional expansion of the very committee that received the new information-demand authority.

This packet does **not** claim that committee reorganization caused section 2 or explains the seven/five-member thresholds. It establishes a close institutional chronology and identifies the records needed to test transmission and design intent.

## New finding

The House Committee on Expenditures in the Executive Departments was itself newly consolidated when Congress enacted the 1928 report-pruning statute:

```text
pre-1927: eleven departmental expenditure committees
        ↓
Dec. 5, 1927: House abolishes the eleven committees and consolidates their functions
        ↓
new Committee on Expenditures in the Executive Departments (21 members)
        ↓
Mar. 17, 1928: jurisdiction expanded to independent establishments and commissions
        ↓
May 29, 1928: §2 gives this committee / any seven members a statutory executive-information request route
```

The National Archives' official *Guide to the Records of the U.S. House of Representatives* states that the eleven departmental expenditure committees had become relatively inactive after World War I, a condition aggravated after creation of the GAO in 1921 because members believed GAO was protecting congressional interests. Because the committees were accomplishing little, the House abolished them on December 5, 1927 and replaced them with one Committee on Expenditures in the Executive Departments. The Guide states that the new committee had 21 members and that its jurisdiction was expanded in 1928 to independent establishments and commissions.

Source:

- National Archives, *Guide to House Records: Chapter 11 — Records of the Government Operations Committee and Its Predecessors*, paras. 11.13–11.14: https://www.archives.gov/legislative/guide/house/chapter-11.html

**Evidence strength: high** for committee creation, consolidation, size, and jurisdictional development. NARA is an official institutional archival guide; it cites the House Journal / Cannon's Precedents for the underlying House action.

A later official House committee history independently quotes *Cannon's Precedents*, vol. VII, §2041: the committee was created December 5, 1927 by consolidating the eleven expenditure committees, and on March 17, 1928 the House enlarged its jurisdiction to include independent establishments and commissions.

Source:

- House Committee on Government Reform and Oversight, historical background, 105th Congress: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CPRT-105HPRT46663/pdf/CPRT-105HPRT46663.pdf

**Evidence strength: high** for the House institutional chronology; this is later official retrospective documentation, not actor-contemporaneous explanation of §2.

## Section 2 now sits inside an institutional reorganization, not only a report-repeal story

The enacted section 2 required every executive department and independent establishment, upon request of the House Committee on Expenditures in the Executive Departments or any seven of its members, or the corresponding Senate committee or any five members thereof, to furnish information relating to matters within the committee's jurisdiction. A 1947 Congressional Record discussion reproduced the operative statutory language and identified it as May 29, 1928, ch. 901, §2, 45 Stat. 996.

Primary source / later actor quotation of statute:

- *Congressional Record*, House, 1947, reproducing former 5 U.S.C. §105(a): https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1947-pt1/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1947-pt1-19-2.pdf

Official current codification history:

- 5 U.S.C. §2954, House Office of the Law Revision Counsel, derivation from May 29, 1928, ch. 901, §2, 45 Stat. 996: https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&f=treesort&fq=true&granuleId=USC-prelim-title5-section2954

**Evidence strength: very high** for statutory text and derivation.

The timing matters. On the House side, the request power was not assigned to an unchanged old committee. It was assigned to a body that had been consolidated only about six months earlier and whose jurisdiction had been broadened roughly ten weeks before enactment.

This yields a stronger process context:

```text
fragmented departmental expenditure oversight
        ↓
House consolidates oversight committee structure
        ↓
new committee gains government-wide / independent-establishment reach
        ↓
Congress eliminates 128 inherited routine reporting mandates
        +
places a demand-triggered information route in the consolidated oversight committee
```

The final arrow is a **chronological and institutional conjunction**, not yet a demonstrated causal design chain.

## Actor formulation versus present reconstruction

### Historical / legally explicit

Directly supportable:

- the House abolished eleven expenditure committees and consolidated their functions in December 1927;
- the new House committee had 21 members;
- its jurisdiction was expanded in March 1928 to independent establishments and commissions;
- §2 of the May 29, 1928 Act vested information-request authority in the House committee or any seven members, and in the Senate counterpart or any five members;
- the House report on the report-pruning bill said the information provision was added so that abolition of periodic reports would not cast doubt on the House's ability to obtain the information.

### Later institutional reconstruction

NARA and later House histories explain the 1927 consolidation partly through the inactivity / low productivity of the old departmental expenditure committees and the changed oversight environment after GAO's creation. This is high-quality institutional history, but it is not yet evidence that the sponsors of the 1928 report-pruning bill themselves invoked those considerations when designing §2.

### Present analytical shorthand

The following are researcher labels only:

- `oversight consolidation`;
- `routine push -> targeted pull`;
- `committee-centered demand route`;
- `information architecture redesign`;
- `institutional complementarity`.

Do not attribute these phrases to Congress, the Bureau of Efficiency, or the committees.

## Why this changes the Problem Episode

The 1928 episode has so far been recoverable as:

```text
inventory of inherited statutory reports
→ Bureau of Efficiency review
→ proposed repeal
→ congressional concern over information loss
→ request-based safeguard added
→ 128-report repeal + demand route enacted
```

The new evidence adds a parallel institutional track:

```text
old departmental expenditure committees judged weak/inactive (later official reconstruction)
→ Dec. 1927 House consolidation
→ Mar. 1928 jurisdictional expansion
→ May 1928 new committee becomes statutory recipient of demand authority
```

That suggests a more general Problem-History schema: when a solution removes one information mechanism, ask whether the substitute mechanism depends on **simultaneous institutional change in the receiving actor**.

Useful fields:

```text

```

This case demonstrates why `periodic report -> on-demand request` is incomplete if the institutional identity and jurisdiction of the requester are ignored.

## A useful numerical clue, but not yet an explanation

NARA states that the new House committee consisted of 21 members. Section 2 permitted **any seven members** to trigger the information duty: exactly one third of the committee's stated membership.

This is potentially important, but currently only an arithmetic observation:

```text
House committee membership = 21
statutory subgroup threshold = 7
7 / 21 = 1/3
```

Do **not** write that Congress deliberately chose a one-third minority threshold unless contemporary debate, committee minutes, bill drafts, or reports say so. The Senate's five-member threshold likewise requires the contemporary Senate committee size and design history before comparison.

The threshold is especially worth pursuing because the modern litigation literature often treats §2954 as a minority-member information mechanism. That later characterization must not be projected backward without 1928 actor evidence.

## Relation to later life of the provision

The request route survived the immediate 1928 cleanup episode. Current 5 U.S.C. §2954 derives directly from §2, with committee names updated through later reorganizations. Later litigation therefore encounters a provision whose origin was embedded in the same statute that abolished 128 routine reports.

A 2020 D.C. Circuit opinion, *Maloney v. Murphy*, reconstructed this origin and emphasized that Congress paired repeal of mandatory reports with a mechanism enabling members of the oversight committees to request information. That opinion is useful as later legal reconstruction, not as evidence of what 1928 actors consciously meant beyond the surviving legislative history.

Source:

- *Maloney v. Murphy*, D.C. Cir. (2020): https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/18-5305/18-5305-2020-12-29.html

**Evidence strength: high for later legal genealogy; not actor-primary for 1928 intent.**

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not call §2 a modern minority-party right in 1928 without evidence.** Seven members could constitute a subgroup without the statute specifying party composition.
2. **Do not infer causation from chronology.** December 1927 consolidation and March 1928 jurisdiction expansion closely precede §2, but we do not yet have an actor saying that §2 was designed because of those reforms.
3. **Do not merge House and Senate institutional histories.** The House consolidation is now well documented; the Senate committee's membership, jurisdiction, and contemporaneous reorganization need separate reconstruction.
4. **Do not say the 1927 committee reform was itself part of the Bureau of Efficiency report-pruning project.** They may intersect, but transmission evidence is still missing.
5. **Do not infer why seven/five were chosen.** The one-third House ratio is a research clue, not a historical explanation.

## Bottom line

The new evidence does not yet tell us *why* Congress selected seven House members and five Senators. It does establish that the House-side demand route was attached to a newly consolidated and freshly expanded oversight institution. The 1928 episode therefore belongs not only to the history of eliminating obsolete reports but also to a contemporaneous reconfiguration of **who in Congress would receive, request, and organize executive information after routine reporting requirements were removed**.
