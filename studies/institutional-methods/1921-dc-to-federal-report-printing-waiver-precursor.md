# 1921 DC → federal report-printing waiver precursor

## Research question

The repository already establishes the June 16, 1921 federal-wide authority allowing executive departments and establishments to discontinue printing annual or special reports when necessary to remain within printing-and-binding appropriations, while retaining originals for public inspection. This note asks whether that architecture appeared earlier in 1921.

## New finding

Yes. A closely matching mechanism was enacted first for the District of Columbia and debated before the federal-wide June 1921 provision.

### January–February 1921: District of Columbia mechanism

In Senate consideration of the District of Columbia appropriation bill on January 15, 1921, an amendment authorized the District Commissioners to discontinue printing annual or special reports in order to remain within the relevant appropriation, while requiring originals of unprinted reports to remain on file for public inspection. The amendment was agreed to.

On February 15, 1921, the House considered the Senate amendment in disagreement. The amendment reduced the printing appropriation from $10,000 to $5,000 and added the discontinuance/public-inspection proviso. The House receded and agreed to the Senate amendment. The resulting statutory text retained the same architecture: printing could be discontinued to stay within the appropriation; originals had to remain available in the Commissioners' offices for public inspection.

Primary evidence:

- Congressional Record, Senate, Jan. 15, 1921: amendment text and adoption. Congress.gov: https://www.congress.gov/66/crecb/1921/01/15/GPO-CRECB-1921-pt2-v60-8.pdf
- Congressional Record, House, Feb. 15, 1921: conference/disagreement handling; House receded and agreed. GovInfo/Congress.gov: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1921-pt3-v60/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1921-pt3-v60-18-2.pdf
- Statutes at Large, District of Columbia appropriation provision (42 Stat.; text preserved in volume scan): annual/special report printing could be discontinued within the appropriation, with originals retained for public inspection.

Evidence strength: **very high** for existence, chronology, and statutory architecture.

### June 1921: federal-wide generalization

On June 3, 1921, during Senate consideration of the Second Deficiency bill, a Senate amendment inserted a federal-wide provision authorizing heads of executive departments and Government establishments to discontinue printing annual or special reports in order to keep FY1922 printing-and-binding expenditures within appropriations, again requiring originals to remain on file for public inspection.

The June 9 conference record shows the House receding from disagreement to the Senate amendment with a revised package that retained this provision. It became law June 16, 1921 in the Second Deficiency Act, fiscal year 1921, 42 Stat. 29.

Primary evidence:

- Congressional Record, Senate, June 3, 1921, p. 2084: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1921-pt2-v61/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1921-pt2-v61-24.pdf
- Congressional Record, June 9, 1921, conference handling: https://www.congress.gov/67/crecb/1921/06/09/GPO-CRECB-1921-pt3-v61-4.pdf
- Second Deficiency Act, fiscal year 1921, approved June 16, 1921, 42 Stat. 29: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-42/pdf/STATUTE-42-Pg29.pdf

Evidence strength: **very high**.

## Textual/structural comparison

The two mechanisms share a distinctive four-part architecture:

1. a bounded printing appropriation;
2. discretionary authority to discontinue **printing** annual or special reports;
3. the purpose of keeping expenditures within that appropriation;
4. mandatory retention of the original copy in the responsible office for **public inspection**.

Chronology therefore supports:

`DC-specific mechanism (Jan–Feb 1921) → federal-wide mechanism (June 1921)`

But chronology plus textual similarity does **not yet prove** that the drafters of the June Senate amendment copied or consciously generalized the DC provision. No direct statement of borrowing has yet been recovered.

Evidence strength for **textual precursor / candidate template**: **high**.

Evidence strength for **documented transmission or borrowing**: **medium-low / not yet demonstrated**.

## Actor-level problem formulation

The safe actor-level formulation is narrower than a modern information-policy story. In both provisions, the explicit problem was how to keep printing expenditures within a fixed appropriation while preserving an inspectable original when mass printing was discontinued.

The House's later January 5, 1923 debate over the recurring DC proviso is unusually useful for clarifying what contemporaries thought the mechanism did. Representative Cramton insisted that it did not remove a legal obligation to make/file a report; it only permitted discontinuance of printing. Other members worried that stopping printed submission might in practice reduce Congress's access. Cramton also complained that boards and branches had been printing reports to an unreasonable extent.

Primary evidence:

- Congressional Record, House, Jan. 5, 1923: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1923-pt2-v64/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1923-pt2-v64-5-2.pdf

Evidence strength: **high** for the 1923 actors' distinction between making a report and printing it; **medium** if used to interpret the motives of the original January 1921 drafters, because it is a later debate over renewal rather than the original enactment debate.

## Problem transformation

The new chronology suggests a potentially important scale transition:

`local/federal-district appropriation problem`
→ `DC-specific print-discontinuance mechanism`
→ `same architecture generalized across executive departments and establishments`
→ `annual federal renewal`
→ `1953 permanent authority`
→ `1968 codified 44 U.S.C. §1117`

This is now a >47-year statutory chain from 1921 to 1968, and the authority remains in current Title 44. The repository's existing 1921–1953 study supplies the later nodes.

A useful present-day research label is **jurisdiction-to-general-capability scaling**: a mechanism first visible in a narrow jurisdiction becomes a government-wide capability. This is a researcher-side abstraction, not an actor term.

## Three-layer distinction

### 1. Historical actors

Directly evidenced:

- DC appropriators faced a bounded printing appropriation and authorized selective non-printing while retaining originals for inspection.
- By June 1921 Congress enacted the same basic architecture for executive departments and Government establishments generally.
- In 1923 House members explicitly disputed whether non-printing affected congressional reporting, with defenders distinguishing the obligation to make a report from the obligation to print it.

Not directly evidenced:

- that June 1921 drafters consciously copied the DC language;
- that Congress understood the June provision as a deliberate policy experiment scaled from DC;
- who first proposed the architecture or why the public-inspection safeguard took this exact form.

### 2. Later reconstruction

Later Public Printer reports reconstructed the June 1921 authority as the beginning of the recurring federal-wide printing-waiver practice. That institutional genealogy remains valid for the **government-wide authority**, but it can obscure the earlier DC-specific instance of the same architecture.

### 3. Present researcher reconstruction

Candidate analytical concepts:

- `jurisdiction-to-general-capability scaling`
- `printing/reporting obligation separation`
- `retention safeguard under distribution retrenchment`

These labels must not be attributed to 1921 actors.

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not say the June provision was copied from the DC provision yet.** Similarity + chronology establishes a precursor, not a transmission edge.
2. **Do not equate non-printing with non-reporting.** The 1923 debate explicitly shows contemporaries distinguishing them, even while some worried about practical congressional access.
3. **Do not treat public inspection as equivalent access.** A single original in an office is materially different from printed distribution.
4. **Do not let later Public Printer institutional history erase narrower predecessors.** “First federal-wide authority” and “first appearance of the mechanism” are different claims.
5. **Do not back-project 1923 explanatory debate as the exact motive of January 1921.** It is strong evidence for how the recurring provision was understood, not direct proof of original drafting intent.
