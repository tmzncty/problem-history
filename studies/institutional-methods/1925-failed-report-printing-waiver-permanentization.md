# 1925: failed permanentization of the report-printing waiver

Status: **M2 source-control / institutional-method candidate / not a frozen Problem Episode**

## 1. New finding

The existing `1921-1953-report-printing-waiver-genealogy.md` correctly identifies a long sequence in which Congress repeatedly authorized executive departments, through annual appropriations, to discontinue printing annual or special reports while preserving an original for public inspection, before making that authority permanent in 1953.

New primary evidence shows that the transition was **not simply annual repetition followed by permanentization in 1953**. Congress considered a standing, non-fiscal-year version of essentially the same authority in **1925**. The Senate accepted it, but the House explicitly struck the provision from the bill. The annual FY1926 appropriation version nevertheless remained in force.

The genealogy therefore contains a failed institutionalization attempt:

```text
1921 onward
annual fiscal-year waiver
        ↓
1925 proposed standing authority
(no fiscal-year limitation)
        ↓
Senate accepts
        ↓
House strikes section 6
        ↓
annual waiver continues
        ↓
1953 standing authority finally enacted
```

This is stronger than a simple `annual → permanent capability transition`: the permanent form was **available and legislatively attempted decades earlier, but did not survive bicameral passage**.

## 2. Primary evidence: proposed standing authority

In January 1925 the Senate considered a printing-reform bill containing section 6, headed **PRINTING OF ANNUAL REPORTS**. The section stated that heads of executive departments, independent offices, and Government establishments were authorized to discontinue printing any annual or special reports under their jurisdiction, provided that the original remained on file for public inspection.

Crucially, unlike the annual appropriations language, section 6 contained **no fiscal-year limitation** and no wording tying the authority to a particular year's printing-and-binding appropriation.

Primary source:
- *Congressional Record*, 68th Cong., 2d sess., 19 January 1925, Senate debate: https://www.congress.gov/68/crecb/1925/01/19/GPO-CRECB-1925-pt2-v66-15-1.pdf

Evidence: **very high / contemporaneous legislative text and floor record**.

The Senate record also explains the broader bill as an effort to put into legislation printing economies already achieved through Joint Committee on Printing regulation, so that a later committee could not simply restore discontinued publications. That rationale is direct for the bill's publication-reform program, but the currently recovered excerpt does **not** prove that every part of that rationale was specifically directed at section 6. Treat the section-specific motive as **medium**, not very high.

## 3. House rejection of section 6

On 28 February 1925 the House took up the same measure with section 6 still present. The printed section again contained the standing authorization to discontinue printing annual/special reports while preserving originals for public inspection.

Representative McKeown then offered a simple amendment:

> Page 3, line 10, strike out section 6.

Representative Kiess accepted the amendment, and the House agreed to it. The recovered floor excerpt gives **no substantive explanation** for McKeown's objection or Kiess's acceptance.

Primary source:
- *Congressional Record*, 68th Cong., 2d sess., 28 February 1925, House proceedings: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1925-pt5-v66/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1925-pt5-v66-6-2.pdf

Evidence:
- section 6 existed in the House text: **very high**;
- McKeown moved to strike it: **very high**;
- Kiess accepted and House agreed: **very high**;
- reason for deletion: **unknown**.

This negative legislative event matters. Absence from the enacted law was not mere drafting omission: the standing authority was visible, separately numbered, and affirmatively removed in the House.

## 4. Annual authority survived the failed permanentization

The failure of section 6 did not abolish the existing annual mechanism. The Legislative Branch Appropriation Act for FY1926 again authorized executive departments and independent establishments to discontinue printing annual/special reports **for fiscal year 1926** in order to remain within that year's printing-and-binding appropriations, while preserving originals for public inspection.

Primary source:
- 43 Stat. 1301 (1925), GovInfo: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-43/pdf/STATUTE-43-Pg1286-2.pdf

Evidence: **very high / enacted statute**.

Thus the immediate outcome was not `authority rejected`, but:

```text
standing/general authority rejected
        +
annual/fiscal authority retained
```

That distinction is essential.

## 5. A nearby actor-level clarification: printing is not filing

A closely related District of Columbia debate on 5 January 1923 supplies unusually explicit actor language about what this kind of proviso was understood to do. Representative Cramton repeatedly rejected the claim that discontinuing printing relieved an agency of its duty to make/file a report. He insisted that the proviso concerned printing only; the report still had to be made, with the original kept for inspection.

Primary source:
- *Congressional Record*, 67th Cong., 4th sess., 5 January 1923: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1923-pt2-v64/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1923-pt2-v64-5-2.pdf

Evidence: **high for contemporaneous interpretation of a closely parallel D.C. provision**.

Boundary: this is not direct legislative history for the 1925 federal section 6. It is evidence that contemporary legislators could explicitly distinguish **making/filing a report** from **printing it**. It should not be used to assign Cramton's formulation to McKeown, Kiess, or the 1925 Senate.

## 6. Historical actor problem

The safe actor-level reconstruction is now more complicated than the existing genealogy suggested.

By 1925 Congress had experience with annual printing waivers. A Senate bill offered a standing version without fiscal-year limitation. The House deliberately removed that section, while Congress continued the annual fiscal-year mechanism through appropriations.

What can safely be said:

```text
annual waiver already available
        ↓
standing form proposed
        ↓
standing form fails in House
        ↓
annual form remains acceptable
```

What cannot yet be said is **why** the House preferred or tolerated annual authorization over permanent delegation. The floor record recovered so far does not state the reason.

## 7. Historical actors / later reconstruction / researcher reconstruction

### Historical actors

- Congress repeatedly enacted fiscal-year-specific printing waivers.
- In 1925 the Senate accepted a general section 6 without a fiscal-year limit.
- The House affirmatively struck section 6.
- Congress still enacted the FY1926 annual waiver.
- Contemporary 1923 debate on a parallel D.C. proviso explicitly distinguished report-making/file duties from printing duties.

### Later reconstruction

The modern 44 U.S.C. §1117 revision note points to the 1953 permanent statute, not to the earlier annual appropriations or the failed 1925 standing provision. A genealogy reconstructed only from the modern Code therefore misses both the repeated temporary ancestry and the failed permanentization attempt.

### Researcher reconstruction

Useful analytical labels, not actor vocabulary:

- **failed permanentization**: a standing version of a repeatedly renewed temporary capability is proposed but rejected, while the temporary form survives.
- **temporal-form selection**: historical actors may accept the same substantive discretion in annual form while not enacting it as standing authority.
- **negative transmission evidence**: deletion of a visible clause is evidence about institutional selection, even when the motive for deletion remains unknown.

## 8. Why this changes the existing 1921–1953 interpretation

The existing packet currently supports a relatively smooth chain:

```text
annual fiscal exception
→ repeated administrative routine
→ demonstrated savings
→ 1953 permanentization
```

The 1925 evidence requires an interruption:

```text
annual fiscal exception
        ↓
repeated routine
        ↓
1925 early standing version proposed
        ↓
House deletion
        ↓
annual form continues for decades
        ↓
1953 permanentization
```

This matters methodologically because repeated renewal does not by itself imply a linear drift toward permanence. A capability can be routinized while its **legal temporal form remains contested or deliberately temporary**.

## 9. Evidence grades

- 1925 Senate section 6 standing text: **very high**.
- Absence of fiscal-year limitation in section 6: **very high / textual**.
- House amendment striking section 6: **very high**.
- Kiess acceptance and House adoption: **very high**.
- Specific House reason for striking section 6: **unknown**.
- FY1926 annual waiver after the failed standing version: **very high**.
- 1923 D.C. distinction between filing/making and printing: **high**, but parallel rather than direct 1925 legislative history.
- Claim that 1925 was an attempt to permanentize the annual waiver: **high**, based on close textual identity plus removal of fiscal-year limitation; exact drafting transmission from the annual appropriations language remains to document.

## 10. Hindsight risks

1. **1953 was the first time anyone imagined standing authority** — false. A standing version was before Congress in 1925.
2. **annual repetition naturally matured into permanence** — too linear. A permanent form was tried and removed decades before 1953.
3. **House deletion proves opposition to report-printing economy** — false. The House continued to accept annual fiscal-year waiver language.
4. **House deletion proves Congress wanted annual review** — plausible but currently unsupported. No recovered actor statement says this.
5. **McKeown's amendment reveals his motive** — false. The recovered record states the amendment but not its rationale.
6. **the 1923 D.C. debate directly explains the 1925 federal deletion** — false. It only supplies a nearby contemporaneous distinction between filing and printing.
7. **failure to enact = absence of a historical problem/solution** — false. Failed clauses are evidence of alternatives actors actually considered.

## 13. Compact finding

> The federal report-printing waiver did not move smoothly from annual appropriation proviso to permanent authority in 1953. In 1925 Congress considered a standing version of essentially the same mechanism: the Senate accepted a section authorizing executive departments to discontinue printing annual or special reports while preserving originals for public inspection, without a fiscal-year limitation. The House then affirmatively struck that section, while the FY1926 annual waiver continued in enacted appropriations. The genealogy therefore contains a failed permanentization attempt. This cautions against treating repeated temporary authority as a monotonic path toward permanence and shows why rejected clauses belong in Problem History alongside enacted institutions.
