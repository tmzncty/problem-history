# 1921–1980: a printing-economy standard survives its maker, then is reversed

## Why this episode matters

The existing printing-coordination research has established that the Permanent Conference on Printing (PCP) disappeared organizationally in 1933 while some of its standards and routines survived. A particularly strong object-level test is the Federal Government letterhead size. It can now be followed for roughly fifty-eight years: from the 1921 standardization campaign, through the death of the PCP, into an explicit 1969 reconsideration, and finally to a Joint Committee on Printing decision effective in 1980 that replaced the old government-specific size with the commercial 8.5 x 11 inch standard.

This is useful for Problem History because the same artifact changes status. In the early 1920s, reducing paper sizes and variants is presented as an economy. By the late 1960s and 1970s, the continued difference between the government and commercial standard has itself become an object of reconsideration. A solution can therefore survive the organization that made it, become infrastructure, and later become a new problem.

## Evidence chain

### 1. 1921–1924: the smaller government letterhead is an economy solution

The FY1923 Annual Report of the Public Printer reports that, through cooperation with government boards under the Bureau of the Budget, the size of letterheads and forms such as proposals, contracts, and leases had been fixed at **8 x 10.5 inches**. The Public Printer estimated that this single size saved the Government at least **$50,000 per year**. The same passage places the change among a larger simplification program: publication sizes had been reduced from more than fifty sizes to eight, and envelope varieties were also sharply reduced.

Source: *Annual Report of the Public Printer*, FY1923, Serial Set / GovInfo: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-08256_00_00-002-0015-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-08256_00_00-002-0015-0000.pdf

The later Public Printer report summarizing the PCP's first three years says that it had standardized letterheads as to paper stock and size and restricted embossed stationery, with an estimated saving of $15,000 annually. It also describes the PCP as a coordinating agency of the Bureau of the Budget, organized August 4, 1921.

Source: *Annual Report of the Public Printer* (report summarizing PCP work through FY1924), GovInfo: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP-127288cf668d1d78bc1ac9c67fd93cdc/pdf/GOVPUB-GP-127288cf668d1d78bc1ac9c67fd93cdc.pdf

**Evidence strength:** high for the actor-level claim that letterhead-size standardization was understood as an economy measure; medium for exact attribution of the initial size decision because the surviving annual-report summaries compress the work of several cooperating bodies.

### 2. 1929: the standard is still explicitly justified by savings

The FY1929 Public Printer report states that adopting one standard size, **8 x 10.5 inches**, instead of the commercial **8.5 x 11 inches**, produced an estimated annual paper saving of approximately **$50,000**. It also notes that envelope stocks had been reduced from nearly a dozen sizes to four.

Source: *Annual Report of the Public Printer*, FY1929, GovInfo: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP-ca5dcbe943b84fc6414d74f7c0218c2a/pdf/GOVPUB-GP-ca5dcbe943b84fc6414d74f7c0218c2a.pdf

This is important because it shows that the difference from the commercial standard was not merely accidental by 1929: the government-specific size was explicitly described as an economy relative to 8.5 x 11.

**Evidence strength:** very high.

### 3. 1933: organization death does not kill the standard

The PCP was abolished in the Federal Coordinating Service reorganization in 1933. Existing repository work documents that event separately. The letterhead standard nevertheless survived. The 1961 GPO centennial history retrospectively lists standardized letterhead stock and size among PCP procedures and says the savings from PCP recommendations continued into the anniversary year.

Source: *100 GPO Years, 1861–1961*, GovInfo: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP3-4938ab5a455054f8d34d9c153eba2bf1/pdf/GOVPUB-GP3-4938ab5a455054f8d34d9c153eba2bf1.pdf

**Evidence strength:** high for later institutional reconstruction of procedural survival. Do not use the 1961 statement as evidence for why the PCP was abolished in 1933.

### 4. 1969: the inherited standard itself becomes an explicit object of review

A June 30, 1969 interagency meeting on standardizing paper sizes, sponsored by the National Archives and Records Service / GSA and the National Bureau of Standards, explicitly traced the Government's 8 x 10.5 inch letterhead standard back to 1921 and contrasted it with the 8.5 x 11 inch industry standard established in the same general period. The meeting was convened specifically to reconsider paper-size standardization.

Declassified copy: CIA FOIA Reading Room, *Report of Informational Discussion Meeting: Standardizing Paper Sizes*, June 30, 1969: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP74-00390R000300360002-4.pdf

This is a crucial Problem History transition. The 1920s actor-level problem was multiplicity/waste and the 8 x 10.5 standard was a solution. By 1969, the existence of a separate Government standard alongside a commercial standard had itself become an object requiring study.

**Evidence strength:** very high that reconsideration occurred; medium for any stronger causal claim about why the old standard had become problematic until the full meeting discussion is coded speaker by speaker.

### 5. 1978–1980: the government-specific standard is deliberately reversed

On December 18, 1978, Joint Committee on Printing chairman Claiborne Pell notified heads of departments and agencies that the JCP had **unanimously adopted 8.5 x 11 inches as the new standard paper size, effective January 1, 1980**. The notice explicitly says that the new size conforms to the private sector. It applies to stationery and related correspondence materials, including letterheads, copy sets, second sheets, carbon paper, and electrostatically copied reports. The delayed effective date was intended to let agencies exhaust their stocks of 8 x 10.5 inch stationery.

Primary source: Joint Committee on Printing, Dec. 18, 1978, reproduced in *Government Printing and Binding Regulations / related JCP compilation*, GovInfo: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CPUB-101spub9/pdf/GPO-CPUB-101spub9.pdf

**Evidence strength:** very high for the decision, scope, effective date, private-sector conformity rationale stated in the notice, and stock-transition provision.

## Problem transformation

A defensible transformation chain is now:

```text
1921–1920s
many sizes / paper and production waste
        ↓
standardize Government correspondence at 8 x 10.5
        ↓
measured/claimed paper savings
        ↓
1933 PCP organizational death
        ↓
standard survives as administrative infrastructure
        ↓
1969
separate Government and industry standards become an object of review
        ↓
1978 decision / 1980 effective date
Government abandons its special size
and conforms to 8.5 x 11 private-sector standard
```

The key transformation is not simply `standard A -> standard B`. It is:

```text
solution to heterogeneity
        ↓
long-lived infrastructure
        ↓
legacy divergence
        ↓
object of renewed standardization
        ↓
replacement by a wider compatibility standard
```

A useful provisional research label is **solution-to-legacy-divergence transition**. This is present-day analytical vocabulary, not an actor term.

## Actor / reconstruction / present-analysis separation

### Historical actors

High-confidence actor claims:

- Public Printer reports in the 1920s described reduction of paper/form/publication sizes as economy and standardization work.
- The Public Printer explicitly compared 8 x 10.5 Government letterheads with commercial 8.5 x 11 letterheads and claimed paper savings from the smaller standard.
- In 1969 Federal officials explicitly reopened the paper-size standardization question and knew the Government and commercial standards had diverged since the 1920s.
- In December 1978 the JCP unanimously chose 8.5 x 11, explicitly noting conformity with the private sector, with effect on January 1, 1980.

### Later institutional reconstruction

The 1961 GPO centennial history attributes standardized letterhead stock and size to the PCP and describes its savings as continuing after organizational abolition. This is valuable evidence for institutional memory and procedural survival, but it is retrospective.

### Present-day analysis

The following are project-level analytical constructs and must not be attributed to historical actors without direct evidence:

- `procedural sediment survival`
- `solution-to-legacy-divergence transition`
- `compatibility boundary expansion`
- the claim that the 1921 solution *became* a problem in a singular causal sense

The safer formulation is that the inherited divergence became an explicit object of review and was eventually replaced by a standard chosen in part for conformity with the private sector.

## What this adds to the repository

This is the first clean object-level chain in the printing-coordination material that crosses more than fifty years and includes both survival after organizational death and later reversal.

It supports a stronger endpoint model for Problem Episodes. For a standardized artifact, record separately:

- originating problem formulation;
- adopting organization;
- standard/rule artifact;
- organization end date;
- artifact survival after organization death;
- later re-problematization;
- replacement/reversal date;
- stated rationale for replacement;
- disposal/migration arrangements for the old artifact.

This avoids treating `organization abolished` as the endpoint of the solution and avoids treating `standard persisted` as evidence that the original problem formulation remained unchanged.

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not say the PCP 'caused' the 1980 reversal.** The causal chain is not continuous in that sense.
2. **Do not infer that the 1921 standard was irrational because it was later replaced.** Contemporary Public Printer reports explicitly represented it as producing savings.
3. **Do not infer that 1969 officials had already decided on 8.5 x 11.** The meeting establishes reconsideration, not the final decision.
4. **Do not equate persistence of a standard with persistence of the original problem.** The artifact can survive after the problem formulation changes or disappears.
5. **Treat claimed savings as actor evidence.** The $50,000 and later PCP savings figures are institutional estimates unless independently audited evidence is recovered.
6. **Do not collapse 'private-sector conformity' into a complete causal explanation.** It is explicit in the 1978 JCP notice, but other cost, copying, filing, equipment, or records-management considerations require separate primary evidence.

## Uncertainties / source debt

- Recover the original March/September 1921 departmental and PCP records fixing 8 x 10.5, rather than relying on 1923–24 summaries.
- Code the full June 30, 1969 meeting transcript speaker by speaker: which actors framed the problem as equipment compatibility, filing, copying, cost, aesthetics, records management, or something else?
- Recover the studies and deliberations between 1969 and the JCP's December 1978 decision, especially GSA/NBS analyses and JCP materials.
- Determine whether 8 x 10.5 was continuously mandatory government-wide or whether important jurisdictions had already defected before 1978.
- Establish whether the 1978 JCP decision explicitly quantified projected savings/costs beyond the conformity statement.

## Next candidates

1. **1969 -> 1978 decision chain:** recover NBS/GSA paper-size studies and JCP hearing/committee material to explain why a standard once defended as economical was finally abandoned.
2. **Technology interaction:** test whether photocopiers, typewriters, filing equipment, and automated reproduction changed the cost function of maintaining a special Government size.
3. **Defection map:** identify agencies/Congress/courts that moved to 8.5 x 11 before the formal 1980 government-wide change.
4. **Comparative standardization:** compare this reversal with PCP standards that survived unchanged; ask what distinguishes standards that become infrastructural lock-in from those later displaced by compatibility pressure.
