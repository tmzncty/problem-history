# 1928–1941–1945: direct precedent and predecessor memory in federal report pruning

## Research question

The current branch inserted a 1940–1941 Bureau-of-the-Budget survey / H.R. 5508 episode between the 1928 mass repeal and the 1945 H.R. 2504 round, but deliberately left two transmission questions unresolved:

1. did the later actors explicitly invoke the 1928 statute as precedent, rather than merely reproduce a similar solution form?;
2. did the 1945 actors remember an intervening predecessor bill, rather than independently rediscovering the problem?

Fresh primary evidence in the legislative history of H.R. 2504 / Public Law 79-615 resolves the first question directly and substantially strengthens the second.

## Core finding

House Report 79-311 (March 13, 1945) explicitly states that H.R. 2504 was **"of the same character" as the Act of May 29, 1928 (45 Stat. 986)**, identifying the earlier statute and its repeal of 128 statutory reporting provisions. The same report also relies on what had been shown during House consideration of **"a predecessor bill of this character"** when arguing that little or no use was made of many unprinted reports.

Thus the 1945 episode contains explicit historical memory in two directions:

```text
1928 enacted mass repeal
        ↓ explicitly named by 1945 committee as same-character precedent
intervening predecessor bill / House consideration
        ↓ explicitly remembered, identity not named in H. Rept. 79-311
1945 H.R. 2504
        ↓
P.L. 79-615
```

The 1941 H.R. 5508 episode is the strongest currently recovered candidate for the unnamed predecessor, because it had the same title/problem object, came from the same House committee under John J. Cochran, passed the House, and was sent to the Senate. But **the 1945 report does not name H.R. 5508**, so this identification remains a high-probability hypothesis rather than a closed transmission edge.

## 1. 1945 actors explicitly invoke the 1928 statute

H. Rept. 79-311 explains the general problem before its item-by-item discussion. It says Congress naturally imposes periodic reports when establishing or expanding governmental functions in order to remain informed, but that after years of operation those reports may become relatively low-value.

The report then states that the H.R. 2504 proposal is of the same character as the Act of May 29, 1928, 45 Stat. 986, which repealed 128 provisions requiring reports to Congress.

Primary source:

- H. Rept. 79-311, *Discontinuing Certain Reports Now Required by Law*, Mar. 13, 1945, reproduced in the legislative history of P.L. 79-615, pp. 1–2: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/86/Legislative_History%2C_Public_Law_615_-_79th_Congress%2C_Chapter_770_-_2d_Session%2C_H.R._2504_%28IA_PL79615%29.pdf

**Evidence strength: very high.** This is direct committee-level precedent language, not a present-day similarity judgment.

This closes an important source-control gap. The repository no longer needs to describe the 1928 → 1945 relation merely as a reconstructed resemblance. At least in 1945, the committee itself explicitly located its proposal in the same statutory problem/solution family as 1928.

## 2. The 1945 committee also remembers an intervening predecessor bill

The same report gives a second transmission clue. In discussing the fact that many reports were transmitted in manuscript to committees rather than printed, it says that House consideration of **a predecessor bill of this character** had indicated that little if any use was being made of these unprinted reports. The committee uses that earlier House experience as evidence that discontinuance would not adversely affect congressional business.

This is important because it means the 1945 proposal was not narrated solely through a 17-year jump back to 1928. The committee also remembered a more recent legislative attempt.

Primary source:

- H. Rept. 79-311, same source, general explanation preceding the item-by-item statement.

**Evidence strength: very high** for the existence of an actor-remembered predecessor bill; **not yet sufficient** to identify that bill by number.

## 3. Why H.R. 5508 (1941) is the strongest candidate — but not yet proved identical

The branch already recovered H. Rept. 77-1175 and the 1941 House debate on H.R. 5508. That episode has several properties matching the 1945 report's unnamed predecessor:

- same general legislative object: discontinuing reports required by law;
- same House Committee on Expenditures in the Executive Departments;
- John J. Cochran is the relevant House actor in both episodes;
- 1941 H.R. 5508 passed the House and was transmitted to the Senate;
- the 1941 floor discussion included claims that reports consumed personnel/resources despite little observable use.

Official metadata for the 1941 report:

- H. Rept. 77-1175, *Discontinuance of certain reports now required by law*, Aug. 13, 1941: https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/SERIALSET-10557_00_00-052-1175-0000

The chronological and institutional fit is strong enough to make H.R. 5508 the leading candidate for H. Rept. 79-311's "predecessor bill." But a historian should not silently substitute the bill number into the 1945 quotation until either:

- H. Rept. 77-1175 / H.R. 5508 is item-diffed against H.R. 2504 and shows substantial candidate continuity; or
- another 1945/committee source explicitly identifies H.R. 5508 as the predecessor.

**Evidence strength: medium-high as an identification hypothesis; not closed.**

## 4. 1945 preserves the 1928 information-route logic, not merely the repeal form

H. Rept. 79-311 does more than cite the 1928 Act. It also states that information Congress desires can still be supplied on request by Congress or the respective House/Senate committees, in the form desired for the particular purpose.

The committee therefore distinguishes:

```text
fixed periodic report requirement
        !=
Congress's ability to obtain underlying information
```

This is substantively close to the repository's existing 1928 finding that periodic reporting could be pruned while a demand route for information remained available.

The 1945 report also gives concrete reasons for obsolescence: other reporting procedures under the Budget and Accounting Act of 1921; transfer or discontinuance of the underlying activity; duplication with other fiscal/annual reports; production cost; and low observed use.

Primary source:

- H. Rept. 79-311, same legislative-history compilation.

**Evidence strength: very high** for the 1945 actor formulation. The phrase `information-route logic` is present analytical shorthand, not actor vocabulary.

## 5. 1945 item-level review shows that "obsolete report" was not one homogeneous category

The committee's item-by-item explanation distinguishes multiple failure modes. Examples include:

- an 1898 quarterly condition-of-public-business report judged unnecessary because newer governmental/fiscal procedures and direct presidential information requests had displaced it;
- a forest-road/trail report whose preparation consumed substantial staff time and money while related information remained available through other agency reports;
- an Agricultural Adjustment Agency payment report that Agriculture said cost roughly $25,000 annually to prepare and did not benefit its operations;
- Commerce information already available through fiscal reports;
- reports tied to activities transferred, discontinued, or superseded by wartime administrative arrangements.

This matters methodologically because package-level `obsolete` should not be encoded as one causal reason. The same repeal package can contain:

```text
functional substitution
administrative reorganization
activity disappearance
information duplication
cost / burden
low use
alternate on-demand information access
```

**Evidence strength: high** for the categories visible in H. Rept. 79-311. These categories are researcher normalization of item-specific committee explanations.

## Historical actors vs later reconstruction vs present analysis

### Historical actors

Directly supportable in 1945:

- Congress often imposed reports to remain informed when programs/agencies were new or expanded;
- some reports became relatively low-value over time;
- H.R. 2504 was explicitly characterized as the same kind of proposal as the 1928 mass repeal;
- the committee remembered House consideration of a predecessor bill;
- alternative reporting procedures, transferred/discontinued activities, cost, and low use justified particular eliminations;
- Congress could still request information when needed.

### Later reconstruction

The branch's longer 1928 → 1941 → 1945 → 1954 → 1960 → 1965 → 1995 chain is a researcher reconstruction. The new evidence strengthens two edges but does not prove one continuously operating review bureaucracy.

### Present analytical shorthand

Useful labels only:

- `explicit precedent invocation`;
- `periodic artifact pruning + demand-route preservation`;
- `predecessor-memory edge`;
- `heterogeneous obsolescence reasons`.

Do not attribute these phrases to historical actors.

## Problem-History payoff

The chronology can now be represented more strongly:

```text
1928
mass repeal of statutory reports
+ retained ability to obtain information by request
        ↓
        ↓ 1945 committee explicitly says its proposal is "of the same character"
        ↓
1939–1941
Bureau survey -> H.R. 5508 / H. Rept. 77-1175
House passage -> Senate receipt
        ↓ probable but not yet bill-number-proved predecessor relation
1945 H.R. 2504 / H. Rept. 79-311
explicit 1928 precedent
+ explicit memory of a predecessor bill
+ demand-route preservation
+ item-specific obsolescence tests
        ↓
P.L. 79-615
```

This changes the evidentiary status of the long genealogy. The 1928 → 1945 edge is now **actor-explicit transmission**, not merely similarity inferred by the present researcher.

The 1941 → 1945 edge advances from `chronological resemblance` to `1945 actor explicitly remembers a predecessor bill + H.R. 5508 is the strongest matching recovered candidate`, but remains one step short of proof.

## Hindsight risks

1. **Do not say H. Rept. 79-311 explicitly names H.R. 5508.** It says only "a predecessor bill of this character."
2. **Do not collapse 1928 and 1945 into identical statutes.** The committee invokes 1928 as same-character precedent; the candidate lists, administrative environment, and item-specific rationales differ.
3. **Do not infer uninterrupted institutional operation from precedent citation.** Historical memory/borrowing can survive discontinuous administrative processes.
4. **Do not treat all 64 H.R. 2504 items as obsolete for the same reason.** The report's item-by-item explanations are heterogeneous.
5. **Do not equate repeal of a periodic report with elimination of the underlying information.** The 1945 committee expressly preserves on-request information access.
6. **Do not use the later enactment date incorrectly.** H.R. 2504's legislative history shows House committee reporting in March 1945, House passage in June 1945, Senate action later, and approval as P.L. 79-615 on Aug. 7, 1946; the proposal spans the 79th Congress rather than being a single-day 1945 event.
