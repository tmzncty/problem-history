# 1964–1968: periodic grant review, sunset enforcement, and conference removal

## Research question

The branch already establishes that the 1968 Intergovernmental Cooperation Act retained periodic congressional review of grant-in-aid programs while dropping an earlier five-year automatic-termination device. This packet narrows the genealogy: what did the earlier design actually try to solve, how did actors describe the enforcement problem, and at what stage was automatic termination removed?

## New findings

### 1. The periodic-review proposal was already a distinct Senate measure before the 1965 omnibus bill

In the October 4, 1968 Senate debate on the S. 698 conference report, Senator Lee Metcalf described the enacted bill as the culmination of eight years of legislative activity and identified S. 2114, a bill for periodic congressional review of Federal grants-in-aid, as having passed the Senate in 1964. He then identified S. 561, the Intergovernmental Cooperation Act of 1965, as another unanimously Senate-passed predecessor.

This gives an actor-explicit sequence rather than a researcher-inferred similarity:

```text
1961 ACIR recommendation
→ implementing proposals
→ S. 2114 periodic-review bill passes Senate (1964)
→ periodic-review machinery incorporated into S. 561 omnibus bill (1965)
→ later S. 698 (1967–1968)
→ conference substitute / enactment (1968)
```

Primary source: Congressional Record, Oct. 4, 1968, conference-report debate on S. 698, statement of Sen. Lee Metcalf, GovInfo.

Evidence strength: high for the 1964/1965 predecessor sequence because a contemporary participant explicitly reconstructed it during final enactment.

### 2. The 1965 committee explicitly rejected extending automatic termination to existing grants

The 1965 committee report on S. 561 records that the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, National Association of Real Estate Boards, and National Association of Manufacturers wanted both congressional review and automatic expiration extended to existing as well as future grant programs.

The committee rejected that proposal. The subcommittee chairman warned that applying automatic termination to all existing grants without examining the impact program by program could be "reckless" and argued that the device should be restricted to new programs.

This is unusually strong actor evidence because it reveals that automatic termination was not treated as a neutral procedural consequence of review. Historical actors themselves identified a risk in making termination operate across a heterogeneous inherited program stock.

Primary source: *Intergovernmental Cooperation Act of 1965*, Senate committee report on S. 561, Serial Set / GovInfo, discussion of title III and proposed extension of automatic expiration to existing programs.

Evidence strength: very high for the committee's disposition and stated risk boundary.

### 3. The enforcement problem was explicit: review might otherwise lack "teeth"

Florence P. Dwyer's Oct. 2, 1968 letter, printed in the Oct. 3 Congressional Record, states that the principal purpose of automatic termination was to put "teeth" into the requirement that congressional committees review grants and decide whether changing needs warranted extension, adjustment, or elimination.

This supports an actor-level second-order problem formulation:

```text
P1: grant programs require periodic reassessment as needs and circumstances change
S1: require periodic committee review
P2: review obligation may lack practical force
S2: attach automatic termination if Congress does not affirmatively extend a qualifying new program
```

`P1/P2/S1/S2` are present analytical labels. The concern that the review requirement needed "teeth" is actor-explicit.

Primary source: Congressional Record, Oct. 3, 1968, letter of Rep. Florence P. Dwyer.

Evidence strength: very high for Dwyer's explanation; medium-high for treating it as the sole or universal rationale of every sponsor.

### 4. The final removal point is now clear: House had no comparable title; conference deleted the five-year automatic termination rule

Metcalf's Oct. 4 explanation identifies the decisive late-stage text genealogy. Title V of the Senate bill contained congressional review of Federal grants; the House amendment contained no comparable provision. The conference report then inserted a substitute Title VI.

Crucially, Metcalf states that the conference substitute eliminated the provision automatically terminating after five years grant programs enacted in the 91st and subsequent Congresses. The conferees instead agreed on the objective that reasonable termination dates should be placed on new and existing programs.

Thus the final transformation was not merely `automatic termination quietly omitted`:

```text
Senate bill: periodic review + statutory automatic-termination backstop for future programs
House amendment: no comparable periodic-review title
conference: periodic review restored in substitute title
            + automatic termination deleted
            + policy preference for reasonable program-specific termination dates
```

Primary source: Congressional Record, Oct. 4, 1968, statement of Sen. Metcalf on the conference report.

Evidence strength: very high.

### 5. Changed ordinary legislative practice was itself an actor-stated reason for weakening the generic backstop

Both Dwyer's Oct. 2 letter and Metcalf's conference explanation point to the same environmental change: by the late 1960s, few / a great majority of new grant programs were being enacted without specified termination dates. Dwyer says this change in the pattern of legislation reduced the significance of the automatic-termination feature; Metcalf says the conference recognized that few recent programs lacked termination dates.

This supplies direct actor evidence for a mechanism important to Problem History:

```text
solution component designed to force reconsideration
→ ordinary legislative practice changes in the same direction
→ generic enforcement component becomes less necessary / less significant
→ conference removes generic component while retaining review
```

Do not upgrade `less significant` into `obsolete` unless a source uses that stronger claim.

## Historical actor problem vs later reconstruction

### Actor-explicit

- periodic review was meant to reassess grants against changing needs and circumstances;
- Dwyer said automatic termination supplied "teeth" to review;
- the 1965 committee feared indiscriminate application to all existing programs could be reckless;
- the 1968 conference removed automatic termination;
- late-1960s practice of adding specific termination dates reduced the importance of the generic device.

### Later / present analytical reconstruction

Researcher shorthand only:

- `second-order enforcement problem`;
- `generic sunset backstop`;
- `program-stock heterogeneity`;
- `environmental substitution`;
- `solution component becomes less necessary because surrounding practice changes`.

These labels should not be attributed to ACIR, Dwyer, Metcalf, or the committees.

## Problem-History payoff

This episode demonstrates that failed solution components can carry unusually strong evidence about how actors understood the weakness of a surviving solution. The enacted 1968 statute shows periodic review. The deleted automatic-termination mechanism reveals that sponsors had previously worried review alone might not compel consequential reconsideration.

It also gives a clean example of a solution being revised for two different reasons that should not be collapsed:

1. **risk objection**: applying automatic termination too broadly to inherited programs could cause damage without program-specific inquiry;
2. **environmental change**: new grants increasingly acquired their own termination dates, reducing the need for a universal statutory backstop.

These are distinct historical mechanisms.

## Hindsight risks

Do not call the 1965/1968 mechanism a modern "sunset law" without qualification; use the actors' `automatic termination` / `termination date` vocabulary when describing their own formulation. Do not infer that opposition to automatic termination was opposition to periodic review itself. Do not infer that program-specific expiration dates and the generic five-year rule were legally identical merely because actors treated the former as reducing the latter's significance.

## Remaining uncertainty / next candidates

The highest-value remaining source task is a literal bill-text diff across S. 2114 (1964), S. 561 (1965), S. 698 as introduced/reported/passed by the Senate, the House amendment, and the conference text. That would establish when scope shifted from earlier formulations to the final Senate rule covering programs enacted in the 91st and subsequent Congresses.

A second target is the House Government Operations material documenting opposition from the opponents themselves. Dwyer's 1968 retrospective is strong evidence that opposition centered on automatic termination, but an opponent's contemporaneous statement would prevent the episode from being reconstructed only through a sponsor/conferee's account.
