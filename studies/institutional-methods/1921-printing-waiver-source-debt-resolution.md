# 1921: resolving the first report-printing-waiver source debt

Status: **M2 source-control addendum / correction to the open 1921–1953 printing-waiver packet**

## Finding

The open `1921-1953-report-printing-waiver-genealogy.md` correctly identifies the earliest directly recovered provision as the Second Deficiency Act, fiscal year 1921, approved 16 June 1921, but leaves an apparent source debt because the 1933 Public Printer later said the authorization was first included in an appropriation act “for the fiscal year 1921,” while the operative clause in the 1921 statute expressly controls **FY1922** printing expenditures.

That apparent gap can now be resolved without inventing an earlier statute.

The direct Statutes at Large text is 42 Stat. 64. It says, in the Government Printing Office paragraph, that to keep printing and binding expenditures within the **appropriations for fiscal year 1922**, department heads may discontinue printing annual or special reports, provided the original remains on file for public inspection. The same Act then states in §5 that the Act may be referred to as the **“Second Deficiency Act, fiscal year 1921.”**

Primary source:
- Second Deficiency Act, fiscal year 1921, 42 Stat. 64 (approved 16 June 1921): https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-42/pdf/STATUTE-42-Pg29.pdf

Evidence: **very high / enacted statute**.

The 1921 Annual Report of the Public Printer independently confirms administrative use of this exact Act. It says that, “in accordance with the provisions of the second deficiency act, fiscal year 1921, approved June 16, 1921,” the Public Printer decided to discontinue printing additional reports for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1921, while retaining originals for public inspection.

Primary source:
- *Annual Report of the Public Printer* for FY1921, congressional serial-set scan: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-07963_00_00-002-0097-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-07963_00_00-002-0097-0000.pdf

Evidence: **high / contemporaneous administrative implementation**.

## What the 1933 retrospective most likely means

The 1933 Public Printer says the authorization “was first included in an appropriation act for the fiscal year 1921 and has since been repeated annually.” Read against the enacted 1921 statute, “for the fiscal year 1921” should not be treated as proof of a separate, earlier FY1921 appropriation provision. The phrase can identify the **Act by its statutory title**—Second Deficiency Act, fiscal year 1921—even though the printing-waiver paragraph itself was drafted against FY1922 printing appropriations.

This interpretation is strengthened by the FY1921 Public Printer report, which cites that exact June 16 Act by that title and immediately invokes its authority.

Therefore the earlier packet's statement that an “exact FY1921 enactment remains source debt” should be retired. There is currently **no affirmative evidence of a distinct earlier enactment**. Searching for one merely to reconcile the 1933 shorthand would risk manufacturing a false missing link.

## Actor-level problem

The actor-level problem remains narrow:

```text
printing/binding appropriation ceiling
        ↓
permission to suppress printed editions
        ↓
original retained for public inspection
```

The FY1921 Public Printer adds an important implementation detail: the authority could be used immediately by the Government Printing Office itself. This is stronger than a purely enabling-law genealogy.

## Historical actors / later reconstruction / researcher reconstruction

### Historical actors

- Congress enacted the waiver on 16 June 1921 in an Act formally styled the Second Deficiency Act, fiscal year 1921.
- The operative clause referred to FY1922 printing appropriations.
- The Public Printer nevertheless invoked that Act in the FY1921 annual report to discontinue printing additional reports for the year ended 30 June 1921 while retaining originals.
- In 1933 the Public Printer retrospectively described the authorization as first appearing in an appropriation act “for the fiscal year 1921.”

### Later reconstruction

The 1953 permanentization and 1968 positive-law codification expose the later statutory ancestry but do not resolve this early naming ambiguity by themselves.

### Researcher reconstruction

The safe reconstruction is **title/operative-year disambiguation**, not discovery of a hypothetical predecessor statute. “Fiscal year” in a retrospective institutional sentence can refer to the title of a deficiency act rather than the fiscal year governed by every clause inside it.

## Evidence grades

- 42 Stat. 64 text and §5 short title: **very high**.
- FY1921 Public Printer citation and implementation: **high**.
- 1933 statement of first appearance and annual repetition: **high**, but its phrase “for the fiscal year 1921” must be read with the direct 1921 statute rather than isolated from it.
- Claim that a separate pre-16-June-1921 printing-waiver enactment existed: **unsupported / do not assert**.

## Hindsight / source-control risks

1. Do not infer a missing statute merely because a later retrospective uses a fiscal-year label differently from the operative appropriation year inside the Act.
2. Do not rewrite the 1933 Public Printer as “wrong”; the phrase is compatible with the formal title of the June 16 Act.
3. Do not collapse enactment and implementation. The statute is direct evidence of authority; the FY1921 Public Printer report is direct evidence that the authority was actually invoked.
4. Do not infer that discontinuing printed editions abolished the underlying information. The Public Printer expressly retained originals for inspection.

## Compact finding

> The apparent “missing FY1921 statute” is best treated as a naming ambiguity, not a missing link. The June 16, 1921 Second Deficiency Act, fiscal year 1921, contains the first directly recovered waiver; its operative printing clause refers to FY1922 appropriations, while §5 gives the Act its FY1921 title. The FY1921 Public Printer explicitly invoked that same Act to stop printing additional reports while retaining originals. The 1933 retrospective can therefore be reconciled with the enacted text, and the prior source debt should be closed rather than filled with a hypothetical predecessor.
