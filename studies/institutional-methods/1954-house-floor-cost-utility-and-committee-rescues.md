# 1954 House floor rationale: cost/utility test and committee-level rescues

## Research question

The current branch identifies H. Rept. 83-1193 as the committee report accompanying H.R. 6290, but the report text itself has remained a source debt. A contemporaneous Congressional Record passage from April 26, 1954 supplies two important pieces of primary evidence even before the full report is independently recovered: (1) an actor-explicit House-floor formulation of the problem, and (2) the exact committee amendments showing that several Interior reporting requirements were removed from the repeal package before House passage.

This packet does not substitute the floor debate for H. Rept. 83-1193. It narrows what can already be stated securely and identifies the remaining report-level source debt.

## 1. H. Rept. 83-1193 was filed on February 8, 1954, with an amendment

The Congressional Record for February 8, 1954 records George H. Hoffman of Michigan, for the Committee on Government Operations, reporting H.R. 6290, “to discontinue certain reports now required by law,” **with amendment**, as H. Rept. 83-1193.

Primary source:

- Congressional Record, 83d Cong., 2d sess., Feb. 8, 1954, p. 1506: https://www.congress.gov/83/crecb/1954/02/08/GPO-CRECB-1954-pt2-1-2.pdf

**Evidence strength: very high.** This fixes the report date and establishes that the committee-reported bill already differed from the referred text.

### Correction / repository implication

Treat H. Rept. 83-1193 as a February 8, 1954 committee report, not as a late-spring product. The April 26 House floor text below then shows the committee amendments as they were presented for adoption.

## 2. Historical actors framed the problem as experience-tested uselessness plus unfavorable cost/benefit

On April 26, 1954, H.R. 6290 came up on the House Consent Calendar. Representative Overton Brooks of Louisiana asked what reports were being eliminated. Hoffman explained that the purpose was to remove statutory reporting provisions that “experience has demonstrated to be unnecessary.” He then stated that the question was whether the previously enacted reporting requirements had been helpful and whether they produced savings, and said the committee found that the cost of preparing the reports was greater than the savings made. Brooks summarized the target as reports “of no use to the Congress”; Hoffman replied that this was “about the substance of it.”

Primary source:

- Congressional Record, 83d Cong., 2d sess., Apr. 26, 1954, pp. 5511–5512: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1954-pt4/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1954-pt4-17-2.pdf

**Evidence strength: very high** for Hoffman's and Brooks's own formulations on the House floor.

### Actor language vs present analysis

Actor-explicit:

- experience had demonstrated some reports unnecessary;
- usefulness/helpfulness to Congress was a relevant test;
- preparation cost could exceed the savings attributed to the reports.

Present analytical shorthand only:

- `cost/utility test`;
- `report-pruning decision rule`;
- `committee rescue`.

Do not attribute those labels to Hoffman, Brooks, or the committee.

## 3. The House floor text exposes a committee filter inside the 1954 repeal package

The bill as read on April 26 listed 38 reporting requirements. The printed committee amendments then struck three Interior items before House passage:

- original item 26: the Secretary of the Interior's annual report listing lands exchanged under the Recreation Act of June 14, 1926;
- original item 28: the Secretary of the Interior's report of presidential withdrawals of public lands from settlement, location, sale, or entry;
- original item 29: the Secretary of the Interior's report concerning lands within Indian reservations reserved for use in connection with irrigation projects.

The amendments also removed the separate Mutual Security Agency heading and struck original item 35, the quarterly presidential report on expenditures and activities under assistance to Greece and Turkey. The surviving items were renumbered. The House agreed to the committee amendments and passed the bill.

Primary source:

- Congressional Record, Apr. 26, 1954, pp. 5511–5512, especially the bill text and committee amendments.

**Evidence strength: very high** for the fact and location of these removals.

### Important boundary

The floor text proves that these requirements were removed from the repeal package by committee amendment. It does **not by itself reveal why each item was rescued**. The item-specific reasons must still be recovered from H. Rept. 83-1193, committee papers, agency correspondence, or other contemporaneous records.

Therefore:

```text
candidate for repeal
→ removed from repeal package in committee
```

is established, but:

```text
candidate for repeal
→ committee judged it substantively necessary for reason X
```

is not yet established for these items.

## 4. The Indian fiscal report was not among the committee rescues

Original item 27 was the annual Interior report to the Speaker on the fiscal affairs of Indian tribes, the requirement created in 1911 and later codified at 25 U.S.C. §143. The committee amendments struck neighboring original items 26, 28, and 29, but **did not strike item 27**. After renumbering it therefore remained in the House-passed repeal package.

This sharpens the already documented 1911→1945→1954 history:

```text
1911 Indian fiscal reporting requirement
→ 1945 proposed repeal
→ 1945 congressional rescue
→ survives
→ 1954 proposed repeal again
→ unlike several neighboring Interior reports, not rescued by the 1954 committee amendment
→ House passes repeal provision
→ statutory requirement ultimately repealed in Pub. L. 83-706
```

Primary / official sources:

- Congressional Record, Apr. 26, 1954, pp. 5511–5512, for item 27 and the committee amendments.
- Pub. L. 83-706, Aug. 30, 1954, 68 Stat. 966, for enactment.

**Evidence strength: very high** for the procedural contrast.

### What this newly permits us to ask

The comparison is now narrower than “what changed between 1945 and 1954?” The 1954 committee itself was willing to preserve several Interior reporting requirements while allowing the Indian fiscal report to remain in the repeal package. The next source question is therefore:

> What item-specific distinction did the committee or Interior draw between the Indian fiscal report and the neighboring Interior reports it removed from H.R. 6290?

That is a much stronger archival target than a generic search for 1954 report-elimination rhetoric.

## 5. This also strengthens the recurring-review model without making it automatic

The current branch already documents later report-pruning cycles in which agency recommendations were filtered through jurisdictional committees. The April 1954 floor text demonstrates that the same broad process feature was visible in H.R. 6290: the repeal list did not simply pass through unchanged; multiple requirements were removed by committee amendment before House passage.

A defensible process representation is therefore:

```text
candidate list
→ committee review/amendment
→ some candidate repeals removed
→ surviving repeal package
→ House passage
```

This is stronger than modeling report elimination as a binary executive recommendation → repeal pipeline.

But do not infer uninterrupted organizational continuity with the 1960 or 1965 review cycles merely from process similarity. The historical transmission still requires separate evidence.

## 6. Problem-History payoff: compare rejected and surviving solution components

This episode reinforces a general method rule: when reconstructing a pruning/repeal episode, preserve not only the enacted deletions but also **proposed deletions that were themselves deleted**.

Useful schema fields:

```text
candidate_action
committee_disposition
floor_disposition
final_disposition
actor_stated_reason_at_each_stage
reason_source_strength
```

The three Interior rescues and the Indian fiscal non-rescue provide an unusually clean within-package comparison. Because all four sit in the same department section and passed through the same committee process, differences in disposition can potentially expose the historical criteria more clearly than comparing unrelated statutes across decades.

These schema labels are present-day analytical devices, not historical vocabulary.

## Hindsight risks

1. Do not equate Hoffman's package-level cost/usefulness explanation with an item-specific reason for every repeal.
2. Do not say the committee found each surviving repeal individually cost-ineffective unless H. Rept. 83-1193 or agency records say so.
3. Do not say the three Interior items were rescued because Congress affirmatively valued their information; the floor record establishes removal, not motive.
4. Do not infer that the Indian fiscal report was considered useless merely because it survived the committee amendment and was repealed. Recover the item-specific explanation first.
5. Do not collapse committee amendment, House passage, Senate action, and final enactment into one decision stage.
6. Do not project the later 1965 standing-committee filter backward as a formally identical procedure; only the observable filtering behavior is currently established.
