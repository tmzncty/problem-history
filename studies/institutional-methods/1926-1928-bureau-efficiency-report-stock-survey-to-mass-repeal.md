# 1926–1928: Bureau of Efficiency report-stock survey to mass repeal

## Research question

The current branch has established that the 1928 statute became an explicit precedent for later report-pruning rounds and has begun comparing individual reporting artifacts that survived or disappeared in 1928. A more basic provenance question remained underdeveloped: **where did the 128-item 1928 repeal package itself come from?**

Fresh primary evidence from the Bureau of Efficiency's own annual report supplies a much stronger process-level answer. The 1928 package was not merely a congressional list assembled at enactment. In 1926, at the request of the chairman of the House Committee on Appropriations, the Bureau of Efficiency undertook a study of the statutory reports listed in House Document No. 101 (69th Congress, 1st session), prepared a detailed item-by-item report, and drafted a bill proposing repeal of a large number of the reporting requirements. The bill was introduced March 14, 1928 and enacted May 29, 1928, eliminating 128 reports or statements.

This adds a previously missing upstream state to the long report-pruning genealogy:

```text
congressional inventory of reports
        ↓
1926 House Appropriations chairman requests Bureau of Efficiency study
        ↓
Bureau item-by-item review
        ↓
Bureau drafts repeal bill
        ↓
1928 introduction
        ↓
Congressional consideration
        ↓
128 reporting requirements eliminated
```

The episode therefore begins as a **commissioned stock review plus draft legislation**, not simply with the enacted repeal statute.

## 1. The Bureau of Efficiency explicitly records the origin of the review

In its annual report covering the period in which the legislation was enacted, the Bureau included a section headed `DISCONTINUANCE OF CERTAIN USELESS REPORTS`.

The Bureau states that in **1926**, at the instance of the chairman of the House Committee on Appropriations, it studied reports required by law to be made to Congress as listed in **House Document No. 101, 69th Congress, 1st session**. It further states that it submitted a **detailed report covering each item** in that House document and drafted a bill proposing repeal of statutory provisions requiring a large number of those reports.

Primary source:

- Bureau of Efficiency, annual report, section `Discontinuance of Certain Useless Reports`, GovInfo Serial Set 9113: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-09113_00_00-005-0363-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-09113_00_00-005-0363-0000.pdf

**Evidence strength: very high.** This is the institution that performed the review describing its own assignment, source inventory, output, and legislative follow-through.

## 2. The Bureau says its draft became the 1928 legislative vehicle

The same report says the bill was introduced on **March 14, 1928** and enacted on **May 29, 1928**. As approved, it eliminated the requirement to prepare and submit **128 separate reports or statements**.

The Bureau estimated that the legislation would save the government **more than $25,000 per year in clerical work**.

This cost figure is an actor/institution estimate, not an independently audited saving. It should be recorded as such.

Later congressional/legal discussions independently identify H. Rept. 70-1757 as relying on a Bureau of Efficiency study recommending elimination of 128 specified required reports as unnecessary, which corroborates the Bureau's own account of its role.

Supporting later source:

- Congressional oversight literature discussing H. Rept. 70-1757 and S. Rept. 70-1320, including the legislative history of the information-request provision that survived the repeal package.

**Evidence strength: very high** for the Bureau-to-legislation sequence; **actor-claim strength** for the $25,000 savings estimate.

## 3. The review started from a pre-existing inventory, not an undefined universe

The Bureau identifies **House Document No. 101** as the list it reviewed. That matters methodologically. The candidate universe was not simply `all reports anyone could remember`; it was mediated through an existing congressional inventory of reports required by law.

The observed process is therefore at least:

```text
inventory construction
        ↓
review universe
        ↓
item-level administrative assessment
        ↓
repeal candidates
        ↓
draft bill
        ↓
legislative filtering/enactment
```

This makes `candidate-set provenance` a separate historical question from `why a particular candidate was repealed`.

It also sharpens the unresolved comparison between former 25 U.S.C. §§142 and 143. We now know the Bureau says it reviewed each item **listed in H. Doc. 101**. Before interpreting §143's survival as an affirmative judgment, we need to establish whether §143 appeared in H. Doc. 101 and, if so, how the Bureau treated it. If it was absent from the inventory, non-repeal may reflect candidate-universe construction rather than a substantive retention decision.

## 4. This changes the institutional genealogy of report pruning

The branch already has strong evidence that:

- 1940–1941 involved a House committee request to the Bureau of the Budget for a survey of statutory reports;
- 1945 explicitly invoked the 1928 statute as a same-character precedent;
- 1950s–1960s rounds used executive/Budget review and congressional filtering;
- 1995 again used agency recommendations plus committee review.

The 1926 Bureau evidence now shows that the earliest node in this recovered chain already contained a recognizable division of labor:

```text
Congressional actor defines/requests review
        ↓
central executive efficiency body examines inherited reporting stock
        ↓
central body prepares item-level recommendations + draft legislation
        ↓
Congress filters/enacts
```

This is stronger than saying later rounds merely resembled the 1928 *statute*. They also resemble, at a high level, the **upstream review architecture** that generated the statute.

However, direct institutional transmission from the Bureau of Efficiency process to the Bureau of the Budget processes of 1940–1965 is **not yet established**. Similar process architecture plus later explicit citation of the 1928 statute does not prove that later administrators copied the 1926 workflow.

## 5. Historical actors vs later reconstruction vs present analysis

### Historical actors / institutional record

Directly supportable from the Bureau's annual report:

- the House Appropriations chairman initiated the Bureau study in 1926;
- the review universe was the statutory-report list in H. Doc. 101;
- the Bureau prepared a detailed report covering each listed item;
- the Bureau drafted repeal legislation;
- the bill was introduced March 14, 1928 and enacted May 29;
- the enacted law eliminated 128 reports/statements;
- the Bureau estimated annual clerical savings above $25,000.

### Later reconstruction

Later oversight/legal literature identifies H. Rept. 70-1757 and S. Rept. 70-1320 as legislative-history sources and describes the Bureau study as recommending the 128 eliminations. This corroborates the broad sequence but should not substitute for the unrecovered detailed Bureau item report.

### Present analytical language

Researcher-side labels only:

- `report-stock survey`;
- `inventory -> review -> draft -> legislative filter`;
- `candidate-set provenance`;
- `central review body`;
- `upstream solution architecture`.

Do not attribute these phrases to the Bureau, the Appropriations Committee, or Congress.

## 6. Methodological payoff: reconstruct the candidate universe before explaining survival

The strongest methodological result is a source-order rule:

```text
not repealed
!=
affirmatively retained
```

Before interpreting survival, determine:

1. Was the object present in the inventory used to define the review universe?
2. If present, did the reviewing body recommend repeal?
3. If recommended, did the draft bill contain it?
4. If drafted, did committee/floor consideration remove it?
5. If not repealed, at which gate did it leave the termination path?

This is directly relevant to the branch's 1911/1916 Indian fiscal-report comparison. Former §142 was repealed in 1928; former §143 survived. The Bureau evidence tells us exactly what source must be recovered next to distinguish `never candidate` from `considered and retained`.

## 7. Hindsight risks

1. **Do not say Congress independently selected all 128 reports.** The Bureau says it conducted the detailed study and drafted the repeal bill; Congress subsequently legislated on that proposal.
2. **Do not say the Bureau alone enacted or finally determined the list.** Drafting/recommendation and legislative enactment are different stages.
3. **Do not treat the Bureau's heading `useless reports` as proof that every item was useless in an objective sense.** It is the Bureau's own institutional framing.
4. **Do not treat the $25,000 annual saving as independently verified.** It is the Bureau's estimate.
5. **Do not infer that every statutory report in existence appeared in H. Doc. 101.** Inventory coverage must be checked.
6. **Do not infer that §143 was affirmatively protected in 1928.** Its candidate status remains unknown until H. Doc. 101 and the Bureau's detailed item report are recovered.
7. **Do not claim direct procedural inheritance into the Bureau of the Budget era.** The process resemblance is now stronger, but documentary transmission remains source debt.

## 9. Remaining uncertainty / source debt

Highest-value sources now become unusually concrete:

1. **House Document No. 101, 69th Congress, 1st session** — reconstruct the inventory universe and determine whether former 25 U.S.C. §§142 and 143 both appeared.
2. **The Bureau of Efficiency's detailed item-by-item report** to the House Appropriations chairman — recover the recommendation and rationale for the 1916 §142 object and any treatment of the 1911 §143 object.
3. **H. Rept. 70-1757 and S. Rept. 70-1320 in full** — map Bureau recommendations to congressional changes and identify candidates rescued or modified during legislation.
4. Identify the House Appropriations chairman named implicitly by the Bureau's annual report and recover the original 1926 request/correspondence.
5. Diff the Bureau draft against the March 14 introduced bill and final May 29 statute if the draft survives.

## Compact finding

> The 1928 mass repeal of 128 statutory reports had a recoverable upstream review architecture. The Bureau of Efficiency states that, at the request of the House Appropriations chairman, it began in 1926 with the statutory-report inventory in H. Doc. 101, prepared a detailed item-by-item study, and drafted repeal legislation. That bill was introduced March 14, 1928 and enacted May 29; the Bureau estimated more than $25,000 in annual clerical savings. This changes the 1928 episode from a bare `Congress repeals 128 reports` event into `inventory -> commissioned central review -> item recommendations -> draft legislation -> congressional enactment`. It also sharpens the Indian-report source debt: before explaining why the 1911 retrospective report survived while the 1916 prospective report was repealed, determine whether both were even inside the Bureau's review universe and at which selection gate their paths diverged.
