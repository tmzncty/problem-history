# 1928–1954: from automatic reports to demand-triggered congressional information

Status: **M2 source-control / institutional-method candidate / not a frozen Problem Episode**

This packet extends the 1940–1954 productivity-reporting work by recovering a longer genealogy of the congressional problem that made selective report repeal intelligible. The key finding is that 1954 was not an isolated act of pruning. In 1928 Congress had already confronted recurring statutory reports as an information-design problem and paired mass repeal with a demand-triggered information mechanism that survives in 5 U.S.C. §2954.

## 1. New finding: 1928 did not simply abolish reports

The Act of 29 May 1928, Pub. L. 70-611, 45 Stat. 986, repealed 128 statutory reporting requirements. Section 2 simultaneously required executive departments and independent establishments to furnish information requested by the House or Senate Committees on Expenditures in the Executive Departments, including requests by seven House committee members or five Senate committee members. The provision is the ancestor of current 5 U.S.C. §2954.

Primary/statutory source:
- Act of May 29, 1928, 45 Stat. 986, especially §2 at 45 Stat. 996.
- Current codification: 5 U.S.C. §2954.

Evidence: **very high / enacted statute and codification**.

The institutional change therefore was not simply:

```text
mandatory reports → less information
```

It was:

```text
many automatic recurring reports
        ↓ repeal
less routine information push
        +
request-triggered information pull
```

## 2. Actor-level rationale recovered from 1928 committee reports

H.R. Rep. No. 70-1757 and S. Rep. No. 70-1320 are repeatedly quoted in later official/legal treatments. The House report said many reports proposed for abolition had become obsolete or served no useful purpose. It also explained that, when information on a particular subject was wanted, it could be better secured by a request framed to obtain the specific information desired. To avoid losing access to information contained in abolished reports, the bill added the committee/seven-member request provision.

The Senate report similarly described §2 as preserving the ability to require discontinued information again when its necessity became evident.

High-quality reconstruction and quotation locations:
- CRS, *Congressional Oversight Manual*, discussion of 5 U.S.C. §2954 and H.R. Rep. 70-1757 / S. Rep. 70-1320.
- *Maloney v. Murphy*, 984 F.3d 50 (D.C. Cir. 2020), quoting H.R. Rep. 70-1757 and both reports' preference for specially framed requests.

Evidence grades:
- existence and statutory structure of 1928 repeal + request mechanism: **very high**.
- quoted House/Senate actor formulations: **high**, because the quotations and pinpoint citations are reproduced by CRS and a federal appellate opinion; direct scans of H.R. Rep. 70-1757 / S. Rep. 70-1320 remain desirable source-control upgrades.

## 3. Historical actor problem

The safe actor-level formulation is not “Congress invented minority oversight rights” and not “Congress wanted transparency” in a modern generic sense.

The recovered 1928 problem is narrower:

```text
statutes accumulate recurring reports
        ↓
some become obsolete / low-value
        ↓
routine submission is costly or unhelpful
        ↓
but abolishing reports risks losing needed information
        ↓
how can Congress prune routine reporting
without surrendering access when a need actually arises?
```

The institutional answer paired **report repeal** with **information-on-request**.

## 4. Link to the 1954 productivity-report repeal

The repository's `1940-1954-productivity-reporting-decoupling.md` shows that Public Law 83-706 (30 Aug. 1954) removed the dedicated annual §2b productivity report while leaving BLS's continuing productivity/labor-cost research mandate intact. The 27 Apr. 1954 House debate framed H.R. 6290 generically as removing reports that experience had shown to be unnecessary relative to their preparation cost.

The 1928 evidence changes how that 1954 event should be situated. Report-pruning was already an established congressional institutional problem at least twenty-six years earlier, and Congress had already articulated an alternative architecture: replace low-value automatic reporting with targeted retrieval when information is actually needed.

This does **not** prove that the 1954 committee consciously invoked the 1928 Act when selecting the BLS productivity report. No direct transmission edge has yet been recovered. The relationship is a documented institutional genealogy, not yet a documented citation chain.

## 5. A longer transformation chain

```text
pre-1928
many programme-specific statutory reports
        ↓
1928
128 reports pruned
+ demand-triggered §2 information mechanism
        ↓
1940
new productivity capability receives
its own annual congressional report
        ↓
1954
that dedicated annual report is pruned
while substantive research survives
        ↓
1966
1928 request mechanism recodified
without substantive change as 5 U.S.C. §2954
        ↓
2017–2022
§2954 becomes the object of litigation
when committee-minority members seek
specific executive-branch information
```

This is a >90-year institutional chain, but it must not be mistaken for one unchanged Problem Episode. The object changes from recurring-report burden and information retention (1928), to a programme-specific reporting channel (1940/1954), to the enforceability and scope of minority-member requests (modern litigation).

## 6. Historical actors / later reconstruction / researcher reconstruction

### Historical actors

1928 House and Senate actors treated accumulated routine reports as potentially obsolete or low-value while explicitly worrying that abolishing them might deprive Congress of information later found necessary. They paired repeal with targeted request authority.

1954 House actors again framed a package of statutory reports as candidates for elimination when experience showed them unnecessary and preparation cost exceeded congressional usefulness. For the BLS productivity item, only the generic package rationale is currently proven.

### Later reconstruction

CRS and federal courts reconstruct §2954 as deriving from §2 of the 1928 report-repeal statute. Modern litigation also emphasizes the unusual feature that a committee minority can invoke the statute without full committee action. That modern legal characterization must not be projected backward as the complete 1928 actor purpose.

### Researcher reconstruction

Useful analytical labels:

- **push-to-pull information transition**: routine automatic reporting is replaced or supplemented by information retrieval on demand.
- **accountability recomposition**: oversight capacity persists while its transmission architecture changes.
- **channel substitution must be proven**: repeal of report A plus existence of request mechanism B does not by itself prove actors considered B a functional substitute in a later episode.

These are researcher terms, not actor language.

## 7. Hindsight risks

1. **report repeal = less oversight** — unsafe; 1928 explicitly paired repeal with request authority.
2. **§2954 was created as a modern minority-rights statute** — modern litigation stresses minority rights, but the 1928 reports framed the provision around preserving access after report abolition and obtaining specially framed information.
3. **1928 explains the specific 1954 BLS item** — not proven without a 1954 committee report or subcommittee file invoking the earlier architecture.
4. **same statute = same problem for 90+ years** — false. The statutory capability persists while the problem attached to it changes.
5. **automatic report and targeted request are equivalent** — false. They differ in timing, agenda control, search cost, minority access and what Congress learns without already knowing what to ask.

## 10. Compact finding

> The 1954 removal of BLS's dedicated productivity report belongs to a longer congressional history of treating reporting architecture itself as a problem. In 1928 Congress abolished 128 recurring reports but simultaneously preserved a demand-triggered route for obtaining needed information, the ancestor of 5 U.S.C. §2954. This shows that report pruning could be paired with accountability recomposition rather than simple informational retreat. It does not yet prove that the 1954 BLS item was selected because Congress regarded targeted requests as its substitute; that transmission remains source debt.
