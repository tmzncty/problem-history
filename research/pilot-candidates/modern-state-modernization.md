# M1 candidate packet — “现代国家 / 现代化问题”

> Status: **surveyed as an M1 candidate, not selected as the final pilot**.
>
> Branch context: companion evidence packet for `research/pilot-selection.md`.
>
> Hard boundary: **“现代国家 / 现代化问题” is a present researcher umbrella that already conflates at least two historically distinct problem families.** A history of the `modern state` is not automatically a history of `modernization`, and neither term can be silently projected onto actors who spoke instead of `civilization`, `national independence`, `underdeveloped areas`, `economic growth`, `neo-colonialism`, or `underdevelopment`.

## 0. Provisional verdict

**Primary-source rich and exceptionally good for exposing retrospective category collapse, but too broad and too heavily occupied by mature scholarship to be the strongest M2 pilot in its present wording.**

A source chain from Fukuzawa Yukichi (1875) to André Gunder Frank (1966) spans **91 years** and contains several strong formulation breaks. The problem is not source scarcity. It is almost the opposite: the modern umbrella tempts a researcher to turn several different historical problem objects into one linear story of “modernization.”

At minimum, the material separates:

```text
A. modern-state problem family
   state form / legitimate domination / administrative concentration / sovereignty

B. civilization-development problem family
   civilization / national independence / underdevelopment / growth / technical assistance

C. sovereignty-autonomy critique
   formal independence / external economic control / neo-colonialism / produced underdevelopment
```

These families overlap historically, but overlap is not identity.

**Current pilot assessment:** extremely strong as an adversarial/failure case for object instability, policy genealogy, retrospective umbrella terms, and rival diagnoses. As a broad M2 pilot it currently ranks below the technology/labour candidate because prior-art overlap is even more extensive and the candidate title contains multiple problem histories before research begins.

A narrower Problem-History question may eventually be viable, for example:

> **When does formal national independence cease to count as sufficient evidence of substantive autonomy, and what replaces it as the relevant problem?**

That wording is still a present researcher question, not an actor formulation, and would need a longer source chain before use as a 50+ year pilot.

## 1. First failure test: “modern state” and “modernization” are not one object

A modern survey can easily produce the following false lineage:

```text
Fukuzawa -> Weber -> Truman -> Rostow -> Nkrumah -> Frank
        = successive answers to “how does a country modernize?”
```

The historical materials do not license this graph.

### Historical objects that must remain separate

| Source / moment | Actor-side object or relation | Do not silently rewrite as |
|---|---|---|
| Fukuzawa 1875 | civilization, national independence, comparative advancement | twentieth-century modernization theory |
| Weber 1919 | state, legitimate physical force, domination, bureaucracy, politics | development policy for “backward” countries |
| Truman 1949 | `underdeveloped areas`, technical knowledge, capital, production, living standards | a neutral scientific definition of modernization |
| Rostow 1959/60 | stages of economic growth, take-off, a general sequence of development | the timeless historical path of all states |
| Nkrumah 1965 | formal sovereignty versus externally directed economic/political policy | mere failure to complete modernization |
| Frank 1966 | underdevelopment as historically produced relation, not original stage | a slower location on Rostow's same ladder |

The candidate therefore directly tests the repository rule:

```text
shared present-day umbrella
!=
shared historical object
!=
shared problem formulation
```

It also exposes a second error:

```text
state-building
!= nation-building
!= civilization
!= modernization
!= development
!= economic growth
```

Historical actors may connect these terms, but the connection itself must be evidenced.

## 2. Existing scholarship / duplication map

The broad candidate has **extreme prior-art overlap**. That is a reason to narrow it, not to ignore the scholarship.

### 2.1 Cold War modernization theory is already a mature intellectual-history field

Nils Gilman, *Mandarins of the Future: Modernization Theory in Cold War America* (Johns Hopkins University Press):

- https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/2703/mandarins-future
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1353/book.16480

**Layer:** later scholarly reconstruction.

Gilman reconstructs modernization theory as a major Cold War intellectual framework, including Parsons, Shils, Almond, Rostow, MIT, policy uptake, and eventual collapse. A repository history that merely retells this intellectual genealogy under a new “Problem History” label would fail the stop condition.

Michael E. Latham, *Modernization as Ideology: American Social Science and “Nation Building” in the Kennedy Era* (University of North Carolina Press, 2000):

- https://uncpress.org/book/9780807848449/modernization-as-ideology/

**Layer:** later scholarly reconstruction.

Latham traces how social-scientific modernization ideas entered U.S. foreign-policy projects such as the Alliance for Progress, Peace Corps, and Vietnam. Again, transmission from theory into policy is already a well-developed research field.

### 2.2 “Development” has colonial and decolonizing histories that predate a simple 1949 origin story

Frederick Cooper, “Modernizing Bureaucrats, Backward Africans, and the Development Concept,” in Frederick Cooper and Randall Packard (eds.), *International Development and the Social Sciences*:

- https://doi.org/10.1525/9780520918080-004

Frederick Cooper, “Development, Modernization, and the Social Sciences in the Era of Decolonization,” *Revue d'histoire des sciences humaines* 10 (2004): 9–38:

- https://doi.org/10.4000/rhsh.3319

**Layer:** later scholarly reconstruction.

Cooper is especially important for preventing a textbook shortcut in which Truman's 1949 Point Four speech simply “invents development.” Colonial administrations, anticolonial actors, economists, and social scientists had already been struggling over development-like projects and vocabularies. The historical problem is therefore not safely dated from the first famous American speech.

### 2.3 Post-development / doctrines-of-development literature makes duplication risk even higher

Arturo Escobar, *Encountering Development: The Making and Unmaking of the Third World* (Princeton University Press, 1995).

M. P. Cowen and R. W. Shenton, *Doctrines of Development* (Routledge, 1996).

These works differ sharply in approach, but together show that “development” already has an enormous historiographic and critical literature.

### Duplication verdict

**Extreme.** The broad candidate is worth keeping only if Problem History adds something more specific than a history of modernization/development ideas. The plausible increment is to make **problem identity itself** contestable:

- Was Fukuzawa's `civilization + independence` problem later transformed into a development problem, or does a present historian merely place them under one umbrella?
- Is Weber's definition of the modern state historically connected to development policy, or only analytically adjacent?
- Does Truman's `underdeveloped areas` problem become Rostow's stages problem, and if so through what institutions and citations?
- Do Nkrumah and Frank answer modernization theory, reject its object, or replace the diagnosis with a different problem structure?
- Can formal state sovereignty and economic autonomy become historically separable problem objects?

If those questions cannot be answered from actor-recognized transmission and primary evidence, this candidate should not become the pilot.

## 3. Primary/source chain

## Episode lead A — Fukuzawa Yukichi, 1875: civilization and national independence

### Primary / authoritative source leads

Fukuzawa Yukichi, *Bunmeiron no gairyaku* / 《文明論之概略》 (1875).

Keio University bibliographic/digital record:

- https://www2.lib.keio.ac.jp/lod/fukuzawa/9812538823204033/

National Diet Library original-edition volumes, including volume 6 with Chapter 10 `自国ノ独立ヲ論ズ` (“On Our National Independence”):

- https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000039-I993904
- DOI / persistent identifier: https://doi.org/10.11501/993904

Other NDL volume leads:

- volume containing chapters on the intelligence/virtue of a country's people: https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000039-I993900
- volume containing chapter on the origins/course of Western civilization: https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000039-I993902

Modern scholarly translation / edition lead:

- Fukuzawa Yukichi, *An Outline of a Theory of Civilization*, trans. David A. Dilworth and G. Cameron Hurst III, Monumenta Nipponica Monographs, 1973.

### What the source safely establishes

The 1875 original is securely identifiable and directly organizes part of its argument around **national independence** while comparing levels/forms of civilization. This is already enough to reject a simple back-projection of twentieth-century `modernization theory` as Fukuzawa's actor vocabulary.

### Safe problem reconstruction

A cautious researcher-level reconstruction is:

> how can Japan advance in civilization in a way that preserves national independence in an unequal international order?

This formulation is **not yet actor-explicit in exactly these words**. The `national independence` chapter is direct actor evidence; the combined formulation must be page-verified against the Japanese text and/or a controlled translation before it is promoted to high-confidence `reconstructed_actor`.

### Later scholarly reconstruction

Wolfgang Seifert, “A Perspective for Japan: Fukuzawa Yukichi's ‘Theory of Civilization’, 1875,” *Historická sociologie* 13.2 (2021):

- https://doi.org/10.14712/23363525.2021.17

Seifert emphasizes national independence and reform of Japanese society as central goals, learning from advanced societies without mere imitation, and an evolutionary view of social change.

**Layer warning:** this is a twenty-first-century historical interpretation. It helps identify passages and stakes; it does not substitute for Fukuzawa's actor formulation.

### Evidence strength

- **High:** bibliographic identity, publication year, chapter organization, presence of an explicit chapter on national independence.
- **Medium-high:** the combined `civilization -> national independence` problem reconstruction, pending page-level quotation control.
- **Low / prohibited:** calling Fukuzawa an early “modernization theorist” without explicit historiographic qualification.

### Hindsight risk

`civilization` must not be normalized into `modernization`. Nor should later Japanese state-building outcomes be used to infer what Fukuzawa's 1875 problem “really was.”

## Episode lead B — Max Weber, 1919: what is distinctive about the modern state?

### Primary source

Max Weber, “Politik als Beruf” / “Politics as a Vocation,” lecture delivered 1919.

Accessible selected English translation:

- https://www.panarchy.org/weber/politics.html

For mature episode work, use a canonical German/critical edition or stable scholarly translation with page locators.

### Actor-explicit moves

Weber defines the state through its successful claim to the **monopoly of legitimate physical force within a territory**, describes politics in relation to power and domination, distinguishes grounds of legitimate authority, and treats bureaucratic administrative development and concentration/expropriation of the means of administration as characteristic of the modern state.

### Safe problem reconstruction

This is a problem of sociological definition and historical organization:

> what distinguishes the modern state as a form of legitimate domination, and how is political rule sustained and organized?

It is **not** safely rewritten as:

> how should an “underdeveloped” country modernize?

### Problem structure

```yaml
target:
  - state
  - legitimate domination
  - control of administrative / coercive means
stakes:
  - sociological definition of politics and the state
  - legitimacy and obedience
  - political vocation under modern conditions
not_established:
  - development stages
  - technical-assistance policy
  - a universal policy ladder from traditional to modern societies
```

### Evidence strength

**High** for Weber's actor-side state/legitimacy/force/bureaucracy formulation, subject to edition normalization before exact quotations enter an M2 episode.

### Why this matters for candidate selection

Weber is a useful **negative control**. A present researcher can place Weber beside modernization literature because both contain the word `modern`, but lexical adjacency supplies no problem-identity edge.

A provisional relation from Fukuzawa 1875 to Weber 1919 should therefore remain:

```text
analogy_only / undetermined
```

unless actual transmission and shared problem structure are demonstrated.

## Episode lead C — Truman, 1949: `underdeveloped areas` become a U.S. policy object

### Primary source

Harry S. Truman, Second Inaugural Address, 20 January 1949, Truman Presidential Library:

- https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/public-papers/19/inaugural-address

Archive lead for Point Four origins/drafting in the Clark M. Clifford Papers:

- https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/clark-m-clifford-papers

### Actor-explicit formulation

Truman's fourth policy point calls for a new program to make U.S. scientific advances and industrial progress available for the improvement and growth of **“underdeveloped areas.”** The speech frames poverty, low production, technical knowledge, capital investment, higher living standards, democratic cooperation, and rejection of “old imperialism” as connected policy stakes.

This is strong evidence that by January 1949 `underdeveloped areas` had become an explicit U.S. presidential policy object with an answer space centered on technical knowledge, capital, production, and cooperative development.

### Safe problem reconstruction

> how can low-production / low-living-standard regions be transformed through technical knowledge and capital investment without presenting the program as old-style imperialism?

The exact causal and ideological structure needs archive work because the inaugural address is a public speech, not a complete record of internal policy reasoning.

### Askability conditions

Supported context functions include:

- early Cold War competition;
- decolonization / delegitimation of formal empire;
- U.S. self-description as possessing scientific and industrial capacities transferable abroad;
- poverty and production framed as international peace/security concerns.

These conditions do **not** mechanically imply one inevitable modernization problem. The Clifford papers are the right next source for seeing how the problem was drafted and stabilized inside the administration.

### Evidence strength

- **High:** actor-explicit public formulation in the 20 January 1949 address.
- **High:** existence of archival drafting/background collections.
- **Medium:** any stronger claim that Point Four “created” the development problem; colonial/decolonizing development vocabularies predate it.

### Hindsight risk

Do not silently translate the speech's historically loaded `underdeveloped` category into a neutral present-day variable. Do not infer that anti-imperial rhetoric proves the program was experienced as non-imperial by other actors.

## Episode lead D — Rostow, 1959/1960: development becomes a staged growth sequence

### Primary publication record

W. W. Rostow, “The Stages of Economic Growth,” *The Economic History Review*, New Series 12.1 (August 1959): 1–16.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0289.1959.tb01829.x

Expanded book:

W. W. Rostow, *The Stages of Economic Growth: A Non-Communist Manifesto* (Cambridge University Press, 1960).

### Actor-side problem lead

Rostow organizes economic history through a staged model of growth, including the famous `take-off`. The book title itself places the schema in explicit Cold War ideological competition.

### Safe problem reconstruction

The operative problem is not merely “how to help poor places.” It is closer to:

> can economic development be rendered as a general sequence of historical stages through which societies move, and can that sequence provide a non-communist account of transformation?

Exact stage definitions and quotations should be page-verified against the 1959 article / 1960 book before promotion into a formal episode.

### Relation to Truman 1949

It is tempting to write:

```text
Point Four -> Rostow = continuous modernization doctrine
```

That is **not yet a relation verdict**.

Continuity leads:

- shared U.S. Cold War development-policy environment;
- common concern with production, growth, and transformation;
- later policy uptake of modernization theory is well documented by historians.

Discontinuity leads:

- Truman's speech is a policy program directed toward `underdeveloped areas`;
- Rostow offers a generalized historical-development schema;
- target, genre, explanatory ambition, and criteria of success differ.

Current relation status:

```text
transformed_successor? / undetermined pending transmission evidence
```

### Evidence strength

- **High:** publication metadata and existence of staged-growth formulation.
- **Medium-high:** reconstructed problem structure.
- **Not yet sufficient:** direct Truman -> Rostow problem continuity.

## Episode lead E — Nkrumah, 1965: formal sovereignty is not substantive autonomy

### Primary source

Kwame Nkrumah, *Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism* (Thomas Nelson & Sons, 1965).

Open transcription with publication provenance and chapter structure:

- https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/neo-colonialism/index.htm
- introduction: https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/neo-colonialism/introduction.htm

Bibliographic record:

- Smithsonian Libraries: https://siris-libraries.si.edu/ipac20/ipac.jsp?uri=full=3100001~!400797~!0

### Actor-explicit move

Nkrumah's introduction makes a direct distinction between the **outward trappings of international sovereignty** and actual control over a state's economic system and political policy from outside. Formal independence therefore does not settle the question of effective autonomy.

This is an unusually strong Problem-History breakpoint because it splits two objects that a linear modernization story often merges:

```text
internationally recognized state sovereignty
!=
economic / political autonomy
```

### Safe problem reconstruction

> how can colonial domination persist after formal political independence, and through what economic/political mechanisms is an apparently sovereign state directed from outside?

This is not merely an answer to “why has modernization been slow?” The diagnosis changes the object and the causal structure.

### Competing-problem significance

Against a staged or assistance-centered frame in which the main problem is insufficient internal development, Nkrumah makes external relations and control constitutive of the problem itself.

That may represent:

- a competing formulation;
- a displacement of the `underdeveloped stage` diagnosis;
- or a distinct anti-imperial problem field.

Current relation must remain **undetermined** until direct engagement/citation with specific modernization-policy texts is traced.

### Evidence strength

- **High:** actor wording and 1965 publication identity.
- **Medium-high:** exact quotation provenance through a modern transcription; formal M2 use should page-check a print/facsimile edition.
- **High:** historical distinction between formal sovereignty and externally directed policy is explicit in the text.

### Hindsight risk

Do not classify Nkrumah merely as an “anti-modernization theorist.” `Neo-colonialism` is an actor category with its own object, causal mechanisms, stakes, and answer space.

## Episode lead F — André Gunder Frank, 1966: underdevelopment is produced, not an original stage

### Primary publication record

André Gunder Frank, “The Development of Underdevelopment,” *Monthly Review* 18.4 (September 1966).

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.14452/mr-018-04-1966-08_3
- Monthly Review publication record / later archive access: https://monthlyreview.org/1966/09/01/the-development-of-underdevelopment/

### Actor-explicit critical move

Frank rejects the assumption that today's underdeveloped societies simply resemble earlier stages of today's developed societies. His argument treats underdevelopment as historically generated through relations connecting metropolis and satellite rather than as an original/traditional condition waiting to move along the same ladder.

### Safe problem reconstruction

> how should persistent underdevelopment be explained if it is not an original stage through which now-developed countries once passed?

This is a direct challenge to a stage-like problem ontology. Instead of asking how a society moves from a low stage to a high stage, the explanation can make `development` and `underdevelopment` relational and simultaneous products of one historical system.

### Relation to Rostow

A strong conceptual contrast exists:

```text
stage sequence / internal transition
vs.
relational production of development + underdevelopment
```

But conceptual contrast alone does not prove a direct historical `Rostow -> Frank` problem edge. Before assigning `displaced`, `reformulated`, or `transformed_successor`, inspect Frank's references/citations and contemporaneous debate.

### Evidence strength

- **High:** publication identity, date, journal, DOI.
- **Medium-high:** critical formulation from article text / reputable reproductions.
- **Next-source debt:** page-stable original/facsimile and explicit citation map to Rostow / modernization writers.

### Hindsight risk

Do not turn `dependency theory` into an actor umbrella before verifying when and how that label was used. Frank and Nkrumah should not be assumed to belong to one identical school or to ask an identical question simply because later textbooks juxtapose them.

## 4. A 91-year transformation map — as hypotheses, not a lineage verdict

The current evidence supports a **comparison map**, not one continuous genealogy:

```text
1875 Fukuzawa
civilization <-> national independence
        |
        |  no direct problem-identity edge established
        v
1919 Weber
modern state = legitimate domination / coercive-administrative concentration
        |
        |  lexical “modern” adjacency is not continuity
        v
1949 Truman
“underdeveloped areas” -> technical knowledge + capital + production + living standards
        |
        |  possible policy/intellectual transformation; transmission still to prove
        v
1959/60 Rostow
stages of growth / take-off / non-communist historical sequence
        |
        +----------------------------+
        |                            |
        v                            v
1965 Nkrumah                    1966 Frank
formal sovereignty             underdevelopment is
!= substantive autonomy        historically produced relation
```

The strongest result is therefore **not** “modernization evolved from Fukuzawa to Frank.” It is that the present umbrella reveals multiple points at which the object itself changes:

- national survival/independence can be tied to comparative civilization;
- `modern state` can be a problem of legitimate domination rather than development;
- poverty/production can become a technical-assistance policy object;
- economic transformation can be recoded as universal stages;
- formal sovereignty can be separated from substantive autonomy;
- underdevelopment can cease to be treated as a prior stage and become a relational historical product.

This is exactly the kind of case where a clean linear graph would be methodologically suspicious.

## 5. Emergence, transformation, and disappearance conditions

### 5.1 Fukuzawa: civilization becomes tied to independence

**Candidate askability conditions:**

- unequal international order and threat to national autonomy;
- Meiji-era reorganization of political/intellectual institutions;
- newly intensified comparative encounter with Western states and knowledge;
- the need to define what forms of borrowing, social reform, and civilizational advance could secure independence.

**Evidence status:** mixed. National independence and civilization are actor-side objects; the causal configuration needs close primary-text work.

### 5.2 Weber: a different modern-state problem

**Candidate conditions:**

- European historical concentration of coercive/administrative means;
- bureaucratization and legal-rational authority;
- postwar German political crisis and the lecture genre of political vocation.

This is not a chronological “next stage” of Fukuzawa's problem. Different object, audience, stakes, and explanatory task make discontinuity the default until proven otherwise.

### 5.3 Truman: `underdeveloped areas` become actionable at presidential-policy level

**Supported conditions:**

- early Cold War geopolitical competition;
- delegitimation of old formal imperialism;
- decolonization and a changing international order;
- U.S. scientific/industrial capacity represented as transferable knowledge;
- poverty/production represented as security and peace concerns.

The Clark Clifford Papers provide a concrete route for reconstructing **how this formulation emerged inside policy drafting**, rather than treating the inaugural speech as spontaneous origin.

### 5.4 Rostow: policy object becomes generalized historical sequence

**Candidate conditions:**

- Cold War social science;
- demand for a non-communist developmental account;
- institutional traffic among universities, foundations, and policy networks.

These are well established in later scholarship, but direct actor/institutional evidence is needed before they become episode evidence.

### 5.5 Nkrumah / Frank: formal decolonization makes older diagnoses contestable

A crucial transformation becomes askable once many territories possess formal sovereignty while economic dependence and unequal international relations persist.

This allows a different problem structure:

```text
not only:
“how does a poor/traditional society advance?”

but:
“what if the relations producing poverty/dependence survive political independence?”
```

Nkrumah and Frank do not necessarily ask the same question. Their joint importance here is that each undermines a simple equation of formal statehood + internal stage progression with substantive autonomy/development.

### 5.6 No safe disappearance finding yet

This survey does **not** establish that any of these problems became unaskable or disappeared.

Do not write:

```text
modernization theory was criticized
-> modernization problem disappeared
```

Critique, institutional decline, lexical replacement, and loss of askability are different claims. The broad candidate currently offers strong **split / reframing / object-reclassification** evidence but weak disappearance evidence.

## 8. Hindsight-risk register

1. **Modernization back-projection:** treating Fukuzawa's `civilization` as if it were already twentieth-century modernization theory.
2. **Modern-state/development collapse:** treating Weber's modern-state sociology as a development-policy theory because both contain `modern` in later translations/categories.
3. **Vocabulary synonymy:** equating civilization, modernization, development, state-building, nation-building, and economic growth.
4. **Point Four origin myth:** treating Truman's 1949 speech as the first time “development” became thinkable, ignoring colonial/decolonizing histories.
5. **Neutralizing `underdeveloped`:** converting an actor/policy category into an objective timeless measurement without reconstructing its political work.
6. **Truman-to-Rostow teleology:** narrating a smooth doctrine from technical assistance to stages without tracing people, institutions, citations, and changed answer spaces.
7. **Formal-sovereignty naturalization:** assuming internationally recognized independence settles the historical problem of autonomy that Nkrumah explicitly reopens.
8. **Anti-modernization flattening:** treating Nkrumah and Frank as merely negative answers to one modernization question rather than possible changes of problem object and causation.
9. **Dependency-theory backfill:** using a later school label to make Nkrumah and Frank contemporaneously identical.
10. **Outcome hindsight:** using later economic outcomes to judge what 1949/1960 actors “should have known,” instead of reconstructing their answer space.
11. **Policy-impact shortcut:** inferring that an idea caused a government program because the vocabulary resembles it.
12. **Decolonization teleology:** treating the transition from empire to nation-state as a complete transfer of autonomy rather than an actor-contested historical transformation.
13. **Chronology-as-relation:** interpreting 1875 -> 1919 -> 1949 -> 1960 -> 1965 -> 1966 as a genealogy merely because the dates form a sequence.
14. **Critique-as-disappearance:** assuming a criticized theory/problem ceased to be askable.

## 9. What would make this candidate distinctive enough for Problem History?

The candidate should **not** be selected if the final product would merely be:

```text
Meiji modernization
-> Weberian modern state
-> Truman development
-> Rostow modernization theory
-> dependency critique
```

That is exactly the retrospective textbook graph the method is designed to interrogate.

A distinctive Problem-History contribution would instead require findings such as:

- the same institution/actor explicitly replacing one formulation with another;
- a point where `state sovereignty` and `economic autonomy` become separable historical problem objects;
- evidence that `underdevelopment` changes from a condition/stage into a historically produced relation;
- an episode where a development-policy answer remains institutionally alive while its original problem diagnosis loses credibility;
- a documented case in which actors reject not only an answer but the opponent's **question structure**.

Without evidence of that kind, mature intellectual/development history already covers the subject more effectively.

## 11. Next-source targets

The highest-value next work is not to extend the chronology automatically. It is to verify transmission and problem replacement at the strongest breakpoints.

### A. Fukuzawa 1875
