## What these passages support and complicate

### W1 — An explicit dispute about criticism and artistic freedom

**Supports actor-side context.** In I's opening paragraph Wilde says he cannot understand criticism of a work of art “from a moral standpoint” and distinguishes art from ethics. In III he protests a theory of government censorship over imaginative literature and, in the final paragraph, rejects an editor's right to restrain artistic subject-matter. These are situated replies to newspaper editors, not an abstract question inferred only from the 1891 Preface.

They establish what Wilde publicly argues in the transmitted letters. His characterization of the critics' attacks is not an independently recovered account of their positions, nor evidence that the government actually censored the novel.

### W2 — Moral content is not the same as an artwork's purpose

**Supports the candidate's cautious counter-formulation; complicates a literal denial of morality.** In V Wilde describes his difficulty as “keeping the extremely obvious moral subordinate to the artistic and dramatic effect.” The next paragraph admits uncertainty about success: “I think the moral too apparent.” He hopes to correct this when the story appears in a volume; this is an intention, not proof of what a later revision achieved.

In the following paragraph he describes the moral as “artistically and deliberately suppressed”: it is not enunciated as a general law but realized in individual lives, becoming “simply a dramatic element in a work of art, and not the object of the work of art itself.” Read these claims together. He neither denies a moral nor straightforwardly declares the artistic subordination successfully completed. This is his account of his own work under criticism, not a finding about every version, every reader or all art.

### W3 — Acknowledged moral reception does not restore a single moral criterion

**Complicates ‘useless’ = no moral content, value or effects.** In VII Wilde says readers more responsive to ethics “will see its moral lesson” and treats such criticism as gratifying. In VIII he calls it a tribute to the story's “ethical beauty”, while distinguishing ethical responsiveness from attention to style and treatment. The passages support the existence of these acknowledgments in his argument, not independent measurements of audience response.

His accounts of Whibley, religious papers and other readers remain **self-reported reception**. Likewise, VIII's reported number of reviews is not an independently checked statistic. The contrast between I/VI's art–ethics separation and V/VII/VIII's moral acknowledgments should remain visible; a sequence of polemical replies is not automatically a unified, unchanging aesthetic doctrine.
