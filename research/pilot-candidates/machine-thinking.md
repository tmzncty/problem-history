# M1 candidate packet — “机器能否思考？”

> Status: **surveyed as an M1 candidate, not selected as the final pilot**.
>
> Branch context: companion evidence packet for `research/pilot-selection.md`.
>
> Hard boundary: **“机器能否思考？” is a present researcher umbrella.** It must not be back-projected as one timeless actor question stretching from the Analytical Engine to modern AI.
>
> Repository boundary: this packet is a **formal M1 literature/source audit**, not a repeat of `studies/m0-calibration/machine-intelligence/`. The M0 corpus already uses Turing 1950, Dartmouth 1955, Lighthill 1972, Searle 1980, and GPT-4 2023 as an adversarial calibration set and explicitly refuses to treat them as one accepted lineage.

## 0. Provisional verdict

**Very strong method/adversarial candidate; weak choice for the first M2 pilot because prior-art and repository overlap are extreme.**

A source chain from Ada Lovelace (1843) through Geoffrey Jefferson/Turing (1949–50), Newell & Simon (1976), and Searle (1980) spans **137 years** and exposes unusually sharp changes in problem structure. But that very richness creates a trap: modern readers can collapse `origination`, `learning`, `thinking`, `intelligent action`, `understanding`, `intentionality`, and `consciousness` into a single timeless “machine mind” problem when the historical actors do not.

The strongest methodological result is therefore negative:

```text
machine powers / origination
≠ learning
≠ ordinary-language “thinking”
≠ imitation performance
≠ general intelligent action
≠ understanding / intentionality
≠ consciousness / feeling
```

This candidate is excellent for failure cases, identity fixtures, and later M3 work. It is less attractive as the first M2 pilot because the repository already contains substantial machine-intelligence material and because AI/intelligence history is one of the most saturated prior-art fields among the five M1 candidates.

## 1. Existing scholarship / duplication map

- Philosophy of Science Archive preprint: https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/20484/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-021-01318-6

**Layer:** later scholarly reconstruction.

**Use here:** especially high duplication warning. A Problem History pilot that merely retells “how the Turing Test arose” would duplicate a mature historical argument.

**Method use:** the article is valuable as a source map and transmission hypothesis generator, but actor formulations must still be checked against Hartree, Jefferson, Turing, and related contemporaneous documents.

- Bibliographic record: https://wellcomecollection.org/works/eutapssp

**Layer:** later history / retrospective synthesis.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/1354856517715164

**Layer:** later media/history-of-technology reconstruction.

### Duplication verdict

**Prior-art overlap: extreme.**

The only strong Problem History increment would be to adjudicate **problem identity and problem replacement** more strictly than these broader histories normally need to do. The repository must not sell this candidate as “the first history of the question whether machines can think.”

## 2. Primary-source chain

## Episode lead A — Lovelace, 1843: powers and limits of the Analytical Engine

### Source

Ada Lovelace’s notes to Luigi Federico Menabrea, “Sketch of the Analytical Engine Invented by Charles Babbage,” originally published in *Scientific Memoirs*, vol. III (1843).

Open text / volume witnesses:

- Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/75107/75107-h/75107-h.htm
- Biodiversity Heritage Library scan of *Scientific Memoirs*, vol. III: https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/item/52172

### Actor evidence

In Note G, Lovelace states that the Analytical Engine has no pretensions to **originate** anything; it can do what humans know how to order it to perform. Elsewhere the notes distinguish the practical possibility of the Engine from the then-speculative idea of a “thinking or reasoning machine.”

### Problem structure

```yaml
target:
  - the powers and limits of the Analytical Engine
  - whether its operations can originate results beyond ordered procedures
stakes:
  - what kind of intellectual work the Engine can perform
  - how its symbolic operations may affect scientific inquiry
presuppositions:
  - instructions and operations can be formally specified
  - execution of operations is distinct from origination
answer_space:
  - the Engine can execute ordered operations
  - it may produce scientifically surprising consequences indirectly
  - origination is not licensed by Lovelace's formulation
```

### Evidence strength

**High** for the 1843 actor text.

### Hindsight warning

Do not turn Lovelace into an actor explicitly debating twentieth-century `AI`, consciousness, or the Turing Test. The historically safer object is the Analytical Engine’s powers, operation, and originality.

## Episode lead B — 1946–49: “electronic brains,” learning, and consciousness become competing problems

This transition is one of the most historically promising parts of the candidate, but some details currently rely on later reconstruction and therefore remain a source-debt area.

### Later reconstruction

Gonçalves reconstructs a 1940s controversy in which Douglas Hartree, Michael Polanyi, Geoffrey Jefferson, and Turing disputed what electronic calculating machines could do, whether they could learn or “think for themselves,” and what would count as evidence. The reconstruction also traces Hartree’s use of the Lovelace objection and Turing’s responses.

**Layer:** later scholarly reconstruction. It is not a substitute for Hartree’s original 1946/1949 texts.

### Strong primary anchor — Geoffrey Jefferson, 1949

Geoffrey Jefferson, “The Mind of Mechanical Man,” Lister Oration, delivered 9 June 1949 and published in the *British Medical Journal*, 25 June 1949.

- BMJ: https://www.bmj.com/content/1/4616/1105
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.1.4616.1105
- King’s College Turing Digital Archive, Turing’s copy/offprint, `AMT/B/44`: https://turingarchive.kings.cam.ac.uk/publications-lectures-and-talks-amtb/amt-b-44

### Actor formulation

Jefferson’s famous criterion is not merely whether a machine can produce a poem or music. He ties equivalence with a brain to producing such work because of **thoughts and emotions felt**, and to the machine’s knowing what it has done. His target therefore includes consciousness/feeling and self-knowledge, not just outward performance.

### Problem structure

```yaml
target:
  - whether a mechanical system can possess mental life comparable to a brain
stakes:
  - the boundary between simulation/performance and felt consciousness
presuppositions:
  - outward production alone does not establish inner mental life
answer_space:
  - performance without feeling is insufficient
  - equivalence would require felt thought/emotion and knowledge of one's own act
```

### Evidence strength

**High** for Jefferson’s formulation; **medium** for the wider Hartree/Polanyi/Turing controversy until the remaining original texts are individually page-verified.

## Episode lead C — Turing, 1950: explicit replacement of the ordinary-language question

### Source

Alan M. Turing, “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” *Mind* 59, no. 236 (October 1950): 433–460.

- Oxford Academic: https://academic.oup.com/mind/article/LIX/236/433/986238
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433

### Actor-explicit problem move

Turing opens by proposing to consider the question **“Can machines think?”** but immediately argues that defining “machine” and “think” through ordinary usage would be an unsuitable way to settle it. He replaces the original question with the imitation game and later says the original wording is too meaningless to deserve discussion in that form.

This is unusually strong evidence for **problem reformulation inside a single historical text**.

### Problem structure

```yaml
target:
  - not an undefined essence of “thinking”
  - observable success in an imitation-game arrangement
stakes:
  - whether a better-posed operational problem can replace a verbally unstable one
presuppositions:
  - ordinary-language polling/definition is not an adequate decision procedure
  - behavioural discrimination can structure a tractable substitute problem
answer_space:
  - success/failure in the imitation game
  - objections to machine intelligence considered under the reformulated frame
```

### Relation to Jefferson

Turing explicitly discusses Jefferson under the “argument from consciousness,” so there is actor-recognized engagement. But `engagement` does not mean the two actors share the same criterion of success:

- Jefferson makes felt thought/emotion and self-knowledge central;
- Turing deliberately substitutes a behavioural discrimination game for an attempt to settle inner consciousness directly.

A likely relation is `reformulated` or `transformed_successor`, but a final edge requires the full pairwise `IDENTITY-CHECK`.

### Disappearance warning

Turing’s rejection of the original wording is **not evidence that “Can machines think?” disappeared as a public problem**. The wording continued to circulate, including in later BBC discussion. Therefore:

```text
one actor explicitly displaces a formulation
≠ the wider problem becomes unaskable
```

This is a useful future negative-evidence fixture.

## Episode lead D — Newell & Simon, 1976: an empirical theory of systems capable of intelligent action

### Source

Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon, “Computer Science as Empirical Inquiry: Symbols and Search,” *Communications of the ACM* 19, no. 3 (March 1976): 113–126.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/360018.360022
- CMU University Archives materials for the Turing Award lecture: https://digitalcollections.library.cmu.edu/node/58926

### Actor formulation

Newell and Simon present computer science as an empirical discipline and formulate the **physical symbol system hypothesis**: a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action. They treat this as an empirical hypothesis exposed to evidence rather than as a purely verbal definition.

### Problem structure

```yaml
target:
  - what class of physical mechanisms is necessary/sufficient for general intelligent action
stakes:
  - whether computer science can produce empirical theory, not only artefacts
  - how symbol structures/search explain intelligent behaviour
presuppositions:
  - intelligent action can be studied through physical symbol systems
  - building systems can function as empirical tests
answer_space:
  - the physical-symbol-system hypothesis may be supported, modified, or falsified
```

### Discontinuity relative to Turing 1950

The overlap in machine intelligence is real, but the operative question changes:

- Turing focuses on replacing an ill-posed ordinary-language question with an imitation-game criterion;
- Newell & Simon propose a scientific hypothesis about **mechanisms/classes of systems** sufficient and necessary for intelligent action.

`transformed_successor` is plausible; `continuous` is not licensed merely by the shared word “intelligence.”

### Evidence strength

**High** for published actor formulation and bibliographic metadata.

## Episode lead E — Searle, 1980: program instantiation and intentionality

### Source

John R. Searle, “Minds, Brains, and Programs,” *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 3, no. 3 (1980).

- Cambridge Core: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/minds-brains-and-programs/DC644B47A4299C637C89772FACC2706A

### Actor formulation

Searle explicitly argues that instantiating a computer program is **not by itself sufficient for intentionality**. His target is the relation between program execution, understanding, and the causal powers associated with minds/brains.

### Problem structure

```yaml
target:
  - whether program instantiation alone is sufficient for intentionality/understanding
stakes:
  - what computational explanation can establish about minds
presuppositions:
  - syntax/program execution can be distinguished from intentional content
answer_space:
  - program instantiation is sufficient
  - program instantiation is insufficient without further causal properties
```

### Discontinuity relative to Newell & Simon

A modern umbrella can make this look like a direct “yes/no” sequel to 1976, but the target has changed from **general intelligent action** to **intentionality/understanding and sufficiency of program instantiation**. A relation may exist through Strong AI debates, but it must be demonstrated through direct citations/reception rather than inferred from conceptual contrast.

### Evidence strength

**High** for Searle’s explicit published position; **undetermined** for the precise identity relation to Newell & Simon until transmission/reception evidence is audited.

## 4. Emergence, transformation, and disappearance

### 4.1 Emergence

This candidate currently does **not** support a single date when “the question” appears. At minimum, three different historical conditions are visible:

- nineteenth-century calculating engines make machine powers/origination discussable;
- electronic computing and wartime/postwar machine-building make learning and “electronic brain” claims materially salient in the 1940s;
- institutional AI/computer science later makes intelligence an empirical research-program object.

These are researcher analytic breakpoints, not claims that a timeless problem was dormant until technology activated it.

### 4.2 Transformation

The strongest verified transformation is Turing 1950 because the actor himself replaces one formulation with another. The later move from performance criteria to mechanism/theory (Newell & Simon) and then to intentionality/understanding (Searle) is strongly suggestive but still needs pairwise transmission evidence before relation labels are frozen.

### 4.3 Disappearance

**No broad disappearance verdict is currently licensed.**

Turing locally rejects the original question as badly formed, yet the same wording and neighbouring “thinking machine” discourse continue socially. This is precisely why Problem History must distinguish:

```text
rejected by one actor
from
loses institutional/public askability
```

The candidate is therefore better for `reformulation`, `split`, and `recurrence-with-changed-meaning` than for a clean problem-death case.

## 6. Hindsight risks

1. **Perennial-question fallacy:** rewriting every source as an answer to “Can machines think?”
2. **AI back-projection:** calling Lovelace or the 1940s debate `AI` in the later institutional sense without qualification.
3. **Capability/consciousness collapse:** treating performance, learning, origination, intelligence, understanding, intentionality, and feeling as synonyms.
4. **Turing-test simplification:** saying Turing “defined thinking as passing a test” while omitting that he explicitly replaces an ill-posed original question.
5. **Genealogy = identity:** treating Turing’s “Lady Lovelace’s objection” or later AI histories as proof that 1843 and 1950 instantiate the same problem.
6. **Programme = proposition:** treating Dartmouth-style research programme formation as simply a yes/no answer to Turing.
7. **Behaviour = mechanism:** assuming a behavioural criterion and a theory of physical-symbol mechanisms answer the same question.
8. **Intelligence = intentionality:** treating Newell & Simon and Searle as merely opposite answers instead of checking whether the target changed.
9. **Actor rejection = disappearance:** inferring that because Turing rejects the wording, society stopped asking it.
10. **Public recurrence = continuity:** inferring that later “thinking machine” headlines restore the same historical problem unchanged.
11. **Present success = historical answer:** using later AI capabilities to adjudicate what Lovelace, Jefferson, or Turing historically meant.
12. **Repository confirmation bias:** letting the large amount of existing AI material make this candidate look preferable simply because it is already well documented.

---

## 7. Source debts / unresolved questions

Before this candidate could support an M2-quality episode graph, the highest-value missing work would be:

- page-verify Hartree’s 1946/1949 writings rather than relying on Gonçalves’s reconstruction;
- recover Polanyi’s contemporaneous intervention and determine whether Turing actually responds to the same problem formulation;
- inspect 1949 Manchester seminar/newspaper exchange evidence directly;
- audit citation/reception links from Turing 1950 into Newell & Simon 1976 rather than assuming disciplinary ancestry equals problem continuity;
- audit Searle’s direct targets and contemporaneous Strong AI literature, including whether Newell/Simon-style claims are explicitly identified;
- separate specialist research discourse from popular “thinking machine” discourse when making any askability/disappearance claim.

### Final candidate verdict

**Do not choose broad “机器能否思考？” as the first M2 pilot.**

The candidate is one of the best possible stress tests for the project’s anti-hindsight method, but choosing it now would over-invest in a domain where both external historiography and this repository are already unusually dense. Its strongest future role is likely:

- an M3 failure-case cluster;
- a real-world identity-fixture suite;
- a narrow 1946–50 exchange episode testing explicit reformulation;
- a demonstration that `actor genealogy`, `public recurrence`, and `problem identity` are three different claims.

For M2, the current **technology/labour** candidate remains stronger because it offers comparably long source coverage, strong contemporaneous competing formulations, and a more promising opportunity to test not only reformulation/split but also de-generalisation or disappearance without simply duplicating the repository’s existing AI research.
