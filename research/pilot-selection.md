# Candidate 4 — “技术是否替代劳动？”

## 0. Verdict before detail

**Provisional assessment: strong pilot candidate, but not yet selected.**

It is unusually good for testing Problem History because a modern umbrella such as “will technology replace workers?” can organize a long bibliography, while the historical actors repeatedly formulate materially different problems:

- machinery and the **interests of social classes**;
- a temporary **labour-absorption maladjustment**;
- a cybernation-induced break between **jobs and income**;
- an adjustment problem of **aggregate demand, displacement, mobility, and public policy**.

The available evidence already spans **1821–1966 (145 years)** and contains strong contemporaneous disagreement in 1964–66. This makes it possible to test continuity *and* discontinuity rather than merely collect repeated predictions of job loss.

The main danger is equally clear: there is already mature scholarship on the “Machinery Question,” “technological unemployment,” and “automation.” The project would add value only if it adjudicates **problem identity / transformation / disappearance** across those literatures rather than renaming their contents “problem history.”

---

## 1. Do not start with the modern question

### Researcher analytic umbrella

> **“技术会不会替代劳动？” / “Will technology replace labour?”**

This is useful for selecting sources. It is **not** yet evidence that Ricardo, Keynes, the Ad Hoc Committee on the Triple Revolution, Lyndon Johnson, Daniel Bell, and the 1966 Commission all asked the same question.

Candidate relation graph — **hypotheses only**:

```text
18C improvement / machinery discourse
        ↓ ?
1815–1848 “Machinery Question”
        ↓ ?  (de-generalisation / displacement / evidence gap)
1930 technological unemployment as temporary maladjustment
        ↓ ?
1964–66 automation/cybernation controversy
        ├─ cybernation → distribution / jobs-income break
        └─ adjustment frame → aggregate demand / local displacement / policy
```

No arrow above is a frozen `continuous` relation.

## 2. Existing scholarship map

### 2.1 Maxine Berg — *The Machinery Question and the Making of Political Economy, 1815–1848* (1980)

**Type:** major scholarly monograph / later historical reconstruction.

Cambridge University Press book record and DOI:

- https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/machinery-question-and-the-making-of-political-economy-18151848/0D8821A8FBEC62CDE2AD36B5C49B0469
- https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511560330

Especially relevant:

- Introduction, pp. 1–8: https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511560330.002
- “Engels and Mill,” pp. 315–342: https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511560330.016

**What Berg contributes to this project:**

- She reconstructs a historically specific early-nineteenth-century **Machinery Question**, rather than treating all concern about technical change as one timeless controversy.
- Her introduction argues that eighteenth-century “improvement” did not yet amount to the same generalized Machinery Question; the early nineteenth-century conjunction of mechanisation, expanding industrial towns, population growth, and post-Napoleonic crisis altered the problem field.
- Her final chapter is methodologically valuable because it does **not** say the Machinery Question was simply “solved.” By mid-century its context and significance had changed; machinery continued to matter in particular industries, but it was no longer generalized and singled out in the same way. Mid-Victorian prosperity and organized intermediary bodies changed the conditions under which the earlier generalized problem had operated.

**Evidence strength for our claims:** high for historiography; **not actor evidence**.

**Problem-History use:** excellent candidate for testing `emergence` and a possible **loss of generalized askability/salience without a single solution**. Before formalizing that conclusion, however, the transition needs contemporaneous mid-century evidence rather than Berg alone.

### 2.2 Amy Sue Bix — *Inventing Ourselves Out of Jobs? America’s Debate over Technological Unemployment, 1929–1981* (2002)

Johns Hopkins University Press:

- https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/1808/inventing-ourselves-out-jobs
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.56021/9780801870163

**Type:** major scholarly monograph / later reconstruction.

**Relevance:** Bix maps a U.S. debate beginning in the Depression, with business, science/engineering, labor, government and popular culture offering competing accounts of mechanization, unemployment and progress. This makes the 1930s and postwar periods source-rich and warns against reducing “technological unemployment” to economists alone.

**Evidence strength:** high for literature map; not a substitute for actor texts.

### 2.3 Jason Resnikoff — *Labor’s End: How the Promise of Automation Degraded Work* (2021)

University of Illinois Press / Illinois Scholarship Online:

- https://academic.oup.com/illinois-scholarship-online/book/44260
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.5622/illinois/9780252044250.001.0001

**Type:** major scholarly monograph / later reconstruction.

**Relevance:** Resnikoff argues that postwar **“automation” was not a stable, self-evident technical object**. The word organized an ideological discourse about work, freedom and the expected abolition of labor even when actual labour processes did not match that rhetoric. For this repository the methodological warning is more important than accepting every substantive thesis:

```text
“automation” vocabulary continuity
≠ stable technical referent
≠ actual labour displacement
≠ problem continuity
```

**Evidence strength:** high for historiographic warning and postwar source map.

### 2.4 Anton Küsters & Benjamin Schneider — “What is technological unemployment?” (2025)

Oxford Economic and Social History Working Papers, University of Oxford, 13 March 2025:

- https://www.economics.ox.ac.uk/publication/2093714/ora-hyrax

**Type:** recent historiographic / history-of-economic-thought mapping.

**Relevance:** the paper explicitly finds that “technological unemployment” has been **defined differently** across economic writing; it examines classic texts and 153 journal articles and reports a long-run narrowing / technicalization of the debate with renewed intensity during periods of technological anxiety.

**Problem-History implication:** even the apparently stable technical term does not give us a stable problem identity.

**Evidence strength:** high for literature map; use primary texts for episode verdicts.

## 3. Primary-source chain and actor formulations

## Episode lead A — Ricardo, 1821: machinery and class interests

### Source

David Ricardo, *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation*, 3rd ed. (1821), ch. 31, “On Machinery.”

Open authoritative text:

- https://www.econlib.org/book-chapters/chapter-ch-31-on-machinery/

### Source status

- historical actor text;
- chapter added in the third edition (1821);
- stable chapter-level locator available;
- key wording can be verified directly in the text.

### Actor-explicit formulation

Ricardo says the chapter investigates the **influence of machinery on the interests of the different classes of society**. He also explicitly records that his own earlier opinion had changed.

The important historical move is not a generic prediction “machines will replace humans.” Ricardo is analysing whether an innovation that increases net produce can simultaneously reduce the fund supporting labour, create redundant population relative to employment funds, and therefore injure labourers even while landlords/capitalists benefit.

He explicitly accepts that machinery can be harmful to the labouring class, while also arguing that machinery should not therefore simply be discouraged.

### Problem structure

```yaml
target:
  - machinery as a change in production and capital allocation
stakes:
  - distributional effects across landlords / capitalists / labourers
  - demand for labour and labourers' condition
presuppositions:
  - distinction between net and gross produce
  - class-specific interests may diverge
answer_space:
  - machinery is generally beneficial to all classes
  - machinery can injure labourers while increasing net produce
  - discouraging machinery is nevertheless unsafe / counterproductive
```

### Evidence strength

**High** for Ricardo’s actor formulation and revision of his own position.

### Hindsight warning

Do not rewrite this as Ricardo asking whether “AI/technology will take all jobs.” His object, causal mechanism, social classes, economic vocabulary, and policy constraints are historically specific.

---

## Transition lead A→B — a generalized Machinery Question loses its earlier form

This is currently **later scholarly reconstruction**, not yet an actor-level disappearance verdict.

Berg’s Chapter 14 argues that by the mid-nineteenth century machinery still caused industry-specific conflicts, yet the earlier generalized national Machinery Question had changed in context and significance. The mid-Victorian boom, heavy use of both machinery and labour, and organization of workers/employers into intermediary bodies altered the earlier crisis configuration.

### Why this matters

This may become one of the repository’s clearest tests of:

```text
problem no longer generalized
≠ machinery stopped changing work
≠ all displacement ended
≠ problem was “solved”
```

### Current evidence strength

**Medium** as a candidate transition because the claim currently rests primarily on Berg’s later reconstruction.

### Required primary-source work

Before labeling `weakened`, `displaced`, or `became_unaskable`, collect contemporaneous evidence from ca. 1840s–1870s showing changes in:

- how political economists frame machinery/labour;
- parliamentary treatment of machinery-specific distress;
- union/employer organization and problem responsibility;
- whether “machinery per se” ceases to aggregate otherwise distinct disputes.

Negative evidence alone will not suffice.

## Episode lead B — Keynes, 1930: technological unemployment as temporary maladjustment

### Source

John Maynard Keynes, “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren” (1930).

Publication / edition control:

- first appeared in *The Nation and Athenæum*, 11 and 18 October 1930;
- *Collected Writings of John Maynard Keynes*, pp. 321–332, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/UPO9781139524162.031
- open text derived from *Essays in Persuasion*: https://www.economicsnetwork.ac.uk/archive/keynes_persuasion/Economic_Possibilities_for_our_Grandchildren.htm

### Actor-explicit formulation

Keynes explicitly names **“technological unemployment”** and defines it as labour-economising discoveries outrunning the pace at which new uses for labour are found. Crucially, he calls this a **temporary phase of maladjustment**.

The essay then makes a second move: in the long run, technical efficiency and capital accumulation are presented as solving the old “economic problem” of subsistence; the prospective problem becomes how people live when economic necessity no longer provides their organizing purpose.

### Problem structure

```yaml
target:
  - labour absorption during rapid technical change
stakes:
  - present unemployment and painful readjustment
  - future life under abundance / leisure
presuppositions:
  - technical efficiency continues rising
  - new uses for labour can eventually absorb displacement
  - long-run abundance changes the meaning of the economic problem itself
answer_space:
  - short-run maladjustment
  - long-run reduction of necessary labour
  - social/cultural preparation for leisure rather than permanent anti-machine policy
```

### Evidence strength

**High** for actor formulation. The modern label “technological unemployment” is actually present here, but lexical identity still does not settle relation to 1821 or 1964.

### Discontinuity evidence relative to Ricardo

- Ricardo’s central object is machinery’s **differential effect on classes** through gross/net produce and labour demand.
- Keynes explicitly embeds displacement in a **temporary temporal adjustment** and projects a later problem of abundance and purpose.
- The units of analysis, stakes, causal framing and expected long-run answer differ.

At present, `transformed_successor` is a plausible research hypothesis, not a verdict.

## Episode lead C — 1964–66: one difficulty, competing automation/cybernation problems

This period is especially strong for a pilot because **contemporaries explicitly disagree about what the problem is**, not merely about the answer.

### C1. Ad Hoc Committee on the Triple Revolution, 1964

#### Primary source

*The Triple Revolution: Cybernation, Weaponry, Human Rights*, Ad Hoc Committee on the Triple Revolution, reprinted from *Liberation*, April 1964.

Digitized facsimile, Wisconsin Historical Society:

- https://www.wisconsinhistory.org/record/multi/W01CVNK/triple-revolution-cybernation-weapons-human-rights
- Unique ID: `W01CVNK`
- physical extent: 12 pages
- source collection: Pamela P. Allen papers, Mss 327, Folder 1

Also independently catalogued at Claremont Colleges Special Collections:

- https://claremont.as.atlas-sys.com/repositories/3/archival_objects/63135
- Social Movements Collection H-Mss-1031, Box 5, Folder 10, Item 1

#### Facsimile verification this survey

- p. 2: statement of the “Cybernation Revolution” as a new production era combining computer and automated self-regulating machine and requiring progressively less human labour;
- p. 3: identifies the fundamental problem in distribution: productive abundance exists while income remains tied to jobs and scarcity-oriented institutions;
- p. 5: “Need for a New Consensus / Proposal for Action”; argues that the jobs–income link is breaking and proposes an adequate income as a right.

#### Actor formulation

This is **not merely a mass-unemployment forecast**. The problem is a claimed structural mismatch between cybernated abundance and an economic/institutional order that distributes income principally through employment.

```yaml
target:
  - cybernation as a new production system
  - jobs–income linkage
stakes:
  - exclusion from abundance despite productive capacity
  - institutional legitimacy and social order
presuppositions:
  - cybernation radically reduces required human labour
  - productive abundance is technically available
  - employment cannot remain the adequate distribution mechanism
answer_space:
  - income as a right
  - major public/institutional reorganization
```

**Evidence strength: high** for what the manifesto itself says; predictive accuracy is a separate claim.

### C2. Lyndon B. Johnson, March and August 1964

#### Primary sources

March 9, 1964, proposal for a National Commission:

- https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/letter-the-president-the-senate-and-the-speaker-the-house-proposing-national-commission

August 19, 1964, signing remarks:

- https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/remarks-upon-signing-bill-creating-the-national-commission-technology-automation-and

#### Actor formulation

Johnson recognizes dislocation, lost jobs, and poverty amid technological progress, but frames the task as obtaining maximum benefits while limiting hardship, creating more/better jobs, understanding change and planning for adjustment. In the signing speech he explicitly rejects treating automation as the enemy.

This is a contemporaneous competing frame:

```yaml
target:
  - harmful adjustment costs of technological progress
stakes:
  - worker/family hardship
  - employment growth and prosperity
presuppositions:
  - technology also creates industries/jobs/opportunities
  - policy can manage adjustment without rejecting automation
answer_space:
  - investigation
  - planning
  - employment creation
  - adjustment policy
```

**Evidence strength: high** for executive/public institutional formulation.

### C3. Daniel Bell, 1965: contemporaneous critique of the problem definition

Daniel Bell, “The Bogey of Automation,” *The New York Review of Books*, 26 August 1965:

- https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1965/08/26/the-bogey-of-automation/

Bell explicitly reconstructs the Triple Revolution position as three propositions — radical discontinuity, accelerating technical change, and unemployment as its visible consequence — and disputes them. He argues unemployment is serious but should be explained primarily through insufficient economic growth / aggregate demand rather than automation as a unique new force.

**Status:** contemporaneous actor/opponent evidence. Bell was also a member of the National Commission, but this article is his own public argument and should not be silently treated as the Commission’s collective view.

**Evidence strength: high** for a competing 1965 formulation.

### C4. National Commission, 1966: institutional adjudication

#### Primary source

United States, National Commission on Technology, Automation, and Economic Progress, *Technology and the American Economy*, Vol. I (Washington: GPO, February 1966).

ERIC record/full facsimile:

- https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED023803
- https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED023803.pdf

Verified locations in the facsimile:

- **printed p. 9** (PDF image p. 20), Chapter 2, “Technological Change and Unemployment”;
- **printed p. 109** (PDF image p. 117), Chapter 10, “Summary of Major Conclusions and Recommendations.”

#### Institutional formulation

The Commission explicitly distinguishes:

1. the **general level of unemployment**, which it does not attribute primarily to accelerated technological progress;
2. the **displacement and temporary unemployment of particular workers**, for which technological change can be a major factor.

Its macro explanation emphasizes the interaction of productivity, labour-force growth and aggregate demand; its policy frame therefore shifts toward demand management plus assistance for workers, occupations and regions bearing displacement costs.

The report also rejects two extremes: a near-future productivity glut making gainful employment obsolete, and complacency that denies serious social/economic problems from technical change.

**Evidence strength: very high** for the Commission’s collective 1966 position. The report itself states that it represents combined judgment/general agreement, with occasional member reservations noted separately.

### Why 1964–66 is a particularly valuable Problem Episode

A modern literature review can classify all four sources as “the automation debate.” Problem History should preserve their **competing problem structures**:

```text
Triple Revolution:
cybernation → work becomes structurally unnecessary
             → job/income distribution mechanism breaks

Johnson:
technological progress → opportunities + dislocations
                       → plan adjustment and protect workers

Bell / 1966 Commission:
technology → real local/occupational displacement
           ≠ primary cause of aggregate unemployment
           → demand growth + adjustment policy
```

Shared topic and direct interaction are strong evidence of a common **problem field**. They are not evidence that every participant accepts the same problem formulation.

## 4. Plausible historical breaks to test

These are **source targets, not final periodization**.

### Break 1 — late 18C “improvement” → early 19C Machinery Question

Later reconstruction: Berg argues the social/intellectual conditions change enough that machinery becomes a generalized object of political-economic and social controversy.

Needed actor tests: pamphlets on machinery, Luddite-era/handloom evidence, parliamentary debate, early political economy before/after Ricardo.

### Break 2 — 1840s/50s: Machinery Question loses generalized form

Later reconstruction: Berg sees culmination in the 1840s and changed context/significance by mid-century.

Needed actor tests: positive transition evidence. Do **not** infer disappearance from fewer keyword hits.

### Break 3 — 1929–30s: “technological unemployment” as labour-absorption / progress problem

Keynes supplies explicit formulation; Bix supplies a much wider U.S. source map showing Depression-era debate over technology and progress.

Needed expansion: contemporary U.S. labor, business, engineer/scientist and government materials so Keynes is not used as a canonical stand-in for an entire period.

### Break 4 — 1964–66: automation/cybernation problem splits internally

This break already has unusually strong primary evidence. Actors disagree on whether technical change represents a radical break, whether aggregate unemployment is technologically caused, what must be distributed, and what policy should manage.

A formal episode can likely be built here with ≥3 primary sources immediately.

## 7. Hindsight risks

### H1 — “AI job loss is the same old question”

Today’s AI debate makes it tempting to write a 200-year story of people repeatedly asking whether machines will take jobs. That modern compression is precisely what the pilot should test, not assume.

### H2 — lexical continuity = problem continuity

`machinery`, `technological unemployment`, `automation`, `cybernation`, `computerization`, and `AI` are not interchangeable labels for a stable object.

### H3 — later macroeconomic success refutes historical stakes

If aggregate employment later recovers, that does not retroactively make localized displacement, class losses, distributional conflict, or worker bargaining concerns unreal to actors.

### H4 — “Luddite fallacy” as retrospective verdict

Calling earlier actors irrational Luddites can erase the mechanisms they actually disputed — class distribution, timing, locality, bargaining power, income institutions — and replace their questions with a present-day yes/no employment forecast.

### H5 — technology as a natural autonomous cause

Resnikoff is a strong warning that “automation” itself was historically contested. The pilot must separate:

```text
technical change
managerial reorganization
labour-process change
rhetoric/discourse of automation
measured displacement
aggregate unemployment
```

### H6 — canonical-author compression

Ricardo and Keynes are unusually accessible but cannot stand in for all workers, firms, policymakers, unions, engineers or economists of their periods.

### H7 — disappearance = solution

The possible fading of the early Machinery Question is valuable only if we can show how its problem conditions changed. Fewer later mentions would not prove it was solved or became unaskable.

### H8 — policy institution = consensus

The 1966 Commission has high institutional authority for its own collective formulation, not for what every U.S. actor believed. Triple Revolution, Bell and organized labor/business must remain visible as competing frames.

## 8. Overlap with existing scholarship

### Overlap risk: high

There are already mature histories of:

- the British Machinery Question;
- technological unemployment;
- Depression-era American technology debate;
- postwar automation ideology and policy.

A pilot that merely narrates these works chronologically would fail the repository’s stop condition: **mature history relabeled as Problem History**.

### Where this repository could add a real methodological test

The contribution would be to ask, with primary evidence and `IDENTITY-CHECK`:

1. Does the early-19C Machinery Question actually continue into “technological unemployment,” or is the later debate a transformed successor / revival / analogy?
2. Can we document the **de-generalization** of one problem without claiming the underlying technical difficulty vanished?
3. Does Keynes’s temporary-maladjustment problem share enough target/stakes/presuppositions with Ricardo to justify lineage?
4. In 1964–66, should the cybernation/distribution frame and aggregate-demand/adjustment frame be one compound episode, a `split`, or adjacent competing episodes?
5. What kind of historical recognition exists? Do actors themselves cite earlier controversies, or is the long lineage mostly made by later researchers?

That is more demanding than a history of the term `technological unemployment` and more falsifiable than a story of recurring “automation anxiety.”

## 10. Next source targets

### Highest priority

1. **Mid-century transition, ca. 1845–1870** — find positive evidence for reclassification / de-generalisation of the Machinery Question rather than relying on silence.
2. **Depression-era U.S. competing actors** — use Bix to select at least one labor source, one business/scientific source, and one government investigation around 1929–1939.
3. **1964–66 organized labor and business** — recover testimony/submissions to the National Commission, especially UAW/AFL-CIO and employer associations.
4. **1966 Commission supplementary volumes** — identify which empirical studies produced the aggregate-demand/local-displacement distinction and where commissioners dissented.
5. **Historical-recognition links** — check whether 1930s and 1960s actors explicitly invoke Ricardo, earlier mechanization controversies, or a recurring “technological unemployment” tradition. Actor genealogy is continuity evidence, not an identity verdict.

### Only after the above

Extend the candidate into computerization and AI. A 1980s/2010s/2020s extension is easy to find but methodologically low-value if the 1821→1930→1964 identity questions are still unresolved.

---

## 11. Candidate-specific stop condition

Do **not** choose this as final pilot merely because it offers a spectacular 200-year story.

Reject or narrow the candidate if, after primary-source testing:

- the long chain is held together only by today’s “technology replaces labor” umbrella;
- each period is already fully explained by separate mature historiographies and there is no evidence-bearing identity question left;
- the mid-century “disappearance” can only be inferred from retrieval silence;
- the 1964–66 episode can be explained only as competing predictions, without changes in target/stakes/answer space;
- actor genealogies across periods are absent and continuity depends solely on later economists/historians.

A legitimate result may be that there is **no single long-lived problem**, only several historically adjacent problems repeatedly collapsed by later discourse.
